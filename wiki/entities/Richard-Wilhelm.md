# Richard Wilhelm

## Overview

German sinologist and translator (1873–1930) whose 1924 German edition of the **[[I Ching Divination|I Ching]]** became the definitive Western rendering and the vehicle for Jung's mature engagement with the oracle. Unlike James Legge's earlier English edition — which Jung dismissed because Legge "never bothered to put the method to a practical test" — Wilhelm wrote from **direct practical experience with Chinese diviners**, which is precisely why Jung's interest deepened once Wilhelm's translation appeared.

## Key Facts

- First met Jung in **November 1920**; the two became close collaborators and friends. In December 1921 Jung invited Wilhelm to give a seminar on I Ching divination at the Psychological Club.
- His German I Ching (1924) was later carried into English by Cary F. Baynes (1950), with **Jung's foreword** (Jung, 1950/1975, ¶¶965–1014) — the single richest text for Jung's reflections on the method.
- Cast a hexagram, at Jung's request, on the state of the Psychological Club — a situation Wilhelm knew nothing about — and, per [[Aniela-Jaffe|Aniela Jaffé]], "the diagnosis that resulted was startlingly correct, and so was the prognosis."
- Also translated *The Secret of the Golden Flower*, the Taoist alchemical text Jung annotated — a second major node of the Jung–Wilhelm exchange (Chinese thought entering analytical psychology).
- Jung wrote a well-known memorial address after Wilhelm's death in 1930, crediting him as a bridge between East and West.

## Connections

- The conduit through which the [[I Ching Divination|I Ching]] entered [[C.G. Jung|Jung's]] practice and, via the acausal-meaning question, his theory of [[Synchronicity]].
- His practical, diviner-informed translation is the counter-model to Legge's purely textual one — the same practical-vs-philological split [[zeng2023-i-ching-jungian-application|Zeng]] maps onto the image-number vs. philosophical schools of Yi-ology.
