# Zosimos of Panopolis

Zosimos (fl. c. 300 AD), a Greco-Egyptian living in Panopolis (modern Akhmim, Upper Egypt), is the oldest alchemical author whose substantial texts survive. He is a Gnostic-alchemist: his writings fuse proto-chemical practice with Gnostic cosmology, Hermetic philosophy, and a sustained interest in the *inner* meaning of the *opus*. Jung treats him as the pivotal figure connecting ancient Gnosis to medieval alchemy — the first person to articulate the psychological dimensions of the alchemical work.

Primary source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay II (pars. 85–117, directly read).

---

## Who He Was

Zosimos addressed his writings to **Theosebeia**, his *soror mystica* ("mystical sister") — a female companion in the work, possibly a student or initiatory partner. He was aware of:

- **Gnostic traditions**: archons, demiurges, the imprisonment of the soul in matter
- **Hermetic philosophy**: the Corpus Hermeticum, the *krater* of Poimandres
- **Egyptian religion**: Osiris-Isis mythology, the dismemberment-resurrection pattern
- **Dionysian mystery tradition**: sparagmos (dismemberment), the cooking cauldron
- **"Hebrew" teachings** (i.e., early Christian): the self-sacrificing priest, Christ's death and resurrection

Jung (par. 88): "Psychologically at least, there is no ground for supposing that it is an allegorical invention... the vision of Zosimos may well have been an actual happening." The alchemists testify to visions and dreams occurring *during* the opus itself.

## The Vision Series

The visions are not a sequential narrative but a single essential experience presented as variations on one theme. Each variant features the same figures — the priest-sacrificer, the bowl-shaped altar, the torment, the transformation.

### First Vision (III, i, 2)

The priest Ion submits to "an unendurable torment." The sacrificer draws off the skin of Ion's head with a sword, mingles bones with pieces of flesh, burns them on the fire — "till I perceived by the transformation of the body that I had become spirit. And that is my unendurable torment."

Ion then changes "into the opposite of himself, into a mutilated anthroparion, and he tore his flesh with his own teeth, and sank into himself."

**Central paradox**: the sacrificer and the sacrificed are the same figure.

### Second Vision (III, i, 3)

Same bowl-shaped altar; boiling water; numberless multitude of people burning yet alive. An aged barber explains: "The sight that you see is the entrance, and the exit, and the transformation." And: "This is the place of the operation called embalming. Those who seek to obtain the art enter here, and become spirits by escaping from the body."

The brazen man: **"This brazen man whom you see is the priest who sacrifices and is sacrificed, and spews forth his own flesh. Power is given him over this water and over the people who are punished."**

### Later Visions (III, v, 1–3; III, v bis)

- A guide in royal purple leads Zosimos toward the place of punishments; his whole body is consumed by fire.
- Zosimos realizes: "This barber was the brazen man, clad in a purple garment."
- **Agathodaimon** — a white-haired old man — enters the place of punishments, is transformed into a pillar of fire, groans: **"I am the leaden man, and I submit myself to an unendurable torment."**
- A priest in a white robe celebrating "terrible mysteries": "This is the priest of the inner sanctuaries. It is he who changes the bodies into blood, makes the eyes clairvoyant, and raises the dead."
- At the fourth step: a figure with a sword + the **"Meridian of the Sun"** adorned in white. "Cut off his head, immolate his body, cut his flesh into pieces." "You have completed the descent of the seven steps."

### The Temple Instruction (III, i, 5)

> "In short, my friend, build a temple from a single stone, like to white lead, to alabaster, to Proconnesian marble, with neither end nor beginning in its construction. Let it have within it a spring of the purest water, sparkling like the sun... A dragon lies at the entrance, guarding the temple. Lay hold upon him; immolate him first; strip him of his skin, and taking his flesh with the bones, separate the limbs... The priest, that brazen man, whom you see seated in the spring and composing the substance, look on him not as the brazen man, for he has changed the colour of his nature and has become the silver man; and if you will, you will soon have him as the golden man."

The alchemical sequence in condensed form: **brazen → silver → golden** = nigredo → albedo → rubedo. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]].

## Jung's Psychological Reading (pars. 91–117)

### The Sacrificial Act (par. 91)

"The central image in our dream-vision shows us a kind of sacrificial act undertaken for the purpose of alchemical transformation. It is characteristic of this rite that the priest is at once the sacrificer and the sacrificed." This important idea reached Zosimos from the "Hebrews" (i.e., Christians): Christ was a god who sacrificed himself. Also: the Dionysian tradition (Dionysus torn by the Titans; called ὁ ἀμέριστος καὶ μεμεριομένος νοῦς — the undivided and divided spirit).

### Ion = Uroboros = Sacrificer (par. 111)

Jung's central equation: Ion, the uroboros, and the sacrificer are three aspects of the same principle. The homunculus into whom Ion is transformed devours himself — this is the uroboros. The temple that has "neither beginning nor end in its construction" is the uroboros made architecturally explicit.

The purpose of the operation: "to create the beginnings of order in the *massa confusa*, 'in accordance with the rule of harmony.'" Psychological parallel: "the reduction to order, through reflection, of apparently chaotic fragments of the unconscious which have broken through into consciousness" (par. 111).

> [!key-insight] The Four Functions
> Par. 111: "Without knowing anything of alchemy or its operations, I worked out many years ago a psychological typology based on the four functions of consciousness as the ordering principles of psychic processes in general. Unconsciously, I was making use of the same archetype." The alchemical four-element dismemberment = Jung's four psychological functions. Zosimos articulated this archetype first.

### The Temple as Lapis (par. 112)

The temple of "single stone" = the lapis. The spring within = fountain of life. "The light that shines within it can be understood as the illumination which wholeness brings. Enlightenment is an increase of consciousness." The temple appears in later alchemy as the *domus thesaurorum* or *gazophylacium* (treasure-house).

### The Meridian of the Sun (par. 95)

The white-clad figure at the fourth step is the initiate who has undergone *solificatio* — the solar transformation. His head is struck off to obtain the "omega element" (*στοιχεῖον στρογγύλον*, the "round element") — the arcane substance. Head = seat of consciousness = the solar principle. Zosimos calls his philosophers "**sons of the Golden Head**."

### Angels as Unconscious Transmitters (par. 108)

"Angels are personified transmitters of unconscious contents that are seeking expression. But if the conscious mind is not ready to assimilate these contents, their energy flows off into the affective and instinctual sphere. This produces outbursts of affect, irritation, bad moods, and sexual excitement, as a result of which consciousness gets thoroughly disoriented."
