# Viktor E. Frankl (1905–1997)

Austrian psychiatrist, neurologist, philosopher, and Holocaust survivor. Founder of logotherapy — existential analysis as the "Third Viennese School of Psychotherapy" (after Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology). Author of *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946), which sold over 12 million copies in 24 languages and was named one of the ten most influential books in America.

---

## Biography

**Early life**: Born 1905 in Vienna. Decided to become a physician at age three. As a teenager, fascinated by philosophy, experimental psychology, and psychoanalysis. Began a correspondence with Freud that led Freud to submit a Frankl manuscript to the *International Journal of Psychoanalysis*; it was accepted and published. At sixteen, gave a lecture on the meaning of life, articulating what would become his lifelong core: "It is we ourselves who must answer the questions that life asks of us."

**Clinical training**: Worked 1930–1937 as psychiatrist at the University Clinic in Vienna treating suicidal patients. By 1939, head of the Department of Neurology at Rothschild Hospital — the only Jewish hospital in Vienna. During the early war years, Frankl's position provided some protection from deportation.

**Turning point — staying in Vienna**: In 1942, Frankl received a U.S. immigration visa. He chose to let it lapse to remain with his aging parents. He described finding a fragment of the Shema Yisrael on a piece of marble from a burned synagogue — the Ten Commandment fragment read "Honor thy father and thy mother." He saw this as the answer to his question about responsibility.

**Concentration camps (1942–1945)**: Arrested September 1942 with his family; deported. Spent three years in four camps: Theresienstadt, Auschwitz-Birkenau, Kaufering, and Türkheim (part of the Dachau complex). His wife Tilly, his parents, and his brother all died in the camps. He was assigned camp number 119,104 and spent much of his time digging and laying railway tracks.

**Post-war**: Liberated from Türkheim 1945, nearly dead from typhus. Returned to Vienna August 1945; learned his pregnant wife had died at Bergen-Belsen. Wrote *Man's Search for Meaning* in nine days in 1945. Married Eleanore Schwindt (a nurse) in 1947. Remained head of Neurology at Vienna Policlinic Hospital for 25 years. Pursued flying lessons and mountain climbing into old age. Died 1997, age 92.

## Intellectual Development

### Early influences and breaks

Frankl absorbed psychoanalysis (Freud) and individual psychology (Adler) as a teenager, then broke from both:
- From Freud: Rejected the primacy of the pleasure principle. Observed patients who were neurotic despite no libidinal frustration, and who improved when oriented toward meaning.
- From Adler: Rejected the primacy of the will to power. Found superiority-striving insufficient as a fundamental motivational explanation.

Frankl's break with Adler led Adler to expel him from his circle. Frankl then articulated an independent "Third Viennese School."

### The formative synthesis

By the late 1930s, Frankl had developed the core of logotherapy before his deportation. The concentration camp experience was not the origin of his theory — it was its most severe test and its most powerful validation. He confirmed under extreme conditions what he had already theorized: meaning as primary motivation; inner freedom as irreducible; self-transcendence as the path to self-actualization.

### Relationship to Jung

Frankl and Jung were contemporaries working in overlapping territory — both Viennese-tradition psychiatrists, both interested in meaning, both critical of Freudian reductionism. Key parallels and divergences:

| Dimension | Frankl | Jung |
|-----------|--------|------|
| Primary motivation | Will to meaning (conscious, forward-looking) | Individuation drive (unconscious, teleological) |
| The goal | Fulfilled meaning specific to the person | Integration of the Self (wholeness) |
| The mechanism | Conscious responsibility + existential choice | Unconscious compensation + symbolic dialogue |
| Role of suffering | Unavoidable suffering has attitudinal meaning | Suffering as necessary for ego-death / rebirth |
| The unconscious | Acknowledged but not the primary therapeutic focus | Central to the entire method |
| Humor | Used clinically (paradoxical intention) | Less systematically theorized |

Both traditions share: a telos (a direction the psyche moves toward), the irreducibility of the person to mechanistic causation, and the importance of meaning-making in psychological health. See [[Individuation]] for extended comparison.

Frankl joked that in contrast to Freud's and Adler's "depth psychology," he practiced "height psychology" — focused on the person's future and conscious decisions rather than the past and unconscious.

## Major Works

- *The Doctor and the Soul: An Introduction to Logotherapy* (1946; English 1955) — the book confiscated at Auschwitz, reconstructed from memory
- *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946; English 1959) — the core autobiographical/theoretical text
- *The Unconscious God* (1975) — the spiritual/theological dimension of logotherapy
- *The Unheard Cry for Meaning* (1978) — applied logotherapy
- *The Will to Meaning* — systematic statement of the theory
- Over 30 books total; held professorships at Harvard, Stanford, University of Pittsburgh; Distinguished Professor of Logotherapy at U.S. International University, San Diego

## Key Ideas

See [[Logotherapy]] for the full conceptual treatment. Summary:
- **Will to meaning**: the primary motivational force in human beings
- **Existential vacuum**: the 20th-century mass neurosis of inner emptiness
- **Noögenic neurosis**: neurosis originating in meaning-frustration, not instinctual conflict
- **Paradoxical intention**: breaking the anxiety feedback loop by intending what one fears
- **Dereflection**: redirecting hyper-reflective self-monitoring toward self-transcendence
- **Tragic optimism**: affirming life in spite of the tragic triad (pain, guilt, death)
- **Last human freedom**: "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances."
