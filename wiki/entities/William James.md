# William James

William James (1842-1910). American philosopher and psychologist; founder of pragmatism; pioneer of the psychology of religion. His *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) is the foundational empirical study of mysticism, conversion, and the sick soul, and was one of the most important direct influences on C.G. Jung.

## Biography (key facts)

- Born 1842, New York City; died 1910, Chocorua, New Hampshire
- Brother of novelist Henry James
- MD from Harvard Medical School (1869), though he practiced medicine only briefly
- Taught anatomy and physiology, then philosophy and psychology at Harvard for 35 years
- Founded one of the first experimental psychology laboratories in the United States (1875)
- Suffered severe depression in his twenties ("sick soul" by his own accounting); resolved through what he recognized as an act of will
- Major works: *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), *The Will to Believe* (1897), *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), *Pragmatism* (1907), *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909)
- Gifford Lectures on Natural Religion, Edinburgh, 1901-1902 (published as *Varieties*)

## Philosophical Position

James is the founding pragmatist. The pragmatic maxim: the meaning of an idea is its practical consequences; the truth of a belief is its working in experience. Applied to religion, this means the question is not "Is God real in a metaphysical sense?" but "Does religious belief work? Does it transform the person? What are its fruits?"

This empirical, consequence-oriented stance made James unusual among philosophers of religion: he took the inner experience seriously without endorsing any specific doctrinal system.

**The Will to Believe** (1897): where evidence is insufficient to compel belief and the question is both forced and momentous, a person has the right to believe on the basis of the need to act. Religious belief is not irrational in such cases; refusing to believe is itself a choice with its own consequences.

## Key Concepts (from Varieties)

**Healthy-Mindedness (once-born)**: The temperament that finds existence fundamentally good; religion as affirmation; unity achieved by excluding the negative. Emerson and the mind-cure movements are James's exemplars.

**The Sick Soul (twice-born)**: The temperament constitutionally aware of evil, dread, and wrongness. Its religious solution is deliverance, not affirmation. More inclusive and durable than healthy-mindedness because it has incorporated the negative. James's own crisis and Tolstoy's are the canonical cases.

**The Divided Self**: The psychological precondition for conversion. Incompatible impulses or values fracture the centre of personal energy.

**Conversion**: The reorganization of the self around a new centre. May be gradual or sudden. Always phenomenologically passive: the self feels invaded by something larger than itself.

**The Subliminal Self**: Below the threshold of ordinary consciousness lies a "subliminal region" containing memories, impulses, creative contents, and the materials for conversion. James drew this from Frederic Myers. The subliminal self is the proximate psychological mechanism for conversion: subliminal material erupts through the threshold and reorganizes consciousness.

**The Four Marks of Mystical Experience**: ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity. Still the standard phenomenological taxonomy. See [[Numinous Experience]].

**The "More"**: The wider reality contacted in mystical experience and conversion. Functionally it is the subliminal self; phenomenologically it presents as something outside the ego, greater, and transformative. James does not identify it metaphysically.

## Influence on Jung

Jung acknowledged James directly and the debts are structural, not merely incidental:

| James | Jung |
|-------|------|
| Subliminal self / subliminal region | The unconscious (personal and collective) |
| Subliminal eruption in conversion | Irruption of archetypal contents |
| The "More" (wider self) | The collective unconscious / the Self archetype |
| Divided self reorganized by new centre | Individuation: ego reorganized in relation to Self |
| Four marks of mystical experience | Numinosity of archetypes (via Otto) |
| Sick soul requiring deliverance | The "second half of life" problem; the analysand who cannot be saved by the conscious will alone |

James's empirical, non-reductive stance (the experience is real; its metaphysical interpretation is provisional) was also congenial to Jung's insistence that he makes empirical, not metaphysical, claims.
