# Socrates (470-399 BCE)

## Historical Overview

Athenian philosopher, founder of Western philosophy as we know it through the dialogues of Plato. Son of a stonemason (Sophroniscus) and a midwife. Never wrote anything; all knowledge comes through Platonic dialogues.

Known for:
- The Socratic method: asking questions to expose contradictions in thought
- Devotion to philosophical inquiry above all else
- Claimed to know nothing (*Socratic ignorance*)
- Stated his mission was to care for the souls of Athens
- Condemned to death in 399 BCE for corrupting youth and impiety

## Life and Character

Small, ugly, almost grotesque in appearance (as described in contemporary sources). Yet charismatic and morally compelling. Spent his days in the marketplace engaging anyone in dialogue.

Married Xanthippe, reportedly a shrew, but had no sympathy for his wife's emotional protests when he was condemned to death. In the *Phaedo*, when she wept and cried out, "Socrates, your friends will never see you again!", Socrates dismissed her without warmth.

Homosexually inclined, attracted to young men (Alcibiades, etc.). Yet maintained a fierce intellectual separation from physical desire. Believed the philosopher must transcend Eros in pursuit of truth.

Valued reason above all. Felt nothing in the face of death, as if reason could master feeling and mortality itself.

## Dreams in Platonic Dialogues

### The First Dream (*Phaedo*)

Socrates tells Cebes:

> "I thought I saw the fair form of a beautiful woman approaching me, clothed in bright raiment, and she called to me, saying: 'O Socrates, the third day hence to cloddyb Phthia shalt thou come!'"

**Von Franz's Interpretation:**

This is a **compensating dream**. Socrates' attitude was too rational, detached from feeling and Eros. The unconscious brought him the image of a white, noble woman (his anima) to warn him that he must turn his attention to the development of his feeling side.

Yet Socrates did not understand the dream. He interpreted it literally: he thought it meant he should pursue music and poetry before death. He composed a hymn to Apollo and put Aesop's fables into verse—a superficial obedience to the dream's command without grasping its psychological meaning.

The woman represents Diotima, the wise priestess who, in the *Symposium*, revealed to Socrates the mystery of Platonic Eros—love as the ascent from physical beauty to eternal forms. Yet Socrates, in his concrete life, never achieved this integration.

**Why this is significant:**

Socrates exemplifies the intellectual who **cannot relate to the feminine principle**. His wife was deeply disappointed in him; his anima remained undeveloped. The dream was the unconscious begging him to integrate this missing dimension before death.

### The Second Dream (*Crito*)

The night before his execution, Socrates dreams of a white woman in robes calling to him: "Socrates, on the third day you will reach Phthia" (the mythical land of Achilles, the land of the dead).

**Von Franz's Interpretation:**

This is his anima as **psychopomp**—the guide to the underworld, the messenger of death. She comes to take him across. 

Yet again, Socrates misses the psychological reality. He thinks she is merely confirming his death. He does not see that his refusal to integrate the feminine during life means that death itself will come as a female figure—cold, white, abstract—rather than as a transformation that includes relatedness and feeling.

## Von Franz's Thesis

Socrates represents a profound **split in Western consciousness**: the triumph of reason (Logos) over relatedness (Eros). His anima—the capacity for feeling, beauty, connection—remained a white, ethereal figure, never made flesh in his life.

This set the template for Western philosophy: the thinker as a being who transcends the body, emotion, and relationship in pursuit of abstract truth. Women, sexuality, feeling were relegated to the inferior realm.

The tragedy is that Socrates **needed** his anima. Without her, his rationality became brittle and his relatedness impossible. His wife, his young followers (whom he loved but could not relate to sexually), and even his own unconscious tried to reach him. But he would not hear.

## Historical Figures and Projections

The Socratic tradition made Socrates a **projection screen**. Each generation of philosophers saw in him what it needed to see. Olaf Gigon argued that much of the Platonic dialogues contains "deliberate fiction"—details invented by Plato.

Yet regardless of historical accuracy, the dream material recorded by Plato contains genuine psychological truth. The unconscious of Greek culture was expressing something through these dream narratives: the danger of Logos without Eros.

## Relevance to Individuation

Socrates' path was **incompletely individuated**. He developed the conscious masculine principle (Logos, reason, truth) to a high degree but refused integration with the unconscious feminine (Eros, feeling, relationship, body).

In Jungian terms, his anima remained projection (onto Diotima, onto philosophical ideals) rather than integrated into his conscious personality.

His death was thus **lonely and one-sided**—the price of refusing wholeness.
