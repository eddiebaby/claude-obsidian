# Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim)

**Born**: 1493, Einsiedeln, canton Schwyz, Switzerland  
**Died**: 1541, Salzburg  
**Full name**: Theophrastus Philippus Aureolus Bombastus von Hohenheim (self-styled "Paracelsus")

Swiss-German physician, alchemist, and spiritual philosopher of the Renaissance. The central figure of Jung's Essay III in CW 13 ("Paracelsus as a Spiritual Phenomenon," delivered as a lecture 1941 on the four-hundredth anniversary of Paracelsus's death).

Primary source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay III (pars. 145–).

---

## Who He Was

Paracelsus's driving force was **compassion**: "Compassion is the physician's schoolmaster." Everything in his life — medical practice, alchemical research, philosophical speculation, magical arts — served the goal of healing. "Around this cardinal principle were grouped all his experiences, all his knowledge, all his efforts."

He was a man of volcanic energy and extreme contradiction:
- Firsthand experience over scholastic authority (*lumen naturae* vs. Galen, Avicenna, Rhazes)
- Firm Christian piety alongside deep immersion in magic, alchemy, astrology, folk superstition
- Humility before God alongside assertions that rang like "I under the Lord, the Lord under me" (echoing Angelus Silesius)
- He styled himself "Monarcha" — sovereign ruler — while being perpetually driven from town to town

His father (a physician) gave him his medical training; his mother (who died young) gave him his psychic template. The mother-imago forged by her early death was "all the more formidable" for its remoteness: no real woman could compete with it, so it was displaced upward onto Mater Ecclesia and Mater Natura, his two lifelong mothers.

---

## The Two Mothers (par. 147–148)

Paracelsus had two equal and irreconcilable maternal authorities:

1. **Mater Ecclesia**: the Catholic Church. He remained faithful to it despite levelling savage criticism at its institutions. He resisted the Protestant schism his whole life, even though he had the temperament to go over.

2. **Mater Natura**: the natural world, accessed through the *lumen naturae*. He wrote "like a pagan and yet am a Christian" — hence his name for knowledge from the light of nature: **"Pagoyum"** (*paganum* + Hebrew *goyim*). He named the first five sections of his *Paramirum de quinque entibus morborum* accordingly.

"Conflict was deeply rooted in Paracelsus's nature; indeed, it had to be so, for without a tension of opposites there is no energy, and whenever a volcano erupts, we shall not go wrong in supposing that water and fire have clashed together." (par. 147)

---

## The Two Sources of Knowledge (pars. 148–149)

The duality of his mothers reflects his epistemology:

**Divine revelation**: the eternal light from the Holy Spirit, the source of faith, Christian truth.

**Lumen naturae**: the light of nature, from the stars, from direct experience of creation. Accessible even to pagans — indeed, "to act in the Light of Nature and to rejoice in it is divine despite being mortal." See [[Lumen-Naturae]].

From his *Philosophia sagax*: "There are, therefore, two kinds of knowledge in this world: an eternal and a temporal. The eternal springs directly from the light of the Holy Spirit, but the other directly from the Light of Nature."

Paracelsus believed both lights derived from the unity of God: "Man is twofold, one part temporal, the other part eternal, and each part takes its light from God." But he never resolved the conflict, naming his pagan-natural writings "Pagoyum" even while insisting "I would be condemned as an idolater: but the number one preserves me."

## Magic and Alchemy

**Magic** (pars. 151–156): Paracelsus believed in magic as fervently as in divine revelation, because it proceeded from the *lumen naturae*. He scoffed at doctors who kept away from magic — not knowing they did so out of justifiable fear. Conrad Gessner's testimony: he was accused of sorcery, dealings with demons, Arianism, geomancy, necromancy. Jung's assessment: "Dabbling in these arts — no matter how enlightened one thinks one is — is not without its psychological dangers."

He used wax manikins in healing, designed amulets and seals, absorbed folk superstition with "natural innocence and naïveté." For him, "magic and the wisdom of nature had their place within the divinely ordained order as a *mysterium et magnale Dei*."

**Alchemy** (pars. 157–158): The pagan tradition he absorbed most deeply. Chief figure: Mercurius (quicksilver + world soul). The alchemical operation: separate the *prima materia* into active soul and passive body, reunite in the *coniunctio* (Sol-Luna marriage) → *filius sapientiae* or *filius philosophorum* = transformed Mercurius = hermaphrodite. The *opus alchymicum* was always understood as a kind of *opus divinum*, an analogue to divine creative work. Long before Paracelsus, the *filius* or *lapis philosophorum* had been equated with Christ. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]].

The *Paragranun* says the physician himself is "ripened" by the art. "The alchemical maturation should go hand in hand with the maturation of the physician."

## The Unconscious Split (par. 155)

Jung's psychological verdict on Paracelsus:

> "He consisted of two persons who never really confronted one another. He nowhere betrays the least suspicion that he might not be at one with himself."

Paracelsus always projected the conflict outward — onto medical authorities, onto his enemies, onto the host of physicians against whom he let fly "like the proper Swiss mercenary he was." His inability to see the internal enemy made him what Jung calls an inflated personality: "He was the 'Monarcha,' the sovereign ruler, which secretly and unknown to himself was the very thing he was not."

The unconscious conflict between "I under God" (Christian humility) and "God under me" (alchemical presumption) produced his daemonic energy — but also his "impatience, irritability, and an impotent longing to get one's opponent down whatever the means."

> "Every unconscious conflict works out like that: one obstructs and undermines oneself."

---

## Paracelsus as Prototype of Faust (par. 154)

Jacob Burckhardt called Faust "a great primordial image in the soul of every German." Paracelsus was the prototype. The line:

> Paracelsus → Goethe's Faust → Nietzsche's Zarathustra → "God is dead; create a god for yourself out of your seven devils"

"Medieval alchemy prepared the way for the greatest intervention in the divine world order that man has ever attempted: alchemy was the dawn of the scientific age... Science and technology have indeed conquered the world, but whether the psyche has gained anything is another matter." (par. 163)

## Key Paracelsian Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| *Iliaster* | Matter-star principle; the stellar life-substance; determines length of life. See [[Iliaster]] |
| *Lumen naturae* | Light of nature; the second source of knowledge from the Astrum. See [[Lumen-Naturae]] |
| *Pagoyum* | "Pagan knowledge" (from *paganum* + goyim); his name for natural philosophy |
| *Balsam* | The natural elixir preserving the body from corruption; roughly = *spiritus mercurii* |
| *Homo maximus* / Adech | The Primordial Man; "The star in us"; the Father of the inner heaven. See [[Anthropos]] |
| *Corpus astrale* | The astral body; inner stellar principle |
| *Filius philosophorum* | The son of the philosophers = lapis = light-bringer = parallel to Christ |
