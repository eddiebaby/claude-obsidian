# René Descartes (1596-1650)

## Historical Overview

French philosopher and mathematician, founder of modern Western philosophy through "Cogito, ergo sum" ("I think, therefore I am").

Developed:
- Cartesian coordinate system (the mathematical foundation of analytic geometry)
- Dualism of mind and matter (res cogitans vs. res extensa)
- The method of systematic doubt (methodological skepticism)
- Empiricism via experimentation and reason

Born to minor nobility; mother died a year after his birth, leaving him with emotional coldness (inherited his mother's "pale countenance and incessant cough"). Sent to Jesuit college at age 8.

Physically delicate, intellectually restless. Spent years traveling, in the Dutch army (witnessing the Thirty Years' War), and finally in Holland where he conducted experiments on nature and corresponded with Queen Christina of Sweden.

Called to Sweden in 1649 as her tutor; died of pneumonia a year later, likely hastened by the early morning lectures the young queen demanded.

## The Great Enlightenment and the Three Dreams (1619)

In November 1619, at age 23, Descartes experienced his famous "enlightenment"—a mathematical and philosophical breakthrough—during the siege of Breda (Thirty Years' War).

The event occurred during winter quarters in Ulm (Germany), when he withdrew into what he called a "poêle" (a warm room, likely a stove-heated chamber). In profound solitude and meditative withdrawal, he made what he later described as his greatest discovery—the foundations of the marvelous science.

That night he had **three consecutive dreams**, so vivid and symbolically charged that he wrote them down in detail and later reflected on them extensively.

### The Three Dreams

**Dream One:**
He finds himself in the street, buffeted by a violent wind that spins him around. He is helpless, unable to maintain balance. He sees people near him unaffected. He struggles to a college, where he tries to enter but feels weak. He observes a melon has been left for him.

**Dream Two:**
He hears a loud, thunderous noise. Sparks of fire fill the room. He wakes in terror.

**Dream Three:**
A dictionary and a book of poems appear on his table. He opens them and finds passages: "What path shall I follow in life?" (Qualis vitae curriculum?) Then he awakens and opens a real book of poems, finding passages on the same theme.

## Von Franz's Analysis

### The Dream as Archetypal Enlightenment

These dreams depict **the encounter with the Self and the transformation of consciousness**. The three dreams represent stages of the individuation journey:

1. **Struggle in the outer world** (Wind = chaos, instability): His old ego-based life is being torn apart
2. **Encounter with the numinous** (Thunder, fire, noise): Contact with something beyond rational control; the Self's power
3. **The gift of new order** (Dictionary, poetry, guidance): New knowledge, a new psychological orientation

### Mathematical and Philosophical Implications

Descartes consciously interpreted these as divine or super-conscious guidance toward his mathematical method. Yet the dreams show something deeper: a **psychological and spiritual crisis** leading to transformation.

He had been wandering, living a life of the world ("the street," "the wind"). The dreams show him turning inward ("the warm room," withdrawal) and encountering forces larger than himself.

The **melon** (associated with Hecate, the goddess of underworld knowledge and secrets), the **thunder** (the voice of the divine or the Self), the **fire** (transformation), and the **poetry** (the voice of Eros, the irrational, what he later excluded from his philosophy) all point to contact with the unconscious.

### The Fateful Split

Here is the tragedy: Descartes' **conscious response** to these profound dreams was to develop a philosophy that **rejected** what the dreams were trying to teach him.

His dreams invited him to integrate:
- **Irrational intuition** alongside reason (the melon, the thunderous voice, the poetry)
- **Feminine principle** (Hecate, the muses, Eros)
- **The body and matter** (the concrete physical experience)

Instead, Descartes created a philosophy that:
- Elevated **reason** as the sole pathway to truth
- Relegated **matter** to mere extension, empty of meaning
- Divorced **mind from body** (creating the mind-body problem that still haunts Western philosophy)
- Excluded **feminine, intuitive, poetic knowledge** from legitimate understanding

### The Cartesian Split

Descartes' waking consciousness took the dreams as confirmation that he should pursue **rational philosophy**. But the dreams were showing him something else: the need for **wholeness**.

By divorcing mind from matter, thought from feeling, masculine from feminine, he created a consciousness that is fundamentally **split and incomplete**. This split has dominated Western thought for 400 years.

### The Multiplication of the Personality: Full Dream-by-Dream Reading

Von Franz's analysis ([[vonfranz-dreams-1998]]) goes well beyond the three-stage summary above. Each dream contributes a distinct symbol of the [[Self (archetype)|Self]], and the sequence traces a movement from unity to multiplicity that cracks open Descartes's assumption that the thinking ego (*âme pensante*) is the only psychic reality.

**Dream One — the wind, the ghosts, Mr. N., and the melon.** The ghosts that terrify Descartes are the primordial, autonomous form of "spirit" as primitive man experiences it — before it is domesticated into a controllable mental function. Fear compels Descartes to bend toward his weak left side (the *sinister*, feminine side he habitually undervalues), while the ghosts appear on his unguarded right — the side of one-sided, over-developed consciousness. Whirled around three or four times, he embodies the alchemical axiom of Maria Prophetissa ("one becomes two, two becomes three, and out of the third comes the one as the fourth") — Jung reads the three/four ambiguity as "a wavering between the spiritual and the physical," exactly Descartes's own unresolved split. Fleeing the storm into a college quadrangle (a *temenos*, echoing his Jesuit schooling at La Flèche), Descartes fails to greet an acquaintance, "Mr. N." — a shadow-figure representing the undifferentiated feeling side of his personality, overlooked rather than rejected. A second man then charges Descartes with delivering a **melon** to Mr. N. Von Franz amplifies the melon at length: a Manichaean *electi* food symbolizing captured "germs of light"; a Chinese *I Ching* image of the Yin principle that "drops down... from heaven"; a mandala-like microcosm (its rind's meridian lines mirror a globe); and, via Descartes's own gloss, "*des charmes de la solitude*" — the secret ripening (Greek *πέπων*, "ripe") of his own personality. The melon is the mediating symbol meant to unite Mr. N. (unconscious shadow) with the man in the quadrangle (the rigid, "fides non quaerens intellectum" Catholic in him) — an inner marriage Descartes never actually completes, since he alone, of everyone in the quadrangle, cannot stand upright against the wind.

**Dream Two — the sparks.** A thunderclap, then a "multitude of fiery sparks" glowing in the room. Von Franz links these to the alchemical ***scintillae*** or *oculi piscium* — Jung's "multiple luminosities" of the unconscious, the plural, not-yet-unified counterpart to the single central "*sol invisibilis*." (See [[Scintillae]].) The melon — the Manichaean "light-germ receptacle" of Dream One — has effectively burst open. Descartes rationalizes the phenomenon away by opening and shutting his eyes until it vanishes, the same reflex he uses on all so-called "miraculous" phenomena of nature.

**Dream Three — the dictionary, the poems, and the portraits.** A dictionary (standing for the totality of the sciences) and a *Corpus Poetarum* (standing for wisdom, enthusiasm, and inspiration) appear and vanish on his table without explanation — ghosts still at work, now playing with books instead of appearing directly. An unknown man (a **trickster**-figure, paralleling but distinct from Mr. N.) steers Descartes toward Ausonius's idyll "*Est et Non*" ("Yes and No"), undermining his faith in the possibility of absolutely valid proof — precisely the faith his later *Cogito ergo sum* would enshrine. Small colored copperplate portraits then appear, absent from the real edition: a **multiplicity of individual images**, corresponding to the still-dissociated fragments of Descartes's own personality, "like the many seeds of the melon." Von Franz reads the progression First→Third dream as unity (the single round melon/Monas) giving way to multiplicity (many sparks, many portraits): the first encounter with the Self always duplicates the personality, cracking open the Jesuit-trained ideal of a single unified consciousness and will. As Jung put it, the Self "is paradoxically the quintessence of the individual and at the same time a collectivity."

**Descartes's own (mis)reading.** He decided, even while still asleep, that the experience was a dream and interpreted it in the same breath — deciding the dictionary meant "the connection between all the sciences" and the poems "the union of philosophy with wisdom." On waking he felt crushing religious contrition and vowed a pilgrimage to the Madonna of Loretto, though von Franz judges this an "illegitimate shifting" of a real, unexamined feeling-inferiority onto conventional sin. He also read the final portrait-motif as a straightforward premonition of an Italian painter's visit the next day — missing that the portraits pointed to his own neglected feeling function and individuality, not an external event. See [[Privatio-Boni]] for his one substantive philosophical blind spot the dreams pressed on directly, and [[Larvatus-Prodeo]] for the personal motto von Franz reads as the key to his lifelong self-concealment.

> [!note] A rival, non-Jungian reading exists
> Von Franz's footnotes record that Freud also analyzed this dream, in a 1929 letter to Maxim Leroy (reprinted in Freud's *Gesammelte Schriften*, vol. 12; English in *SE* vol. 21). Heinrich Quiring separately argued the dream was merely a consciously constructed cipher for Descartes's vortex cosmology — a reading von Franz rejects, noting that the dream displays typical, non-inventable dream-motifs throughout.

### Conclusion: Descartes as the Prototype of Modern Rationalism's Cost

Von Franz closes by generalizing beyond Descartes: the dreams "sketch, in a nutshell, the actual problem of the man of our time" — heir to the rationalism Descartes inaugurated. She credits Rittmeister's more sympathetic view that Descartes's work also built a genuine ego-strength and ego-freedom without which modern science could not have arisen. But her verdict stands: the unconscious does not fault Descartes for anything except his failure to greet Mr. N. — a single, almost gentle rebuke — yet the historical cost of that one oversight, multiplied across four centuries of Cartesian consciousness, is the split between mind and body, reason and feeling, that "still awaits the scientist of today."

## The Woman in His Life

Descartes' liaison with a maidservant, Helena Jans, produced a daughter, Francine. He called this a "dangerous engagement from which God had rescued him." When the child was 5 years old, she died.

His cold, dissociative response to this loss mirrors his response to his anima in the dreams: he could not engage with feeling, relationship, or the feminine as live realities. Instead, he theorized about them (the pineal gland as the seat of the soul, the union of mind and body) while his actual life remained split.

## Legacy and Tragedy

Descartes' mathematical and philosophical achievements are genuine. The Cartesian system enabled modern science and rationalism.

Yet the cost was profound: a **consciousness cut off from its roots in the body, nature, feeling, and the feminine principle**.

Von Franz suggests that his great dreams were a warning he did not heed. Had he integrated the message—the need for wholeness, the irrational as carrying truth, the feminine as a way of knowing—Western consciousness might have developed differently.

Instead, we inherited a Cartesian consciousness that must constantly attempt to reunite what Descartes split asunder: mind and body, reason and feeling, masculine and feminine, thought and matter.
