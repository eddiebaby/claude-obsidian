# Erich Neumann

German-born Israeli depth psychologist (1905-1960). Jung's most systematic theoretical heir. Where Jung identified the archetypes and mapped their phenomenology, Neumann systematized their developmental sequence — constructing a comprehensive psychology of consciousness grounded in comparative mythology.

---

## Biographical

Born in Berlin, 1905. Studied medicine and philosophy, completed his doctorate in philosophy. Encountered Jung's work and underwent analysis with Jung in Zurich (1933-1934). The Nazis' rise forced him to emigrate to Tel Aviv in 1934, where he established a private practice and became the central figure in Israeli analytical psychology. Died of cancer in 1960 at age 55, cutting short what would have been a larger theoretical output.

He and Jung maintained an active correspondence and deep mutual respect. Jung wrote forewords to Neumann's major works.

---

## Core Intellectual Contribution

Neumann's project was to give Jungian psychology a developmental axis. Jung's work was largely phenomenological: he described what the archetypes are and how they appear. Neumann asked: in what order do they appear? What is their sequence in the development of both the individual and humanity?

His answer, worked out across his major books, is a staged developmental psychology using mythology as its primary evidence. The underlying claim: the phylogenetic development of human consciousness (traceable in world mythology) is structurally identical to the ontogenetic development of every individual psyche.

This is not a sociological or historical claim — it is a claim about archetypal structure. The stages of the hero myth encode universal developmental necessities, not contingent history.

## Major Works

**The Origins and History of Consciousness** (1949; English 1954)
His magnum opus. Maps the stages of consciousness evolution from the uroboric pre-ego state through the hero myths to individuation. Three main mythological sequences: creation myth (uroboros and Great Mother), hero myth (birth, dragon fight, transformation), transformation myth (captive/treasure, integration). Foreword by Jung. See [[neumann-origins-history-consciousness]].

**The Great Mother: An Analysis of the Archetype** (1955)
The companion volume focused entirely on the feminine archetypal principle. Iconographic survey of goddess figures across cultures as evidence for the maternal archetype's structure. Distinguishes the nurturing-elementary matrix from the transformative-vessel aspect. Foundational for Jungian work on the feminine.

**Depth Psychology and a New Ethic** (1949)
An application of analytical psychology to ethics. Argues that the traditional moral stance (repressing the shadow) produces projection, scapegoating, and mass violence. The "new ethic" demands that individuals take responsibility for their shadow — this is not relativism but a more demanding morality.

**The Child** (1973, posthumous)
On the psychological development of the child from the Jungian perspective. Extends the framework of *Origins* into developmental psychology.

**Amor and Psyche** (1952)
Jungian analysis of the Apuleius tale as the feminine individuation journey — a counterpoint to the masculine hero myth of *Origins*.

## Key Concepts

**Uroboros**: Neumann's central symbol for the primordial pre-ego state. The self-devouring serpent circle = undifferentiated wholeness before subject/object distinction. See [[Uroboros]].

**Great Mother**: The archetypal maternal principle in its dual positive (nourishing) and negative (devouring) aspects. Dominates the matriarchal stage of consciousness. See [[Mother Archetype]].

**Centroversion**: Neumann's term for the psyche's innate drive toward wholeness and integration. Operates unconsciously at first, then increasingly consciously as individuation proceeds. Related to Jung's Self archetype but emphasizes the directional, organizing character of the drive.

**Stadial development**: The view that consciousness develops through discrete stages, each with characteristic mythology, psychological structure, and symbolic expression. Each stage must be genuinely traversed — there are no shortcuts.

**The dragon fight**: The central psychological event of ego development. The hero-ego confronting the uroboric-maternal pull back to undifferentiation. See [[Hero Archetype]].

## Relationship to Jung

Neumann described himself as applying Jung's discoveries rather than extending them. Jung viewed Neumann as a genuine collaborator and peer. The relationship was intellectually close but never uncritical — Neumann developed independently and Jung's forewords, while enthusiastic, noted where they diverged.

Neumann's work is best read as a systematic development of implications already present but unsystematized in Jung: the developmental sequence of the individuation process, the cultural-historical argument for the collective unconscious, the role of the feminine principle in individuation.

One point of extension beyond Jung: Neumann was more willing to commit to historical-developmental claims about collective human psychology, whereas Jung was more cautious about mapping psychological typology onto historical sequence.

## Legacy

- Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) appeared the same year as *Origins* and covers overlapping territory. Campbell knew Neumann's work and was influenced by it. Both drew on Jung. Campbell is more literary and accessible; Neumann is more psychologically rigorous.
- Marion Woodman, Robert Moore, James Hollis, and other post-Jungian analysts draw heavily on Neumann's framework
- *The Great Mother* remains the standard reference for the feminine archetype in Jungian scholarship
- The developmental sequence in *Origins* is the primary framework for understanding the relationship between myth and individual psychological development in the Jungian tradition
