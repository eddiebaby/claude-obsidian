# Hannibal Barca (247-183 BCE)

## Historical Overview

Carthaginian military commander, sworn enemy of Rome during the Second Punic War (218-202 BCE). One of antiquity's greatest generals, famous for:

- The crossing of the Alps with elephants (218 BCE), achieving tactical surprise
- The Battle of Cannae (216 BCE): his most brilliant victory, where he encircled and decimated a larger Roman army
- Years of sustained warfare in Italy despite supply difficulties and eventual isolation from Carthage

Eventually defeated by Scipio Africanus and exiled. Died in exile in Bithynia (modern Turkey).

## Dreams and Military Decisions

Like Themistocles, Hannibal is recorded in ancient sources as a military leader whose decisions were influenced by dreams. The dream-life of the ancients was seen as a direct communication from the gods or, in Jungian terms, from the collective unconscious.

## Paired with Themistocles

Von Franz analyzes Themistocles and Hannibal together in the book as parallel examples of how the unconscious (via dreams) **guided the fate of civilizations**. Both were warriors who listened to the psyche rather than just rational calculation.

Yet the pairing also shows contrast:
- **Themistocles:** Victorious, his dreams validated by Greek triumph
- **Hannibal:** Brilliant but ultimately defeated; the dreamer whose vision could not overcome Rome's material power

## Jungian Significance

The duo embodies the **warrior archetype's relationship to the unconscious**. Not abstract thinkers like Socrates or Descartes, but **men of action who trusted their dreams** and shaped history by acting on that trust.

This contrasts sharply with the modern Western dismissal of dreams as fantasy. Themistocles and Hannibal demonstrate that dreams can carry strategic and spiritual wisdom.
