# Mysterium Coniunctionis (CW Vol. 14)

Jung's last major work (written 1941-1954, published 1955-56), completed in his eightieth year. Subtitle: "An Inquiry into the Separation and Synthesis of Psychic Opposites in Alchemy." This is the capstone of Jung's three-volume alchemical project — [[jung-cw12-psychology-alchemy]] (1944) laid the theoretical foundation via dream-series parallels; [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] (1929-1954) worked through individual alchemical texts (Rosarium, Aurora Consurgens, Paracelsus); CW 14 is the synthesis, tracing the coniunctio — the central alchemical operation of uniting opposites — through its full symbolic development to its final psychological meaning: the *unus mundus*.

The book's argument in one line: the alchemical opus, read psychologically, is individuation's own self-description, given by the unconscious in the only language available to a pre-scientific mind working bodily with matter — and its final goal, the union of the realized self with the *unus mundus*, is not mysticism but a precise (if paradoxical) statement about the relationship between psyche and world.

## Foreword

Jung situates this as the final installment of work begun in *Psychology and Alchemy* and continued through *Psychology of the Transference*, *Aion*, the "Philosophical Tree" essay, *Synchronicity*, and *Answer to Job*. The book's animating principle is *solve et coagula* — dissolve and recombine — alchemy's basic operational rhythm, which Jung reads as the rhythm of psychic transformation itself.

## Chapter I: The Components of the Coniunctio (paras 1-21, read in full)

### §1 The Opposites

The coniunctio unites dualistic factors: humidum/siccum (moist/dry), frigidum/calidum (cold/hot), and centrally Sol/Luna. These appear arranged in a quaternio and are frequently rendered theriomorphically — eagle and toad, two birds or dragons, stag and unicorn. Jung calls the opposites "transconscious" and the whole structure "the phenomenology of the paradoxical self."

### §2 The Quaternio and the Mediating Role of Mercurius

Mercurius is the unifying agent — the *aurum philosophorum*, the germ of unity standing between the pairs of opposites (Ostanes: the adept "stands between two pairs of opposites"). Mercurius is "the true arcane substance, a spirit" that "penetrates all things." Jung draws the Ephesians 2:13 parallel: Christ as peacemaker who breaks down "the dividing wall of hostility" — Mercurius performs the equivalent function alchemically. The mandala, the Hermetic vessel, and the circle are functionally identified.

### §3 The Orphan, the Widow, and the Moon

"Orphan" is one of the names for the stone (Hermes Trismegistus, Dorn); "widow" and "children of the widow" trace to Manichaean usage. The *prima materia* accumulates an enormous range of feminine synonyms — mater, matrix, Venus, regina, femina, virgo, Luna, meretrix, vetula — folding the Isis/Osiris myth (Isis as healer and poisoner) and Cabalistic material (Malchuth as the widow, Tifereth as son/King; the Zohar's identification of Malchuth with Luna, "the moon is the way to heaven") into the same symbolic field. Jung closes the section noting the duplicity of both Mercurius and Luna as mediating figures — neither is a simple, "safe" agent of union.

## Chapter III: The Personification of the Opposites (key sections read)

### §3 Sulphur, closing (paras 147-153)

"The alchemists had discovered the psychological existence of a shadow which opposes and compensates the conscious, positive figure." Sulphur is named the "motive factor in consciousness" — the question of will versus compulsion. The *lapis exilis, in via eiectus* — the insignificant, rejected fragment — is the piece needed to round consciousness out into wholeness (the *homo totus* / Chên-yên, the "true man"). Compulsion has two sources: the shadow, and the Anthropos.

### §4 Luna (paras 154-233, read in full)

**a. The Significance of the Moon** (154-172): Luna is Sol's feminine counterpart and the universal receptacle of the sun — the "bountiful nurse of the dew," *aqua mirifica*. The Tabula Smaragdina parallel: "Its father is the Sun, its mother the Moon." Simon Magus's Helen-gnosis (Helen as *protē ennoia* / sapientia / epinoia) is read alongside the Rosarium's "Unless ye slay me" passage — the mortificatio of the anima/Mercurius, Sol-Rex slain by ten men, the coniugium of dragon and woman.

> **Par. 172** (key): "Psychologically, this means that the union of consciousness (Sol) with its feminine counterpart the unconscious (Luna) has undesirable results to begin with: it produces poisonous animals such as the dragon, serpent, scorpion, basilisk, and toad..."

**b. Psychology of the Moon** (paras 215-233): Dorn on the six spiritual metals composing Luna.

> **Par. 217** (key): "The moon with her antithetical nature is, in a sense, a prototype of individuation, a prefiguration of the self: she is the 'mother and spouse of the sun, who carries in the wind and the air the spagyric embryo conceived by the sun in her womb and belly.'"

> **Par. 219** (key): "Luna is really the mother of the sun, which means, psychologically, that the unconscious is pregnant with consciousness and gives birth to it."

Jung then generalizes to gendered psychology:

> **Par. 224** (key — the Logos/Eros formulation): "For purely psychological reasons I have, in other of my writings, tried to equate the masculine consciousness with the concept of Logos and the feminine with that of Eros. By Logos I meant discrimination, judgment, insight, and by Eros I meant the capacity to relate."

Paras 225-233 extend this: lunar anima in man, solar animus in woman; Sol/Luna preferred as archetypal images over the "pedestrian" Logos/Eros terms; feminine consciousness as lunar/nocturnal; *Sol niger* (dark sun) of feminine psychology; the father-imago/animus link; closing on "the disastrous repetition of the family pattern... the psychological original sin."

### §5 Sal, opening (paras 234-240)

**a. Salt as the Arcane Substance.** The Sulphur-Mercurius-Sal triad is presented as a quaternity in disguise, because Mercurius's duplex/androgynous nature secretly doubles it. Jung diagrams both the alchemical quaternio (Mercurius lapis / Sulphur—Sal / Mercurius Serpent) and a parallel Christian quaternio (Holy Ghost-Dove / Christ—God the Father / Mary), explicitly tying the latter to the 1950 dogma of the Assumption. Dorn calls the quaternity/devil "the four-horned serpent" and identifies woman/the *binarius* with the devil. Salt is read as cosmic, feminine, lunar, light-side ("the whole secret lies in the prepared common salt" — Rosarium).

### Chapter III, closing (paras 345-348, read)

"Only the 'light which the darkness comprehendeth not' can illuminate the darkness." **Active imagination** is named explicitly as a method offering "excellent examples of this." Nigredo is "an indispensable prerequisite for synthesis, for unless the opposites are constellated and brought to consciousness they can never be united." Jung criticizes Freud for halting at "reduction to the inferior half of the personality," overlooking "the daemonic dangerousness of the dark side."

## Chapter IV: Rex and Regina (paras 349-367, read in full)

### §1 Introduction

The king is the carrier of collective-unconscious myth: crown (sun rays), mantle (starry firmament), orb (world), throne. Egyptian theology of kingship is the richest source — Pharaoh as incarnation/son of God, the *ka*, a double trinity (divine: father-god/divine son/*ka-mutef*; human: father-god/Pharaoh/Pharaoh's ka). The Egyptian trinity amulet (Horus, Hathor, winged serpent) is explicitly paralleled to "the illustrations of the coniunctio in the Rosarium, showing King, Queen, and the dove of the Holy Ghost."

### §2 Gold and Spirit

Zosimos/Pseudo-Democritus/Komarios are the oldest alchemical strata. Zosimos's "True Book of Sophe" has the divine Pneuma chained in the elements; the king is gold, copper "the earthly sun." Chrysopoeia (gold-making) runs as a psychic operation parallel to (and independent of) the physical process. "Philosophic gold" (*aurum non vulgi*) is the embodiment of psyche and pneuma and corresponds to the lapis.

### §3 The Transformation of the King

The king as dynamic process — birth, coronation, Heb-Sed festival, death — mirrors alchemy's hierosgamos/death-rebirth structure. The "Allegoria Merlini" is given in full: a king drinks water "closest to his heart," swells and dies, is dismembered, dried, and reconstituted (Egyptian then Alexandrian physicians, sal ammoniac and nitre), is resurrected, and cries "Where are my enemies?" — read as "the primitive motif of the murder or sacrifice of the king for the purpose of renewing his kingly power," and linked to dropsy and the *aqua permanens* ("It kills and vivifies"). Dorn's seven stages of the work introduce the **spagyric marriage**:

> **Par. 364** (key): "The dissolution of the heart in water would therefore correspond to the union of the male with the female, and this in turn to the union of conscious and unconscious, which is precisely the meaning of the 'spagyric marriage.'... The spagyric union produces an embryo whose equivalents are the homunculus and the lapis. The lapis, of course, is a symbol of the self."

## Chapter VI: The Conjunction — Dorn's Three Stages (paras 749-789, read in full)

This is the climactic argument of the entire work and of Jung's alchemical project as a whole.

### §8, closing — Active Imagination as the First Stage (*unio mentalis*) (paras 749-758)

Jung translates Dorn's recipe for the *phlegma vini* (Mercurius non vulgi, the *anima mundi*) directly into clinical method:

> **Par. 749** (key): "Take the unconscious in one of its handiest forms, say a spontaneous fantasy, a dream, an irrational mood, an affect, or something of the kind, and operate with it. Give it your special attention, concentrate on it, and observe its alterations objectively... In short, the alchemical operation seems to us the equivalent of the psychological process of active imagination."

Jung distinguishes mere passive observation of fantasy images (the "foolish Parsifal" who never asks the question because he doesn't recognize his own participation) from genuine engagement, where the subject enters the drama as themselves and thereby creates "an effective counterbalance to its tendency to get out of hand." This is "the decisive rapprochement with the unconscious... the beginning of individuation, whose immediate goal is the experience and production of the symbol of totality" (par. 753). Successfully achieved, this state is the *unio mentalis* — Dorn's **first stage** of conjunction.

### §9 The Third Stage: The Unus Mundus (paras 759-771, read in full)

Dorn's three degrees of conjunction, stated explicitly:
1. *Unio mentalis* — psychic self-knowledge; uniting the warring contents of one's own mind (active imagination's achievement, above).
2. The union of the unified mind with the body — the **second stage**, accomplished by Dorn's "dissolution of the heart in water," i.e. the spagyric marriage (Ch. IV, par. 364, above). Jung: "we would say that with this production of a physical equivalent the idea of the self had taken shape" (par. 758).
3. The union of the whole man (mind + body, now one) with the *unus mundus* — the **third and highest stage**.

> **Par. 760** (key): "The One and Simple is what Dorn called the *unus mundus*. This 'one world' was the *res simplex*. For him the third and highest degree of conjunction was the union of the whole man with the *unus mundus*. By this he meant, as we have seen, the potential world of the first day of creation, when nothing was yet 'in actu,' i.e., divided into two and many, but was still one."

Jung traces the immediate philosophical ancestry to Philo Judaeus (*De mundi opificio*: God created an incorporeal heaven, invisible earth, and finally man as a "little heaven" reflecting the stars — the Microcosm) and notes Plotinus (Fourth Ennead) as an earlier witness to "the unity of the soul," resting on "the basic psychic structure common to all souls." Jung explicitly equates Dorn's third stage with "the relation or identity of the personal with the suprapersonal atman, and of the individual tao with the universal tao" (par. 762) — an Eastern parallel he treats as substantively, not loosely, equivalent.

The union of opposites does not happen by simple fusion but in **a third thing**:

> **Par. 765** (key): "If a union is to take place between opposites like spirit and matter, conscious and unconscious, bright and dark, and so on, it will happen in a third thing, which represents not a compromise but something new... a transcendental entity that could be described only in paradoxes." Man himself, like the stone, is a *lithos ou lithos* — "the stone that is no stone."

The chapter's central metaphysical claim, grounding both alchemy and Jung's synchronicity theory:

> **Par. 769** (key): "The background of our empirical world thus appears to be in fact a *unus mundus*. This is at least a probable hypothesis which satisfies the fundamental tenet of scientific theory: 'Explanatory principles are not to be multiplied beyond the necessary.' The transcendental psychophysical background corresponds to a 'potential world' in so far as all those conditions which determine the form of empirical phenomena are inherent in it."

Jung explicitly parallels this to physics: "Microphysics is feeling its way into the unknown side of matter, just as complex psychology is pushing forward into the unknown side of the psyche... both have developed concepts which display remarkable analogies" (par. 768) — the same micro­physics/depth-psychology convergence argument that underlies the *Synchronicity* essay co-authored with Wolfgang Pauli.

The *caelum* (Dorn's alchemically-produced quintessence) is named with an extraordinary list of synonyms in par. 770: panacea, *medicina catholica*, *lapis animatus* (*living stone*, *baetylus*), the "living stone" of the New Testament/Shepherd of Hermas, parallel of Christ, Saviour of the Macrocosm, *imago Dei*, *anima mundi* in matter, the Microcosm, the whole man (*teleios anthrōpos*), Chên-yên, homunculus, hermaphrodite.

### §10 The Self and the Bounds of Knowledge (paras 772-789, read in full)

Jung now states the full psychological reading of the alchemical arcanum:

> **Par. 776** (key): "As I have repeatedly pointed out, the alchemist's statements about the lapis, considered psychologically, describe the archetype of the self. Its phenomenology is exemplified in mandala symbolism, which portrays the self as a concentric structure, often in the form of a squaring of the circle."

He distinguishes alchemy's projected, first-degree coniunctio from the modern psychological achievement: "Here is a coniunctio of the second degree, such as the alchemists at most dreamed of but could not realize" (par. 777) — but warns this advance is only real "if the centre experienced proves to be a *spiritus rector* of daily life," not merely a private aesthetic experience.

The chapter's most direct statement on the Self-God problem (echoing and extending [[Self (archetype)]]'s treatment in *Aion*):

> **Par. 778** (key): "The self, in its efforts at self-realization, reaches out beyond the ego-personality on all sides... the experience of the self is always a defeat for the ego... the self can be distinguished only conceptually from what has always been referred to as 'God,' but not practically. Both concepts apparently rest on an identical numinous factor... The prototype of this situation is Job's encounter with Yahweh."

Jung is careful, however, to refuse metaphysical overreach. He proposes that what is called "God" in numinous experience is better and more cautiously described as an "unknown psychic or perhaps **psychoid** factor" (par. 786) — "It seems to me very much more cautious and reasonable to take cognizance of the fact that there is not only a psychic but also a psychoid unconscious, before presuming to pronounce metaphysical judgments which are incommensurable with human reason" (par. 788). This is the direct conceptual bridge to [[Synchronicity]] and the psychoid archetype.

### Epilogue, opening (paras 790-791, read)

Alchemy is compared to "an *opus divinum*" — an individual, not collective, religious undertaking aimed at producing unity from "apparently incompatible opposites," understood as both physical and moral conflict simultaneously. Jung credits C.G. Carus as the bridge-figure between alchemy's lost trail and the modern psychology of the unconscious — picking up, at the moment of Goethe's *Faust*, "the old alchemical dictum: *in stercore invenitur*" ("it is found in filth") — not in literal street refuse, but "in the distressing darkness of the human psyche."
