## Historical Context (directly read: Introduction by McGuire, pp. vii-xvi)

The seminar began 7 November 1928 and ran until late June 1930, meeting weekly in Zurich at the Psychological Club rooms (Edith Rockefeller McCormick's ivy-covered mansion on Gemeindestrasse). Attendance required Jung's personal permission; all participants were in analysis with Jung or another Zurich analyst. Approximately 50 named persons contributed to discussion.

**Principal recorder:** Mary Foote (born 1872, New England portrait painter; arrived Zurich January 1928; in analytical work with Jung). Notes financed by Foote from subscriptions plus her own resources.
- Autumn 1928 notes: Anne Chapin
- First half 1929: Charlotte H. Deady
- From October 1929: Mary Foote

The transcripts were never intended for publication. Restricted access for decades. Jung agreed to eventual publication in a 1957 letter to the Bollingen Foundation. McGuire took over editorial responsibility in 1980 after earlier editors.

McGuire's summary of significance (directly read): "This volume gives the fullest account of Jung's method of amplification in the analysis of a patient's dreams and the most detailed record of the treatment of a male patient by Jung himself."

## The Dreamer (directly read: Lecture I, pp. 6-7)

One anonymous male patient. Description given by Jung in the first lecture: "a business man of forty-five, a good intellect, cultivated, prosperous, very polite and social, married, with three or four children; not very neurotic but 'touchy'; his main trouble is that he is irritable and particularly anxious to avoid situations where some one might reproach him or hurt him."

His presenting problem is moral compensation: he has grown apart from his wife (who has become frigid); he visits prostitutes; he compensates by trying to be "more and more correct." Jung: "This is an immoral way of behaving towards the problem, for so it is never solved but keeps the person feeling chronically morally inferior."

He has recently developed interests in philosophy, occultism, and theosophy - which Jung traces to the female element in his psychology beginning to assert itself.

## Lecture I: 7 November 1928 (directly read: pp. 3-14)

### Opening methodological statement (p. 3)

Jung's opening sentence: "Dream analysis is the central problem of the analytical treatment, because it is the most important technical means of opening up an avenue to the unconscious."

On the role of the analyst: "If the doctor is honest, he recognizes that he also does not know the way." The analyst's task is to bring both parties to accept "the impartial facts of nature, scientific realities." Personal opinions "are more or less arbitrary judgments and may be all wrong."

On dreams as objective data (pp. 3-4): "Dreams are objective facts. They do not answer our expectations, and we have not invented them." "Our dreams are most peculiarly independent of our consciousness and exceedingly valuable because they cannot cheat."

### Two primitive categories of dream (p. 4)

Citing Swahili terms reported by Rasmussen (editor notes accuracy is debatable):
- *ota*: the great vision, big, meaningful, of collective importance
- *vudota*: the ordinary small dream

Jung: "Our usual prejudice against dreams - that they mean nothing - is probably just the old primitive tradition that the ordinary dreams are not worth noticing." The great dream was treated as a public matter by chiefs and medicine men; "the dream had formerly a social and political function."

### Dream [1] - full text (directly read, p. 7)

"I hear that a child of my youngest sister is ill, and my brother-in-law comes and asks me to go with him to the theatre and dine afterwards. I have eaten already, yet I think I can go with him.

"We arrive in a large room, with a long dining-table in the centre already spread; and on the four sides of the big room are rows of benches or seats like an amphitheatre, but with their backs to the table - the reverse way. We sit down, and I ask my brother-in-law why his wife has not come. Then I think it is probably because the child is ill, and ask how she is. He says she is much better, only a little fever now.

"Then I am at the home of my brother-in-law, and I see the child, a little girl one or two years old. (He adds: There is no such girl in reality, but there was a boy of two.) The child looks rather ill, and some one informs me that she would not pronounce the name of my wife, Maria. I pronounce that name and ask the child to repeat it, to say 'Aunt Maria,' but I really say 'Aunt Mari-,' and instead of merely leaving out the 'a,' I say 'Mari - ah-ah,' like yawning, despite the protests of the people around me against that way of pronouncing my wife's name."

### Jung's interpretation method in action (pp. 8-14)

**On figures in dreams (p. 8-9):** "Such an imaginary case always refers to the dreamer; the memory-image must be taken as a metaphor." On the sister figure: "She represents an unknown woman, or a feminine factor of unknown nature in himself." Against Freudian substitution: "My method throughout has been to make no assumptions but to accept facts. In arbitrary interpretations anything can be a substitute for anything; beware of prejudices in favour of substitution."

**On the theatre (pp. 11-12):** "The theatre is the place of unreal life, it is life in the form of images, a psychotherapeutic institute where complexes are staged." "And to dine afterwards: To eat the complexes. Communion means eating a complex, originally a sacrificial animal, the totem animal." "Theatre and dining are an anticipation of the process of analysis."

**On personas (footnote 11, p. 13):** "in classical drama, the mask worn by an actor to indicate the role he played. In Jungian terminology it is the official, professional, or social face we present to the world." The patient's excessive correctness is his persona operating to suppress what lies behind it.

**On wives as emotional figures (p. 13-14):** "they are the emotions, for that is the way man usually becomes acquainted with woman. He must leave at home the emotional factor."

**On Logos vs. Eros (p. 14):** "A woman cannot take pure Logos from a man, or a man pure Eros from a woman." A man's emotions are "raw material, like unpolished diamonds. The emotion of man is a natural product, there is nothing purposive in it; but it is genuine and valuable if one can make use of it."

## Lecture I: Continuation (p. 15)

On the *femme inspiratrice* and Logos: "When a femme inspiratrice works with her mind she produces in man the 'seed Logos.' The man fears in a woman 'le formidable secret de ses hanches,' her form of creative power. And woman fears in a man 'le formidable secret de son cerveau'; the creative womb of man is in his head."

The patient "thinks he has already dined" = he believes he has already assimilated himself, "feels that he is complete, a perfectly normal, up-to-date individual, with no need to come to me nor to assimilate anything more." He goes anyway with his brother-in-law. "It is not a habit of mine to go out in the evening, I prefer to remain at home." His wife "would rather sleep than bore herself to death with him. Most exciting evening! Therefore yawning with internal resistance: Mari - and yawn!"

## Lecture II: 14 November 1928 (directly read: pp. 16-28)

### The interpretive method problem

Opening question from a member: "How is it that we create symbols in dreams? How can we be sure that the interpretation is correct, especially when there are no associations?" Jung: "That is, of course, a very practical and fundamental question. I had not taken it up here because I took it for granted that you understood the theory of dream analysis. We are by no means sure that dreams have symbolic meaning, and we don't know that the interpretation is correct, but we make the hypothesis that a dream means something."

On the verification problem: "it is most difficult to give a demonstration of dream analysis; one cannot give full particulars of a case to make it plain, because it involves the whole life history of the patient." Dreams "leak out, they are not under control; no matter how innocent and simple a person is, there are dreams from which to get something if one can only catch any little tail that may be sticking out." The interpretation "clicks" when it absolutely hits the fact; "one knows one is on the right track." If a wrong interpretation is given, "the effect on the patient will show it, the unconscious will react in the next dream, and so the interpretation can be corrected."

### The Swiss lieutenant case (pp. 16-18)

An infantry officer, "a very simple-minded man of no great intelligence, quite devoid of any mental complexes, came limping into my room, walking in a very gingerly fashion and complaining of pains in his feet, especially in the heels, and also in his heart 'as if stabbed.'" Treated by several doctors, hypnosis, electricity, baths, etc., with no result. Jung asked for a dream. The patient had great difficulty remembering dreams but finally produced one that had made an impression on him: "I was walking in the open somewhere and stepped on a snake that bit me on the heel, and I felt poisoned. I woke up frightened."

Associations: a dangerous snake. Jung suggested metaphorical snake = a woman (Genesis 3:15: "The serpent shall bruise thy heel"). The man denied then admitted: three months before coming back from service, another man had taken his fiancee. "Were you sad?" "Oh, if she doesn't want me, I take another." He had repressed his feeling. "He maintained an attitude of indifference, tried to bluff it off, but presently he was weeping. The case was perfectly plain." When he realized his real feeling, "he was profoundly moved and the pains in his heels and feet were gone."

"A snake for a man is eternally a woman. The snake of Paradise in old pictures is represented with a woman's head." Also: Egyptian Ra bitten by the snake of Isis; Louis XIV nursing case (woman poisoned manservant to nurse him four years, then poisoned her uncle - torn into four pieces by horses). "One can eventually procure the necessary help without which the analyst cannot unlock a patient's psychology. This is why we consult dreams."

### Continuation of Dream [1] analysis (pp. 18-28)

Dreams of educated people are complex; dreams of peasants, young or simple people, or primitives are as a rule "astoundingly simple." But dreams of young children are sometimes very clear and sometimes very difficult; "the more unconscious children are, the more they are under the influence of the collective unconscious, or they may absorb the unconscious problems of their parents."

The *big room* = Swiss Verein (club), like those where the Vereins meet in Switzerland. On two official occasions the patient remembers participating in such a room.

The *long dining-table* spread "as though for a great number of people." The seats rise on four sides like an amphitheatre, "but with their backs to the table." Why emphasize collectivity? "The dream says: 'Assume that you are in a public place where there are other people, as at a concert, theatre, or ball-game, and you have to do "like so many other people," a collective job, by no means an individual one.'" Analysis is analogous to confession; "confession has always been collective and ought to be collective; it is not done for oneself alone but for the sake of collectivity, for a social purpose."

Seats turned from table = "The one who turns his back on his fellow-beings the game cannot be played, no communion is possible." "What you are doing in your secrecy is what everyone else is doing, everybody is turning his back on his fellow-beings."

**The centre of social groups and the Mithras parallel:** "The centre of a social group is always a religious symbol. With the primitive, it is the totem; later it is a sacrificial symbol, like the Mithraic killing of the bull." The Mithraic triclinium: cave, two parallel rows of benches, picture of Mithras killing the bull at the end of the room; "people reclined on the couches where the picture could be seen." As the god kills the bull, the worshipper kills his own passions. The bells used in Roman Catholic mass came from Mithras. December 25th taken from Mithraism.

**Jeu de paume historical connection (pp. 22-26):** The ball game in the dream is associated with *jeu de paume* (pelota basque, old English tennis). Medieval Latin fragments quoted by Jung from manuscript research: from a ninth-century Naples codex, games of ball celebrated "every year for the comfort and refreshment of the soul" before a great populace at the feast of St. Januarius in Santa Maria Maggiore. From the twelfth century: "There are some churches where even the bishops and archbishops play with their subordinates, stooping even to play ball - although it seems more praiseworthy not to do so." The *jeu de paume* eventually stopped by the Pope in the thirteenth century. "The burial of Alleluia" = Alleluia in the middle ages was believed to be a woman (feminine ending "a"); she was buried at Eastertide so she would be "a sort of queen of the past year." The ball = the clod of earth = the sun renewed through sacrifice at Easter time. Mountain Lake (Ochway Biano/Antonio Mirabal, Taos Pueblo): "If the white man keeps on interfering with our religion, in ten years they will see something!" - the sun would not rise again.

The ill child: the real child who died was a boy, "and has no actual importance here. Therefore if he dreams of a boy it would express something in himself." A man in the nineteenth century walking home at night became aware of "a shadow figure parallel with him in the middle of the road. He recognized the figure as himself about ten years younger. Then the vision disappeared but appeared again as himself about twenty years younger." The allusion to the dead boy = allusion to the patient's own dead youth; "he has arrived in the second part of life, where one's psychology changes: youth is dead, the second part is beginning."

## Lecture III: 21 November 1928 (directly read: pp. 29-42)

### Subjective vs. objective interpretation (pp. 29-31)

Two questions from members: (1) Is the number of associations the criterion for subjective/objective interpretation? (2) What factors decide?

Jung: associations are not the criterion. "The general criterion is: when a person in a dream is known to you intimately, playing a role in your life at the present moment, one may consider an interpretation on the objective level, because the object is then important." But even then, consider the subjective possibility, "because that person gets at your psychology on the basis of *participation mystique*."

"The unconscious does not make disguises, that is what we do. It is disagreeable to have albumen in the urine, but albumen should not be taken as a disguise for sugar. Freud's theory was made by his women patients. The absolute truth is that the unconscious spoke of Dr. Jones and said no word of Dr. Jung." (Contra Freud's substitution theory.)

For remote figures (not seen for ages, no current role): "there is no point in assuming that the person is, say, an autonomous factor in your psychology. Since he doesn't enter your sphere with the onslaught of a real person, since he doesn't arouse a psychic vortex in your mental atmosphere, he is more probably merely an image which has to do with yourself alone."

**Participation mystique** (term from Lucien Levy-Bruhl, 1857-1939): "psychological connection in which the subject cannot clearly distinguish himself from the object but is bound to it by a direct relationship which amounts to partial identity." (Footnote: see *Psychological Types*, CW 6, par. 781.) "The strongest thing in man is *participation mystique*, just 'you and your dog in the dark'; that is stronger than the need for individuality."

### Jeu de paume: mediaeval manuscripts (pp. 32-34)

Jung cites his research into Latin texts. Two examples:

From Auxerre (to the sixteenth century): when the ball (*pelota*) was accepted from the newly-elected canon by the dean, the rest of the canons intoned antiphonally "Praise to the Paschal Victim." The dean "supporting the ball with his left hand, danced, and the rest joined hands and, chanting, executed a choral dance round the labyrinth; meanwhile the *pilota* was thrown by the dean alternately to the dancers, one by one. After the dance, the choir hurried off to the repast."

From Narbonne: "While the bells are ringing for vespers, the whole chapter assembles in the archbishop's house, and his servants serve certain dishes and wine to all. Then the archbishop is to throw the ball."

From Naples (Bishop of Naples, 508-536): games of ball "for the comfort and refreshment of the soul" on the third Sunday in May at the feast of St. Januarius. These games were played "for the consolation and recreation of the soul."

"Probably that this ball symbolizes the sun." The custom "the burial of Alleluia" in the middle ages: Alleluia believed to be a woman (feminine ending). Archive from Toul, Lorraine (written 1497 by Nicolas, canon): "Alleluia is buried." The custom is very old: "the sun would not rise again if not supported - renewed through sacrifice at Easter time. Alleluia was simply Mother Earth, a feminine potency made to suffer death, burial, and resurrection."

Note: Mountain Lake (Ochway Biano/Antonio Mirabal, c. 1890-1975), of the Taos Pueblo, met by Jung in January 1925; letter to Jung 21 Oct. 1932: "If the white man keeps on interfering with our religion, in ten years they will see something!"

### Return to Dream [1]: the brother-in-law as shadow (pp. 35-42)

At the third session, Dr. Shaw reports a dream: she went to Spain, saw a bull-fight; a man and bull were fighting and "a great crowd was shrieking that the bull must be killed. I did not want it to be killed and I fought for the bull all night." Jung connects this to the Mithraic bull-killing discussion. The bull is "natural force, the uncontrolled animal, which is not necessarily destructive." Killing the bull in modern times would be "blasphemous, it would mean killing the natural thing in us." Message of the medicine man in the dream: "Don't make the mistake of killing the bull, because this is the only thing which can connect us; we must come back to the natural and eternal laws."

The brother-in-law as **shadow**: the dreamer is "a director of a business company, and his brother-in-law, being a younger man, has succeeded him; so he followed him, he is the representative of the one that follows us, the shadow." Horace *Epistles* 2.2.187: "scit Genius, natale comes qui temperat astrum, / naturae deus humanae, mortalis in unum / quodque caput, voltu mutabilis, albus et ater" - "a god of changeful face, white and black, that is in everybody, a daemon of contradictory aspects."

The film *Der Student von Prag* (1926, dir. Henrik Galeen; based on Chamisso's *Peter Schlemihls wundersame Geschichte*, 1814): student sells his reflection to the devil; becomes a man without a shadow; finds himself in every embarrassing social situation because he has no reflection. "His shadow is here represented by the one who follows him, his brother-in-law."

**Circle diagram introduced (pp. 50-52):** Jung introduces the diagram: circle divided into conscious half (Persona on outer rim, Ego as small circle inside) and unconscious half (Individual Shadow in hatched area adjacent to conscious, Collective Unconscious in darker hatched outer arc, Anima at extreme edge). "We express the totality of personality, male and female, by a circle." Persona = "a sort of paste one wears over the face." The anima is "only very lately" arrived at by Jung as "the counterpart of the persona, and always appears as a woman of a certain quality because she is in connection with the man's specific shadow."

**The ill girl as anima-child (pp. 38-42):** The child is not the real child (boy, died). The dream says she is two years old; "Two years ago I came back from abroad and settled in Switzerland. I began then the study of occult literature, spiritism, theosophy, all sorts of things." He had also read *Towards the Stars* (Herbert Dennis Bradley, 1924) and *The Visionary of Prevorst* (Justinus Kerner, 1829, the first history of a case of somnambulism psychologically observed).

"The little girl is the child of his anima" (footnote 6 first defines anima formally: "Jung's term for the feminine component of a man's psychology, representing his function of relationship (Eros) with the opposite sex and also with his unconscious. The anima appears personified in dreams as the unknown woman or 'dream girl' and is invariably projected onto a real woman or series of women.") The child is ill because it was "fed on occult literature" rather than "free expansion and the study of his unconscious images, not occultism, since it is the child of his anima and therefore within, not without."

Child will not pronounce "Maria": "The anima doesn't like the wife; that little girl-child in him, occult studies, leads him away from his wife to secrecy." She is the child of the union of shadow and anima; "a dangerous reality, and all the more so because not visible."

## Lecture IV: 28 November 1928 (directly read: pp. 43-56)

### Synchronism: first recorded use (pp. 44-45)

After the Nov. 21 session: the patient spent Nov. 20-24 (four days) making a picture he could not understand - "a bull's head, and it must be a very sacred bull because he holds the disc of the sun between the horns." He came to Jung for an explanation. Jung had been talking of the bull's sacred significance in the seminar. "His drawing synchronizes with that."

Then a letter arrived from Mexico from a friend who had just attended a bull-fight: "The one point of supreme art in the whole thing is the moment when the bull stops still, confused, and faces the matador, and the matador standing in front of him makes the gesture of scorn to show his complete mastery." "The matador is the point of perfect conscious control in that weltering mass of unconsciousness. And the toreador is the hero because he is the only shining light in that dark mass of passion and rage, that lack of control and discipline."

McGuire footnote (p. 44): "Apparently Jung's first use of this term in the sense of 'synchronicity,' or meaningful coincidence." Jung: "It is a living situation; it is like an animal with feelers, or with many umbilical cords. We don't realize that while we are talking of it, it is producing." "The East bases much of its science on this irregularity and considers coincidences as the reliable basis of the world rather than causality." Reference to the *I Ching* ("the oldest Chinese scientific book").

### Completing the analysis of Dream [1] (pp. 45-56)

"In technical matters the ancients never could hold an abstract thought for any length of time, they were always interrupted by the playful instinct." On historical understanding: "We need to consider the historical connotations in trying to explain dreams; we cannot understand them on the personal basis only."

At the end of the dream: "the dreamer is obviously bored by that name, Maria, and yawns in pronouncing it; and the protests of members of the family show that he himself protests against it from the family standpoint." Dream's message: "My dear fellow, you are just bored, that's the truth!" If a man is forced to realize he is bored, "what happens to his life force, his libido?" - "he would begin to look out of the window" (German colloquialism for casting a roving eye for another woman). "He is now in a situation where he is seeking more; he is still bored with his wife, looking out of the window has not helped that."

The dream "contains an answer to his very big problem, so we must read it as a message coming from the unconscious."

On the youngest sister and brother-in-law as subjective figures (pp. 48-49): they represent parts of the dreamer himself, "stage figures in his private theatre." The patient has been "a director of a business company, and his brother-in-law, being a younger man, has succeeded him; so he followed him, he is the representative of the one that follows us, the shadow. The shadow is always the follower."

**Shadow and anima (pp. 50-55):** "The totality of personality, male and female" expressed by a circle. Persona on one side; shadow, collective unconscious, anima on the other. "Consciousness is like a searchlight wandering over the field; only those points which are illuminated are conscious." Anima = "the completion of the man's whole adaptation to unknown or partly known things." "I have chosen to use the word *anima* to avoid all trouble with the meaning of 'soul.'"

"There is not one thing in our civilization that has not first been in the imagination, in fantasy; even houses and chairs have first existed in the imagination of the architect or designer. The World War came about through mere opinions... Fantasies are most dangerous; we would be wise to make up our minds that an imaginary child or woman is a dangerous reality, and all the more so because not visible."

"The anima and animus have tremendous influence because we leave the shadow to them. By not being aware of having a shadow, you declare a part of your personality to be non-existent. Then it enters the kingdom of the non-existent, which swells up and takes on enormous proportions." Reference to Meyrink's *Die Fledermause* (1916): pale, sad people who decrease as the "corpses in the graveyard are growing proportionately fat."

On occult studies: "The union of the shadow and anima has the character of something exceedingly mysterious. That it eventually led our patient to occult studies is an important hint as to the kind of experience." He had gotten "out of it all that outward success could give, but had the idea that this was not all; so he drifted toward the occult, he began to read about Atlantis, etc., in order to find where 'that thing' was hidden."

The shadow invites the dreamer to the theatre so he "may see all that the shadow sees, the scenery of the unconscious." Going with the shadow = accepting the existence of the shadow; "he admits the reality of his shadow side - that he is so terribly bored, that he has fantasies, etc." Lecture ends: "The right beginning is within. Learn of one's own dark side, then one can tackle theosophy. Theosophy means the 'wisdom of God.' Can we have that? Heavens, no! Be wise about yourself, then you know something."

## Lecture V: 5 December 1928 (directly read: pp. 57-68)

### Seminar member interpretations of Dream [1]

Jung invites members to give their own interpretations; result demonstrates multiple valid perspectives all illuminating the same material.

**Dr. Deady (first interpretation):** The problem is an anima one; he went into the occult under the influence of his sister (his anima), "not as a man should but in a mood which always means anima." The anima doesn't busy herself with nonsense alone; she is also the *femme inspiratrice*: "she gives a man very great ideas and generous impulses, she can make a man's life grand and noble, not merely a bundle of moods." But "when the anima is behind a man there is also some trap, as if the incentive were somehow wrong." Napoleon's dream was to be like Alexander the Great; "his life became a quest, a romantic adventure; that shows the influence of the anima." Our dreamer "went at the occult studies in the way of a quest, set out like a knight seeking adventures." His brother-in-law asks him to go to the theatre - "the message came from his unconscious, as if a low voice had spoken: 'Go to the theatre.'" Like Socrates' daemon: "Make more music"; "Take the street to the left."

**Miss Taylor (second interpretation):** "The message of the dream is conversion, a change of attitude. When the old way of living begins to lose interest, any time between forty and seventy, the moment has come for a change, not of outward conditions but one within." Three problems: (1) ultra-correctness; (2) consequent boredom and grey shutters; (3) unconscious primitive feeling expressed as asking a sick child to pronounce correctly and yawning in saying his wife's name. "Girl two years old": two years before, the Eros principle in himself began to be constellated; he had given up occult studies because of "some odium around that study." "The food for the child would be free expansion and the study of his unconscious images, not occultism." "My brother-in-law asks me to go to the theatre and to dine": his irrational side suggested looking at the images of his unconscious, assimilating these unconscious parts of himself. "Alone": without the women, that is, without emotion. Analysis is "the right line."

**Mrs. Fierz (third interpretation):** Little nephew's poem and song "Aunt Maria is a dear boy" and the old Aunt Maria seem important. His wife is "to him something like a distant aunt, and the old aunt seems boring, as his wife does." The song might show he could really "do something with his own wife." "The child who wrote that song is a boy, and he makes his aunt the children's comradeships are active." His wife has an attraction for children, is child-like, "is the playmate of all the children in the family, a fact which is exceedingly important for his own problem, for it means that his wife is not a good playmate for a man."

Jung synthesizes: The anima is "almost entirely negative" until now. "The man must give up his illusions, admit he is not respectable and that he is bored; and he must *tell his wife* he is bored to death and at the same time that 'sometimes my sublimation fails to work.' If he only knew his wife this would be easier." The shadow must be acknowledged first; "since he cannot escape his shadow he will become conscious of the less elegant side of himself. Then the shadow will be detached from his anima, because as he becomes conscious of his shadow, it is released from his unconscious. Then between the shadow and anima a real relation can take place, with the outcome that the child will be normal. And when the shadow and anima have a proper relationship, there is a chance that his relation to his wife will become better, that he can have an individual relationship with her."

### Marriage and participation mystique (pp. 62-64)

"Marriage is a collective institution, and relationship in marriage is a collective relationship." When times become more sophisticated, "the individual gets spoilt; one has more desires and claims, one psychologizes and wants to understand, and then one finds one is not really adjusted." 

"Since *participation mystique* is the usual condition in marriage, especially when people marry young, an individual relation is impossible." If they admit secrets to each other, they might establish a relationship; if they have no secrets, "there is nothing to protect one against *participation mystique*, one sinks into that bottomless pit of identity and after a while discovers that nothing happens at all any longer."

In the patient's case: "His sexual relation with his wife doesn't work at all: she keeps him away as much as possible, and at forty-seven to always do uphill work is terribly uninteresting, and he more or less chucked the whole thing." His attempt at occult studies is "rather like Freud's sublimation idea - intercourse with angels." The dream's shadow says: "Now come, let us look at the true pictures of the unconscious, its real, impartial pictures of things as they are; and let us eat them and assimilate them afterwards without the women - without emotion, objectively, impersonally, just looking at things as they are."

On collective problems requiring collective symbolism: "No collective problems have arisen just today, our conditions are thoroughly historical." Marriage: "Since times immemorial, marriage has been arranged as a system of matches... It was chiefly barter, women bought and sold... That is quite certainly true with peasants for powerful economic reasons. So it is often 'the bacon to the sausage,' as we say, two fat things together." The pelota played "against the wall" may have an element of self-isolation or auto-eroticism - "we must handle dreams with nuance, like a work of art." Last line: "Now to get all this into practice, that is something else!"

## Lecture VI: 12 December 1928 (directly read: pp. 69-74, partial)

### Historical parallels and the collective unconscious (pp. 69-72)

"People say, 'Why bring in the historical parallel at all? It is irrelevant and mere fantasy.' But the historical parallel is not irrelevant, it is exceedingly important, particularly because we white people don't realize to what extent we are the descendants, the children, of a long series of ancestors."

"Our actual mind is the result of the work of thousands or perhaps a million years. There is a long history in every sentence, every word we speak has a tremendous history, every metaphor is full of historical symbolism; they would not carry at all if that were not true."

On comparative evidence: "I analysed dreams of Somali Negroes, very black Negroes whom I have analysed in the United States, and found Greek myths in their dreams - Ixion on the wheel, for instance." (McGuire footnote: Jung "analysed" some Negro patients at the Government mental hospital, St. Elizabeths, Washington D.C., in Fall 1912, during his Fordham University lectures.) "It is only illusion when you think they are far apart; the Negro has the same kind of unconscious as the one that produced those symbols in Greece or anywhere else."

An ancient parchment written in old Germanic containing an invocation to Wotan and Baldur, "exceedingly rare and precious, yellow with age, it is kept under glass in a museum in Zurich. When one reads it, one might say: 'Oh, how far away; it could just as well come from the moon!'" But "there is a village in the Canton of Zurich where the peasants are still living by the same book, only now instead of Wotan and Baldur it is Jesus Christ and his disciples." - "It all comes out of the same unconscious mind, the irrational and eternal stock, the pre-functioning collective unconscious, which repeats itself throughout the centuries, a sort of eternal, imperishable language."

"Our unconscious speaks a language which is most international." The surface language is different yet the underlying facts are the same.

### Anima, persona, and the diagram (pp. 72-74)

On anima "death": "That is only metaphorical. It is as if a personification of the unconscious received life through certain contents, and when the personification is depleted of those contents then that particular personification collapses. Like giving a ghost its right name - it collapses." (In response to question about Zurich 1925 seminar statement "when the anima has a child, she dies.")

Question on animus/anima distinction: Faust and Mephistopheles in Goethe. "Faust would be the grand, heroic, idealized personification of the conscious aspiration of Goethe, and Mephistopheles the personification of all his drawbacks and shortcomings, the negativity of his intellect, the dark part, the shadow. That has nothing to do with animus or anima, however. But if you dream of Goethe, then he functions as an animus figure, the personification of the unconscious Dr. Goethe in you."

Persona: "the mask we turn toward the world." Wagner "had to write letters to a lady in Vienna about pink silk dressing-gowns! This persona may be a very attractive thing; if anyone chances to possess an attractive persona, he is sure to identify with it and believe he is it, and then he becomes the victim of it." "I am partly doing what you want or expect me to do and partly what you don't want or like - that is my choice." The persona is "a ready-made function from which you can withdraw, or into which you can step at will."

Personal unconscious = "perfectly superfluous... a sort of negligence. People should not be unaware of natural facts: there is just no sense in not being aware of hunger, trouble with sex, certain relations to certain people, etc." But "no one approaches the Kingdom of Heaven without having passed through the flame and been burnt through and through. The collective unconscious is the unknown in objects."

### Lecture VI completion: persona, shadow, and the collective unconscious as object (pp. 75-82)

People identified with their persona are forced to do amazing things behind the screen as compensation, "to pay tribute to the lower gods." The opposite of the persona is the anima and animus. Being alone forces you to the personal unconscious; extraverts and persona-identified people hate solitude because they begin to see themselves. "Bush fear": a peculiar collective fear that seizes you when alone in the bush, of eyes watching everywhere; Jung in Africa kept turning in a small circle for half an hour so his back would not be turned to the leopard's eyes.

When the personal unconscious is overburdened, it presses down on the collective unconscious and enhances its uncanny qualities: the collective unconscious = shadow side of the object itself.

**Shadow and sin (pp. 75-77):** "The whole meaning of sin is that you carry it. What is the use of a sin if you can throw it away? If you are thoroughly aware of your sin, you must carry it, live with it, it is yourself. Otherwise you deny your brother, your shadow, the imperfect being in you that follows after and does everything which you are loath to do." To the shadow: "Yes, you are my brother, I must accept you." Against denial: "Raca, I have nothing to do with you!" (Cf. Matthew 5:22). The pious man who denies his shadow gets terrible visions of nude women, sexual fantasies from the abyss: "the more pious he is the more evil are the things that befall him. He is a sort of St. Anthony." "Homo homini lupus" (Plautus, *Asinaria* 495): man is wolf to man, the werewolf idea.

"Even when you think you are alone and can do what you please, if you deny your shadow there will be a reaction from the mind that always is, from the man a million years old within you." (Footnote 6 cites "The 2,000,000-Year-Old Man" 1936 interview in *C.G. Jung Speaking*, pp. 88ff.) "You are never alone because the eyes of the centuries watch you." Violations of eternal laws may reach you not only directly but through children, fellow-beings, waves in your surroundings.

**The Jewish legend of the Evil Demon of Passion (pp. 78-79):** Talmudic tractate *Yoma* 69b (Gershom Scholem footnote: the source is a Jewish-Arab folktale in a collection unknown to Jung). A very pious, wise old man begged God to remove the evil spirit of passion from the world; God complied. The man went into his rose garden that evening: the perfume was not quite the same; something was missing like bread with no salt. He filled his golden cup with wonderful old wine; the taste was flat. He kissed his beautiful young wife; she was "like the wine and the perfume, flat!" He told the Lord and begged him to send back the Evil Spirit of Passion; God did. Then roses had wonderful perfume, wine was delicious, his wife's kiss sweeter than ever. "That story should tell you that you take something out of the world when you trespass against the eternal laws of the Old Man."

**The anima as filter (pp. 79-81):** Q (seminar): What happens when the anima returns to the unconscious? Jung: "The anima acts as a filter: she gives certain effects, as she can receive certain effects. Through the persona we get certain effects and we also produce certain effects on other people, and we must assume it is the same with the anima." In Rider Haggard's *She* (1887; Jung notes earliest citation in "Mind and Earth" 1927, CW 10, par. 75): the anima from the collective unconscious knows all secrets, transmits them to Leo and Holly; Isis always looming behind, with Isis as her priestess. "The anima is a sort of intercessor between Isis and the man in *She*."

An old Chinese text says that when a man wakes heavy in a bad mood, that is his anima: "a peculiar mood which has an influence on him, and on his dark side, the unconscious, as well." Active imagination: "This morning, say, I have been down in the collective unconscious, and then I crept up 365 steps and arrived on the threshold and stepped into my house, into the conscious, where I find my mask, Dr. Jung, all ready, just as in Mme Tussaud's." A bad mood from the collective unconscious ripples back to produce "a series of peculiar images which explain the state you are in." Eastern religions organize religious ritual as substitute for this living process in the collective unconscious.

## Winter Term, Second Part: January/March 1929

### Lecture I: 23 January 1929 (directly read: pp. 85-92)

Jung begins the new term by introducing prejudices to address before analysing the new dreams. Most important: **the age of the individual should make a tremendous difference**. In the second half of life everything important may be utterly negligible in the first, and vice versa. At forty, one should have roots, position, family; people without this have the psychology of the nomad. Young people's fantasies are "often beautiful, but for the most part of a negative importance"; if you open the door of symbolism to them they may live it instead of real life. A young girl in analysis four years, five days a week, three weeks' vacation: "Who told you that you had an obligation to analysis? Your obligation is to life! That girl is a victim of analysis."

**Patient recap (p. 86):** The dreamer is forty-seven (updated from earlier forty-five), not neurotic, a big merchant, very conventional and correct, highly intellectual and cultured. Married with children. "His trouble is that he is too much adapted, he is entirely chained by his environment, by obligation to his world. He has lost his freedom. He has sacrificed all creative imagination to be 'real.'" He began reading theosophy, then came to Jung for two years off and on. From Dream [1] he discovered he was terribly bored with life and with his wife. "The second dream was four days later and dreamed on the basis of his knowledge of the first dream."

**Dream [2] (verbatim):** "My wife asks me to come with her to pay a call on a poor young woman, a tailoress. She lives and works in an unhealthy hole, she is suffering from T.B. I go there and say to the girl that she should not work inside, she should work in the open. I tell her that she could work in my garden, but she says she has no machine. I tell her that she can have my wife's machine."

Dreamer's impression: "In spite of the fact that there is nothing erotic in the dream I felt that it had that atmosphere. When my wife asked me to pay the visit I felt that something might happen." T.B. people often erotic; sewing machine belongs to wife; "I had the feeling that she should say the first word."

**The dreamer cannot allow his feelings to work in the open:** "pressing his feelings back into his marriage... the feelings of a respectable man cannot work in the open." His Eros = inferior principle; "it is exceedingly difficult for a man for whom Logos is the principle... a man must have a fair amount of the feminine in him to realize his relatedness. Eros is the job of the woman."

**Natural mind of women (pp. 87-88):** Eros/Logos asymmetry: women find it natural to relate, men find it natural to think; "you can fight with a man half a year before he will admit his feelings." Jung's mother had "a split mind": conventional mind and natural mind. Story: Jung hated "nice children," his mother scolded him, forgot he was there, said to herself: "Of course one should not have kept that litter." He was instantly reconciled with her. Woman has two minds: traditional/conventional, and "Nature's ruthless and sensible mind which says the truth." Illustrated in Anatole France's *Penguin Island* (tr. E.W. Evans 1948, p. 30; cf. *Mysterium Coniunctionis* CW 14, par. 227): debate over whether penguins have souls; St. Catherine solved it: "Donnez leur une ame mais une petite." "Woman, to a certain extent, is Nature and Nature is terrible, inconsistent and logical at the same time."

**The tailoress as maker of new attitudes (pp. 90-92):** "Feelings and thoughts can grow sick and die." Old patient who "had no problem any more, she had sucked them in, converted them into her body" = heart races for psychological reasons, neurosis like shell-shock. "When problems are converted into body, outer problems go, but the body rots... the neurosis has gone deeply into the physiological processes." Heraclitus (Frag. 68, John Burnet *Early Greek Philosophy* 4th edn. 1930, p. 138): "It is death to the soul to become unconscious."

Tailoress = maker of clothes = "maker of new attitudes. The birth of a new attitude has a long historical background." Negro myth: a time when all were immortal and could take their skin off; an old woman lost her skin, she died; "and that is how death came into the world. By analogy, people must behave like snakes, casting the old garments aside." Catholic confirmation: young girls in white clothes. Africa: boys circumcised in bamboo hut ceremony, covering bodies entirely. Polynesians put on mask for spring renewal. "It is most flattering to the analyst to be called a tailor... he is the maker of the new body, the new skin; he is the initiator of a new immortality."

At forty, women begin to realize their masculinity, and men their femininity, "because it is new and untried." Indian chief: Manitou appears and bids him eat with the women, sit with the women, dress like the women. Old women in Spain grow strong black beards. "The thing that has not been considered, the thing that has been despised, that will be the birthplace of the Saviour. Therefore the feeling, which is the most awkward to him, contains the making of a new attitude."

Two machines = two methods: wife's machine = mechanical, cause-and-effect; girl's machine = her own way. "Sewing is fastening things together. The method must aim to fasten together, to join that which has been separated. That which should be joined together in the man, psychologically, is the conscious and the unconscious. Analysis fastens them together, and that is integration."

### Lecture II: 30 January 1929 (directly read: pp. 93-102)

**Mr. Gibb's question:** Can the sewing-machine symbol be generalized to "method"? Cinderella's wand, Aladdin's lamp, etc.? Jung: "You cannot say the symbol in a dream is merely a facade behind which you can hide and then say what the dream is. The symbol is a fact... we can only go on with the dream by understanding what the sewing-machine means." The specific machine = a mechanical method, purely of cause and effect, a soulless way. "A symbol in a dream is meant to be what it is."

Dreamer associates wife's machine sexually: sex = purely mechanical in his view. "He understands the method as purely mechanical, and that is the way he looks at sex. This is the source of the eternal misconception between man and woman. For most men the idea of sexuality is purely mechanical and unpsychological, while with women it is associated with feeling."

On Freud: "It is Freud's idea that dreams are prearranged, which I do not agree with. A dream walks in like an animal. I may be sitting in the woods and a deer appears." Freud: dreams rational; Jung: irrational, it just happens. Jung told the dreamer to accept things as facts: "I don't know whether your devil or your good angel has suggested this; we must just wait and see how it works out. I admit that it would be very awkward for you to fall in love with this girl and upset your marriage, but you are immensely attracted to the idea of getting your feelings out into the open."

**Natural mind of women, continued (pp. 95-96):** Consciousness depotentiates the animus. "The animus of a woman is always powerful at the expense of the extension of her mind. As her mind expands the animus grows less powerful." H.G. Wells, *Christina Alberta's Father* (1925; footnote: according to E.A. Bennet, *What Jung Really Said* 1966 p. 93, this novel originated in conversation between Jung and Wells; CW 7, pars. 27ff., "The Relations between the Ego and the Unconscious"). "This is the progress of man, that he depotentiates the outside world; the last remnant is the idea of an absolute God, or such figures as the anima and animus. The more you increase your consciousness the less these things exist. This is what the East says."

Mother story: at a critical moment Jung was working very hard on association tests, walls covered with charts; his mother came unexpectedly, looked around, said "Do those things really mean anything?" "What she said was thin as air but it fell on me heavier than tons of lead. I did not touch a pen for three days." Then a terrific fit of anger, and he could work again.

**Dream [3] (verbatim):** "It is as if I were seeing a sort of steamroller from a point above. The machine is going and is apparently making a road, forming a particular pattern like a labyrinth. And in the dream I think, 'That is my analysis'; and then he is in the picture which he looked at from above. He is standing at the bifurcation of the road in a wood, and he does not know which way to go."

Position above = less personal, impersonal; like looking down from an airplane. Arabesque = "There is no sense in building roads that lead nowhere." Bifurcation = the doctor said there is no compulsion to go through analysis if the problem seems too difficult. Association with a wood = Dante, *Divine Comedy* (opens: "Midway in the journey of our life I found myself in a dark wood"; tr. C.S. Singleton 1970). Also story dating to 1450: monk lost his way in the Black Forest, a wolf becomes his guide to the Lower World.

**The spiral and the arabesque design (pp. 98-101; arabesque drawing reproduced p. 98):** The pattern is nearly square, consists of yonic and phallic symbols meaning the productive, generative power of nature, Yang and Yin. "One of the fundamental laws of natural development is that it moves in a spiral, and the true law of nature is always reached after the labyrinth has been travelled." The man who discovered the mathematics of the spiral was Jacob Bernoulli (1654-1705) of a famous Basel family; his gravestone: "Eadem mutata resurgo" (Always changing in the same way, yet I arise; *Die Kunstdenkmaler des Kantons Basel-Stadt* III [1941] pp. 261f.; cf. *Psychology and Alchemy* CW 12, par. 325).

"Psychologically you develop in a spiral, you always come over the same point where you have been before, but it is never exactly the same, it is either above or below."

**Gilgamesh (pp. 100-101):** (Babylonian epic, second millennium B.C.; translated into verse by Herbert Mason 1970). The Perfect Man = two-thirds divine, one-third human; man of woe and joy; the enormous swing from high to low, extraversion to introversion and vice versa. "When life is lived as rhythm, diastole and systole, then it is a whole." Dream context: "You are in the mythological situation of the hero, like Hercules. You are in the wood of the *Divine Comedy*." Two melodies woven through the design = masculine amplitude (more excessive) and feminine amplitude (less excessive).

### Lecture III: 6 February 1929 (directly read: pp. 103-113)

**Mandala: the patient's drawing (pp. 103-108):** "I want to call your attention again to the patient's drawing, because it is very important in his analysis. It is the first intimation of the whole course and purpose of analysis." The drawing = a mandala. "The word *mandala*, Sanskrit, means 'magic circle'" (Footnote 1: first use of this term in Jungian terminology for circular figures of whatever provenience; Richard Wilhelm 1873-1930, footnote 1 cites 13 Feb. 1929 as the date of Wilhelm's attendance). Two interwoven courses meet to form a spiral ending in the centre. "He had no idea to what it referred. If I had shown that design to an Eastern philosopher, he would have said, 'Oh yes, we know all about that, it is a mandala.'"

Western mandala examples: British Museum specimen from an English collection; Prof. Wilhelm recently showed Jung one from a Taoist monastery; Norman art manuscript in the treasury of Cologne cathedral dated c. 1150; Mexican Calendar Stone (Aztec); Loyola's *Exercitia Spiritualia* (Cf. Jung's lectures, Eidgenossische Technische Hochschule, Zurich 1939-1940, privately issued in English translation as vol. 4 of *Modern Psychology*). Christian mandala: Christ in centre, four Evangelists in corners (angel, eagle, ox, lion = four sons of Horus). Mandala Nritya = dance in which figures describe a mandala. Bronze Age sun-wheels in Swiss National Museum. Host in Catholic Church = mandala bread; Mithraic cult bread = mandala bread; eating the god = eating the saviour = reconciling symbol.

**Aim of the mandala exercise (pp. 103-105):** "The aim of the exercise is to shift the centre of guiding factor away from the ego to a non-ego centre in the unconscious, and this is also the general aim of analytical procedure." The ego = "only that field which is in my consciousness, but the psychic system is much vaster... We can as little assume that the earth is in the centre of the solar system as that our ego is in the centre of the psyche." Pueblo Indians make mandalas, sand-paintings, in the same fashion as Eastern mandalas; perhaps remnants of Eastern origin of the Pueblos.

**Dream [4] (next night; verbatim):** "I possess a sort of cage on a wagon, a cage which might be for lions or tigers. The cage consists of different compartments. In one of them I have four small chickens. I must watch them carefully because they are always trying to escape... I put the chickens in a basin with smooth, high sides... I see that one does not move and I think that it is because I have pressed it too hard. I think that if the chicken is dead it cannot be eaten. While I watch it, it begins to move, and I smell an aroma of roast chicken."

Associations: Cage = "Wild animals of a circus are kept in such cages. We human beings are the keepers of our thoughts, and we ought to be careful that our thoughts do not run away." Hind wheel = motive part and indispensable to the car. Four = Pythagorean mystic number (basis of all mandalas); Cf. "Psychology and Religion" 1937, CW 11, par. 61.

**I Ching Hexagram 50, The Cauldron (ting, pp. 106-109):** At the time of this seminar Richard Wilhelm's translation was in use. "The Cauldron" according to Wilhelm = cooking-pot with three legs, signifies in Yoga the technique for producing the new man. "Fire over wood. / The image of the Cauldron. / Thus the superior man consolidates his fate / By making his position correct." Alchemical parallel: the cauldron = *krater* of early Christians = retort of alchemists, "in which the new being is made. Fragments of things are thrown together which do not ordinarily mix, but they unite in the fire and produce the gold, the new man." The pot = *lapis lapidum*, philosopher's stone = stone of stones.

Chickens = "fragmentary souls or tendencies without brains which are outside in the world, in other people." Inferior function (he is an intellectual type) = feeling; "He has squeezed his feeling too much; he has been squeezing it to please his wife, but the apparent gain is not worth it." Pressing the chicken nearly dead = squeezing feeling; "Now comes in a piece of old magic. Through looking at a thing, concentrating or meditating on it, you make it grow or hatch it out." *Tapas* (Sanskrit, footnote 9): self-incubation through meditation; cf. *Symbols of Transformation* CW 5, pars. 588f. (1912 edn.).

**Eagles vs. chickens (pp. 110-113):** Police dream case: man had similar "birds" in a cage; all got out while he was away; Jung thought "Something is wrong with that man"; a year later he went bankrupt and collapsed. "It may be that these chickens are like fleas and they should 'come off.'" On whether to let the chickens run or roast them: "Whether he lets the chickens run or whether he kills them and roasts them is practically the same thing. If the chickens run away he will have a series of chicken adventures out in the open and will return from the play and have to integrate them. Or if it is not worth while to have such adventures then he must integrate these tendencies." Main conclusion: "He must learn to see himself and learn to hold himself together."

### Lecture IV: 13 February 1929 (directly read: pp. 114-124)

**I Ching and the mandala philosophy (pp. 114-118):** Questions about the *I Ching* and whether the mandala dream justifies a philosophy of four functions. Jung: "I feel that at most they indicate that there is a need for a philosophy based on psychic experience instead of on the collected abstractions of physical science." Mogul Emperor Akbar (1542-1605), Divan-i-Khas (hall of private audience) at Fatehpur Sikri (Footnote 1: Gibb had visited it; Jung visited ten years later; see "The Dreamlike World of India" 1929, CW 10, par. 983): red sandstone saucer on jet-black pillar in middle of square hall; four gangways from four corners; Akbar sat in middle while learned men from all religions discussed all philosophies with him. "He tried to make an integration for himself."

Chichen Itza mandala discovery (Earl Morris/Carnegie Institution, *Illustrated London News* 26 Jan. 1929, p. 127): nested pyramid at Chichen Itza, Yucatan; mosaic design on wooden plate inside older temple = mandala based on principle of eight, circle inside green and turquoise-blue fields, filled with reptile heads, lizard claws. "These are expressions apparently so natural that they are to be found all over the world."

Chinese mandala: "Square Inch Field of the Square Foot House" = Imperishable Body. Mead, *The Doctrine of the Subtle Body in Western Tradition* (1919). Geley's *systeme psychodynamique* = new physiology for an old thing; subtle body = *entelechia*, "the thing which tries to realize itself in existence." "By meditation on the mandala all the constituents of the subtle body are withdrawn from the outside and are concentrated in the innermost man, where they build up the imperishable body." Richard Wilhelm and Jung published "Tschang Seng Schu: Die Kunst des menschliche Leben zu verlangern" (*Europaische Revue* V:8, Nov. 1929) = precursor to *Das Geheimnis der goldenen Blute* / *The Secret of the Golden Flower*. Chinese Yoga text 1000 years old found in China, deciphered by Chinese Institute Frankfurt. "It is a sort of psychological bridge between the East and the West."

Analysis = I Ching cauldron = Yoga: "The terminology is different, but the symbolism is the same, the purpose the same." On astrology: our spring point is now at 29 degrees of Pisces, not Aries as horoscopes assume; "The spring point is about to enter Aquarius." Resistance to Eastern things = "most amazing megalomania"; China Institute at Frankfurt recently deciphered text = "a psychological bridge between the East and the West. There is no reason why we should have any sort of megalomania."

White Clouds Temple in Peking (Footnote 7: discussed in Erwin Rousselle, "Spiritual Guidance in Contemporary Taoism," *Spiritual Disciplines*, Papers from Eranos Yearbooks 4, 1960; rubbing of stone tablet in Monastery, formerly in Wilhelm's collection): Taoist Yoga picture showing spine/head/eyes/heart and rocks by a river below = mandala and cooking-pot idea together. Taoist scroll illustration reproduced p. 119.

**Mandala, Horus, and the four functions (pp. 120-122):** Mandala etymology: "orb or circle with a connotation of magic. You can draw a mandala, you can build a mandala, or you can dance a mandala." Bronze Age sun-wheels in Swiss National Museum = sun-wheel with four spokes = old Christian cross design; also the Host in Catholic Church; Mithraic cult "mandala bread" (monument showing eating the saviour); eating the totem animal = strengthening the social unity of the clan. "This is the original idea repeated eternally through the ages."

Four sons of Horus: Horus in centre, sons are his four attributes. "With Horus as with the four Evangelists, the central figure is human and the four are merely attributes. The Horus as well as the Christ (*Rex gloriae*) group symbolizes the self with three unconscious functions and one that has reached consciousness. Thus the sons of Horus are often depicted as three with animal heads and one with a human head." In our dream: four chickens = "the self, as represented by the mandala, is unconscious (animal!)." A fifth figure in centre would represent the sum total of man = four functions (conscious and unconscious) under control of a God or "non-ego centre."

Hind wheel = driving force = motivating libido; chicken escaping = libido going where the motivation is; "one doesn't know with this man whether the motivating libido is his fantasy about women or his occult studies." "A woman counts not by the intensity of feeling but by the time you spend with her. Better four hours with less insistence on beauty of feeling than fifteen minutes with only marvellous words."

**Leakage, restriction mentale, and the counterpole (pp. 122-124):** Leakage in analysis = the patient tries to establish a safe island where nothing is displaced; subterfuges are always rationalized. A certain lady falls in love with another man during analysis: "simply a safety valve for her, she is protecting herself against the transference." *Restriction mentale*: monastery case (18th century) wanting a peasant's estate; abbot learned he could apply restriction mentale by oath; he put earth from his own garden in his shoes, then stood on the peasant's land and swore "I am standing on my own earth." Cf. Antonio Gavin (fl. 1726), *Passe-partout par l'Eglise Romaine* (pub. Dublin 1724 as *A Master-key to Popery*). With this patient: "my idea is that the occult studies are the leak in the motivating libido."

### Lecture V: 20 February 1929 (directly read: pp. 125-134)

**Dream [5] (verbatim):** "I am coming to a place where a saint is worshipped; a saint who is said to cure diseases when one mentions his name. I am there because I suffer from sciatica. I think there are many other people with whom I continue my way, and someone tells me that already one patient has been cured. I think that I need to do something more than to call on the saint, that I should take a bath in the sea. I come to the shore and notice some powerful boulders on the shore towards the land. Between the boulders and the rocky hills is a valley, a sort of bay. The ocean penetrates it in quiet and powerful waves and loses itself slowly towards the recess of the bay, which penetrates the land to a considerable distance. For a while I watch the majestic surf and high swell passing in. I climb the high rocky hill. Then I am with my youngest son. We are just about to climb higher up when I see a spray of water rising above the hill we are climbing, from the other side, and then I am afraid that the surf would be so powerful on the other side that it might wash the hill away, which was not made of bed-rock but heaped-up gravel and boulders. The hill might collapse and a big swell might wash it away. On account of this I take my boy away."

Saint association: "Papatheanon or Papastheanon; I can't explain this Greek or Romanian name." Magical cure like Lourdes, tombs of saints in Mohammedan world, North Africa, Egypt: "I can only explain these cures, whose reality I certainly cannot deny, through belief in the effect, through auto-suggestion, which is always enhanced by cures." Sea = primordial medium of life. "The majestic rollers that come in from the sea: 'The unconscious sends powerful waves with almost certain regularity into our conscious, which is like the valley that contains the bay.'" Conscious = bay or lacuna in the unconscious, connected with the sea but separated by a dam or peninsula. Sea movement = rhythmic respiration of nature, "Goethe's idea of diastole and systole." The dreamer says: "But it can also be quite dangerous to approach the ocean on a stormy day."

Youngest son = his pet, he identifies with the dreamer and wants to become what his father is. "Tension on the other side is dangerous" = hill of gravel might be washed away = he is partly in a primordial ambivalent condition.

**Connection to Dream [4] (pp. 127-128):** Assembling-in-pot motif = "sacrificial food, the alchemical procedure for the reconstruction of the new man." German: *heilig* connected with *heil* = "being whole"; *geheilt* means cured; "Healing is making whole, and the condition into which one gets is a whole or complete condition, while before it was only fragments held together. So collecting and roasting the chickens means curing or making new." *Pharmakon athanasias* = medicine of immortality; *tinctura magna* of the alchemist = "you are cured for ever, you can never fall ill again." Saint Papatheanon = the doctor; "He calls me up on the telephone. 'Are you Dr. Jung? Can you cure me? How long will it take?' He invokes Dr. Jung as a saint."

Belief = "willingness to believe, a sort of expectation: 'My belief and hope are in the Lord.'" When you get into an archetypal situation "there is a great deal of feeling, and the more the unconscious is stirred the more expectation there will be that things will get into the right way."

**Archetype as instinctive natural man (pp. 128-130):** "The archetype is the instinctive natural man, as he always has been. The old priests and medicine men understood this, not by knowledge, but by intuition." Egyptian priest case: man bitten by snake-viper belonging to Isis; priest reads the Isis/Ra hymn over him (cf. *Symbols of Transformation* CW 5, pars. 451-455). "What that old Egyptian priest tried to do was to convey to that man that his suffering was not only man's fate but God's fate." Galen's pharmacopoeia = "a most amazing dung-heap, yet he was an excellent doctor." Clinical medicine today lives by outer facts, but the curative things "come from within; Christian Science recognizes them, but clinical medicine even in our day is living and working by the outer facts."

Student experiment: measured blood viscosity at different stages of analysis; viscosity much less when patient muddled or in bad frame of mind. "You know how close the connection is between the stomach and mental states. If a bad psychic state is habitual, you spoil your stomach, and it may be very serious."

**Ambiguous unconscious and Pleroma (pp. 130-132):** Unconscious contents are both existent and nonexistent simultaneously. "That is a thing you will often find in dreams and in the unconscious. It is as though you have a hundred-dollar bill in your pocket, you know you have it, you have a bill to pay with it, but you can't find it." Gnostic *Pleroma* = state of fullness where opposites (yea/nay, day/night) are together; in the state of "promise" before they become, they are neither black nor white. Symbolized in dreams as two indistinct animals, or an animal eating another. Frescoes of animals eating each other in northern Lombardy; 12th-13th century interlaced manuscript designs. *Mulungu* (central Africa, Swahili) = like *Mana* (Polynesian/Melanesian) = extraordinary power or significance; used in all states of astonishment. "Good" and "bad" originally = favourable and unfavourable, not moral: a chief was asked the difference: "When I take my enemy's woman, that is good. But when another chief takes my woman, that is bad." Categories of the mythological mind = "categories of pure reason" (Kant); Hubert and Mauss, *Melanges d'histoire des religions* (1909).

**Saint name and sciatica (pp. 132-134):** "Papa" in saint's name = Father, also Pope; Cult of Attis had temple on site of St. Peter's in Rome, high priest called "Papas." The patient speaks Italian and Romanian, knows Greek and Latin. Miss Bianchi suggests *The Magic Flute* (Papageno = Papagallo = parrot, Polynesian word of exotic origin; footnote 5: transcriber of the *Sems.* evidently misheard the name). Saint = guru, spiritual advisor; "the saint means the guru, the leader, guide, and spiritual adviser; so the patient is put into an archetypal situation." On the Nile: Arab lying on a barge calling "Allah!" all night for malaria = invocation of God's name for cure. Greek papyrus attributed to Mithras cult: initiate instructed to hold his sides and shout the name of Mithras. "This patient has travelled in the East, and no doubt his unconscious has assimilated these things."

Sciatica = "a painful affection of the leg... it suggests that the machine would not go, he could not go forward, certain developments cannot take place." Leg disease in dreams suggests "things are at a standstill, also that there is not only mental but physical trouble." Mrs. Sigg: "His lack of sexuality, a physiological trouble. He has no physical relation to his wife; call it a gland trouble if you wish, a complicated condition, something physical which leads him to the idea of bathing in the sea."
