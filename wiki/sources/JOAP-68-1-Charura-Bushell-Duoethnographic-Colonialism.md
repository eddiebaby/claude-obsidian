# A Duoethnographic Exploration of Colonialism in the Cultural Layer of the Objective Psyche

Navigation: [[depth-psychology]] | [[sources/_index|Sources]]

## Bibliographic Details

- Authors: [[Divine Charura]] (York, England) and [[Stephen Bushell]] (Girona, Spain)
- *Journal of Analytical Psychology*, 2023, Vol. 68, Issue 1, pp. 27–47
- DOI: 10.1111/1468-5922.12878
- Open access (CC BY-NC-ND); © 2023 The Authors, published by Wiley on behalf of The Society of Analytical Psychology

## Summary

A two-year [[Duoethnography|duoethnographic]] dialogue between a Black British man of Zimbabwean heritage (Charura) and a White British man (Bushell), using Jung's theory of the layered objective psyche and archetypes — supported by African [[Ubuntu]] philosophy — to develop two new theoretical constructs: the **[[Colonizer Archetype]]** and the **[[Ethnic Shadow]]**. The paper argues these are not metaphors for colonialism but the underlying archetypal pattern of which historical colonialism is one manifestation, and that raising this material to consciousness — individually and collectively — is a precondition for a "personal and cultural shift away from oppression."

## The Six Conceptualized Themes

1. **Belonging**
2. **The layered psyche and our understanding of difference**
3. **Facing the ethnic shadow**
4. **The colonizing archetype in the consulting room**
5. **The exploration of colonial structures in the psyche**
6. **Valuing emancipatory encounter**

## Method: Duoethnography

See [[Duoethnography]] for the full method page. In brief: monthly Zoom dialogue + email exchange over two years, following a shared diversity-training encounter; transcripts analysed for themes; direct quotes retained in the published paper "to demonstrate the trustworthiness of our process." Norris & Sawyer's founding claim — that in duoethnography "neither position can claim dominance or universal truth" — is structurally important: the method itself performs the paper's anti-colonizing argument (neither author's account is allowed to become the authoritative one).

## Core Theoretical Moves

**Cultural layer of the objective psyche.** Building on Jung's 1925 seminar notes (*Introduction to Jungian Psychology*, ed. McGuire 2012) and Tacey's 2012 gloss, the authors treat the collective unconscious as internally layered: family and tribal layers sit closer to consciousness, resting on a cultural layer that "undergirds the stability, customs and distinctiveness of cultures." See [[Cultural Layer of the Psyche]].

**The Self as archetype of diversity.** The Self — totality and unity of all opposites — is read as containing "the potentiality for all forms of human cultural expression," with the tendency of oppositional positions (e.g. Black/White) to polarize being a precondition for activating the [[transcendent function]] (Jung 1916/1957). Polarization is not pathology but the raw material of reconciliation. See [[Self (archetype)]].

**The Colonizer Archetype.** A pattern manifesting in all forms of oppression and inequality, focused on the polarized opposites colonizer–colonized. Full treatment: [[Colonizer Archetype]].

**The Ethnic Shadow.** The collective-shadow content specifically organized around a people's or nation's history of racial/colonial domination — split off, unconscious, and requiring a two-level (societal + internal) dialogue to face. Full treatment: [[Ethnic Shadow]].

**Jung's own duoethnographic encounters.** Jung's 1920 North Africa trip, his 1925 conversation with Taos Pueblo chief Mountain Lake (Ochwiay Biano), and the subsequent five-month 1925–26 stay with the Elgonyi people on Mount Elgon, Uganda, are read as evidence for — and against — Jung. For: they were genuinely transformative encounters that produced some of Jung's most important insight (man as "the second creator of the world"). Against: Jung's *theorizing* about the psyche of Africans and other non-European peoples is the documented basis of the charge that he was, in Dalal's (1988) phrase, "a racist" — a charge the authors do not dispute, while declining to let the paper "stop there" (per Samuels 2018).

## The Grandmothers Passage

A key structural device: Bushell's grandmother Nan J (East End London, one of 13 children, working-class poverty, later resentful of 1960s Indian/Pakistani immigration to Tower Hamlets) and Charura's great-grandmother Nana T (Zimbabwe/Rhodesia, colonized as a child, recorded saying of the arriving white colonizers "they had no knees, and they had no ears!"). The authors use the parallel — and the asymmetry — to show how family-layer material carries cultural-layer colonial structuring differently depending on which side of the colonizer/colonized divide a family stands: "come to work here on our terms" (Britain) versus "we will rule you and benefit from your land" (Rhodesia).

## The Colonizing Archetype Intrapsychically

The paper extends the archetype beyond the social-historical register into individual psychic structure:

- **Ego formation as colonization**: the infant's separation from the mother is read as the colonizing archetype providing the psychic energy for the "I" to establish and defend its territory in consciousness.
- **Complexes vying for dominance**: Jung's Word Association Test (1909, CW 2) is cited as evidence that complexes compete to "colonize" the space of consciousness; a simple concentration exercise (holding attention on a candle) demonstrates the same colonizing dynamic in miniature. Meditation is framed as "initially about noticing this colonizing phenomenon."
- **Psychosis as the limit case**: when consciousness is occupied by an archetypally-driven complex that determines speech and behaviour, "consciousness has become colonized by the activated archetypes."

## The Colonizing Archetype in the Consulting Room

The therapist's territory (the consulting room) is offered to the client as an "other," and — the authors argue — *ipso facto* the colonizing archetype is prone to constellate in the resulting dyad. Named mechanisms of clinical colonizing: demanding the client adopt the therapist's interpretive language; holding onto interpretations after they fail to resonate; interrupting; fixing the client into ready-made narrative slots ("she is stuck in early trauma"); supervisor/supervisee dynamics that "oppress" client material with imposed interpretation instead of working from the shared imaginal field. The clinical task: notice the negative thought/feeling about a client, track its origin, and hold the resulting psychic energy in the inner *vas* (vessel) of conscious awareness — an explicitly alchemical container image — rather than discharging it onto the client. See [[Colonizer Archetype]] for the transformation mechanics.

## Decolonizing the Therapeutic Field

Once early-stage safety is established, the work opens into a shared **[[mundus imaginalis]]** — a genuinely non-colonized imaginative space belonging to neither party alone, which both "take the knee" to as emergent material arises. See [[mundus imaginalis]] and [[Ubuntu]].

## Emancipatory Encounter

The paper's closing move: the pain held by the colonized and the shame held by the colonizer are not opposite poles that cancel out, but each conceals the other — "within the colonizer's shame dwells a colossal pain of blind inter-generational wounding... in the pain of the colonized, lies the inter-generational shame of the loss of the dignity of one's life and culture." Recognizing this shared substrate is framed as "the gift of the transcendent function." The authors caution against a subtler danger — treating the other as "a commodity to enhance our own growth... another kind of colonialism" — and close by returning to Ubuntu: "my belonging is found in being the other to you."

## Critique of Jung Engaged Directly

The paper does not defend Jung's writing on race. It cites [[Andrew Samuels|Samuels]] (2018) "Jung and 'Africans': a critical and contemporary review," the 2019 Samuels-led Open Letter (acknowledging "the lack of regard in Jung of the psyche of black people"), Dalal (1988) "Jung: a racist," and the Brewster (2013)/Morgan (2013) exchange in *Jung Journal* / *JAP* ("Wheel of Fire: the African American dreamer and cultural unconsciousness"). It draws on [[Sam Kimbles|Kimbles]]' (2014) *Phantom Narratives* to explain — not excuse — why Jung "would not have escaped these dynamics, given his context." It balances this with [[Namfundo Lily-Rose Mlisa]]'s 2019 IAAP Vienna address naming Jung "our ancestor," and Peter Ammann's characterization of Jung on Mount Elgon as a "participant observer... deeply affected and changed by the African experience."
