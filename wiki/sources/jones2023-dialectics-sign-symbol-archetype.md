# Dialectics of Sign and Symbol and the Utterance of Archetype Theory

[[Raya Jones]]'s 2023 essay (*Journal of Analytical Psychology*, 68:4, 687–705) is a metatheoretical audit of Jung's archetype concept — not a clinical application of it. Read in full directly. Its organizing device is Bakhtin's **[[Utterance (Bakhtin)|utterance]]**: archetype theory treated as a century-long communicative act rather than a settled proposition, pulled between centripetal (fixed-meaning) and centrifugal (contested, evolving) forces.

## The Core Claim: Archetype as Sign AND Symbol

Jung drew a strict dichotomy: "An expression that stands for a known thing remains a mere sign and is never a symbol" (Jung, 1921, para. 817). Jones's thesis is that "archetype" itself lives as both simultaneously:

- **As sign** — a generic technical term implying archetypes are something known to trained psychologists.
- **As symbol** — "the best possible expression at the moment for a fact as yet unknown," standing for something Jung himself calls essentially "irrepresentable" (Jung, 1954a, para. 417).

Full treatment: [[Sign and Symbol]].

## Philosophical Lineage: Plato → Kant → Schopenhauer → Jung

Jung's own history-of-philosophy sketch for "archetype" (from Philo Judaeus and St Augustine through Plato, Spinoza, Kant's [[a-priori|*a priori*]] categories, and Schopenhauer) is, per Jones's earlier work (2003), "less a *history* of an idea than … the *story* of an archetype, in his sense of the term" — the theory performing its own thesis. Jones flags the circularity explicitly: Jung claimed "all the most powerful ideas in history go back to archetypes," including, by his own logic, his own theory.

## Models, Modules, Muddles: Evolutionary Psychology Is Not Archetype Theory

Jones distinguishes archetype-modularity from Cosmides & Tooby's (1992) Massive Modularity Hypothesis in evolutionary psychology. Darwinian modules (e.g., number cognition, well-evidenced in nonhuman animals and preverbal infants) explain *adaptive capacities*; they cannot explain why the number 3 carries *religious/symbolic* significance across cultures ("Arrangement in triads is an archetype in the history of religion," Jung 1948b, para. 173) — there is no survival advantage to a number being "lucky." Non-Jungian uses of "archetype" (Frye's literary archetypes, Cutler & Breiman's statistical archetypal analysis, Owen's 1840s vertebrate archetype in biology) all stay at the level of visible phenomena and pattern-clustering; Jung's is distinctively a claim about invisible psychic causation.

## The Categorization Problem

Jung's own corpus shows the same rebirth motif assigned to different archetypes at different times: mother symbolism in the 1912/1952 *Symbols of Transformation* revisions, but **Individuation** in a 1939 lecture on the Khidr legend (Jung, 1950). Jones treats this not as inconsistency but as the direct, admitted consequence of Jung's own caveat: "Clear-cut distinctions and strict formulations are quite impossible in this field, seeing that a kind of fluid interpenetration belongs to the very nature of all archetypes" (Jung, 1949, para. 301). The Khidr/transhumanist-immortality comparison (Kurzweil's "we will gain power over our fates … we will live forever," quoted via Jones 2017) is used to sharpen the question: do superficial thematic parallels (gaining immortality, dwelling in an "invisible kingdom"/cyberspace) suffice to call two utterly unrelated cultural sources expressions of "the same" archetype-as-such? Jones's answer: it depends entirely on how archetypes are conceptualized — there is no theory-neutral fact of the matter.

## Peirce's Trichotomy — A Comparison That Doesn't Quite Fit

[[Charles Sanders Peirce|Peirce's]] 1903 icon/index/symbol trichotomy is introduced as a rigorous alternative semiotic system and shown *not* to map cleanly onto Jung's dichotomy — Peirce asks how representations formally convey information; Jung asks why some representations carry disproportionate psychic weight. Full treatment: [[Peircean Semiotics]]. Jones extends this to argue archetype theory itself functions like a Peircean *index* (a signpost only legible to those trained to read it) — "one must speak Jungianism in order to follow the signage to specific archetypes."

## Science and Credibility: The Germ-Plasm Problem

Jung grounded archetype credibility in a biological claim: if archetypes "ever 'originated' their origin must have coincided at least with the beginning of the species," hence hereditary, "already present in the germ-plasm" (Jung, 1954b, para. 152). Jones identifies this as effectively Lamarckian ("endless repetition has engraved these experiences into our psychic constitution," Jung 1936, para. 99) despite germ-plasm theory (1880s) being explicitly anti-Lamarckian. Because archetypes-as-such cannot be directly observed, "Jung's hypothesis cannot be confirmed or refuted by means of the scientific method." Jones surveys the modern defenses (Stevens 1982 — archetype as a CNS "centre"; Maloney 2003 — compatible with evolutionary theory/neuroscience/cognitive science) and the modern rejections (Goodwyn 2019, Hogenson 2019, Merchant 2019; Knox 2003 and McDowell 2001 — archetypes as emergent properties of dynamic systems, not brain structures) and sides explicitly with **[[Christian-Roesler|Roesler]] (2012)**: "we Jungians cannot go on basing our theory of archetypes on scientific assumptions which have been falsified.… It is important that we stop arguing that archetypes are transmitted genetically if we want to be taken seriously" (p. 234). This is the same critique the vault already has on record at book length via [[roesler2025-process-of-transformation]] — an earlier (2012), independent statement of it, now doubly evidenced.

Jones also surveys Becker & Neuberg (2019, *Psychological Inquiry*), a non-Jungian evolutionary/developmental/cognitive reading that is favorable to Jung's *insights* but explicitly does not treat myth/fairytale analysis as "legitimate scientific attitude" for psychology — and Boyd et al.'s (2019) blunt commentary that archetype theory is "fundamentally broken … outdated and unnecessary." Jones's own verdict: she prefers to read the internal Jungian debate as "attesting to the vitality of Jung's legacy," but is clear-eyed that outside analytical psychology's own enclave, the theory's credibility problem is structural — Jung wanted a falsifiable scientific hypothesis but built (and defended) an irreducibly hermeneutic method.

## The Positive Proposal: Patterning of the Experiential Realm

Jones's constructive alternative to "archetype as switched-on structure" (the car/ignition-key model): the archetypal as a **probabilistic patterning of the experiential realm** — a pattern that cannot exist apart from the particular way things are arranged, and which not every person will instantiate (not every visitor to Notre Dame experiences its interior as womblike). She borrows Gibson's (1979) ecological theory of **affordances** — "the ground affords support… but water does not; it is different for water bugs" — to bridge physical constraint and symbolic expression: the impossibility of walking on water constrains navigation of the physical world *and* fosters symbolic expressions of that limitation (Jesus walking on water). The "human quality" of being human, per Jung's own phrase (1954b, para. 152), is the *reflective instinct* (Jung, 1937, para. 241) — the uniquely human capacity to form symbolic representations of lived experience, not the instinctual behavior itself (infant mammals attach to mothers via specialized neural circuitry; only humans build symbolic representations of what that felt like).

Jones distinguishes this from setting out to "prove" an innate archetype by hunting for its supposed expressions across unrelated sources — citing Goodwyn's (2013) "resonant attractor states" model (narratives cluster in a field wherever they "align well with the reliably emergent universal patterns in the human mind") and Baydala & Smythe's (2012) "deep background" (Searle) as compatible reformulations, while explicitly rejecting Singer's (2006) "archetypal defences of the group spirit" reading of geopolitical events as a form of "psychohistory that leaves the 'history' out" (Lu, 2013) — a critique Jones extends: it also leaves out the individual psychological subject. In-group favoritism, she notes, is already well-explained without archetype theory by Tajfel's social identity theory and infant preference experiments (Hamlin et al., 2013).

## The Case of the Motherly City

A worked textual example: comparing Jung's 1912 *Psychology of the Unconscious* against its 1952 revision (*Symbols of Transformation*, CW 5) on the "city is a maternal symbol" material. The 1912 version aligns with Freud (incest-taboo diversion of libido toward the mother); post-1912, having decoupled libido from sexuality, the 1952 revision reframes the same material as a desire to return to childlike security, and replaces the 1912 reading of the Biblical harlot-riding-a-dragon as mythical Babylon with a historically grounded reading of the dragon's seven heads as Rome's seven hills — "a direct allusion to Rome, the city whose temporal power oppressed the world at the time." Jones reads this revision as Jung's own thought maturing toward anchoring the archetypal in actual, sociopolitically-situated human experience rather than purely mythological abstraction — evidence for her own thesis in miniature.

## Concluding Reflection

Jung theorized human irrationality while believing his own insights were rational, hence scientific: "Since every scientific theory contains a hypothesis, and is therefore an anticipatory description of something still essentially unknown, it is a symbol" (Jung, 1921, para. 819). Jones's closing line: "the discourse of archetype is thus characterized by tensions between intellectualization and insight, sign and symbol, the named and the nameless" — archetype theory itself, a product of the "disenchantment of the world" (Weber, 1919) that it set out to push back against.
