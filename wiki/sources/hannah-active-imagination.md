# Active Imagination: Encounters with the Soul

Barbara Hannah's full-length practical treatment of active imagination, built around six worked case examples (Edward, Sylvia, Beatrice, the ancient-Egyptian "World-Weary Man and His Ba," Hugh of St. Victor's 12th-century conversation with his anima, and Anna Marjula's own first-person case history) plus a closing chapter on "The Eternal Search for the Inner, Great Spirit." Introduction by [[Marie-Louise von Franz]].

## Von Franz's Introduction — *Imaginatio Vera et Non Phantastica*

Von Franz's short Introduction supplies the precedent later worked out at length in [[vonfranz-alchemical-active-imagination]]: the alchemists already had a term for genuine inner work as opposed to idle fantasizing — *imaginatio vera et non phantastica*, "true imagination, not fantastical [imagination]." This single phrase is the historical bridge connecting Jung's 20th-century clinical method to centuries of alchemical practice, and is the explicit reason von Franz later devoted an entire book to reading Gerhard Dorn's alchemical texts as a record of active imagination already being practiced (without the name) in the 16th century.

## Chapter 1 — "Confronting the Unconscious": The Practical Theory

Hannah opens with the same Rainmaker of Kiau Tchou story used in [[jung-on-active-imagination]] (Chodorow's version) — Jung told it repeatedly at the Psychological Club, including at one of his last Christmas dinners before his death. Hannah's framing of its meaning: active imagination's greatest use is to "put us, like the rainmaker, into harmony with the Tao, so that the right things may happen around us instead of the wrong" — restoring inner order rather than fighting outer disorder directly.

**Discovery, not invention.** Hannah is emphatic: active imagination's contents are *discovered*, not invented. "No one who is convinced that the unconscious has no life of its own should even attempt the method." This is the precondition for the technique working at all — approaching it as creative-writing exercise rather than genuine encounter defeats it.

**The only real danger is panic.** Hannah states this plainly and repeats it: the dangerous element in active imagination is not the unconscious material itself but the ego's panic response to it. Friendliness toward the unconscious — meeting whatever appears with curiosity rather than fear — is what keeps the technique safe. She illustrates with Jung's own Elgonyi/"District Commissioner" anecdote (East African expedition) and a reference to Laurens van der Post's Bushman material, both used to show that traditional societies treat the inner world with a working respect modern Westerners have lost.

**Shadow before anima/animus.** As a rule the personal shadow must be made conscious before a real *Auseinandersetzung* with the anima or animus is possible — though Hannah flags Edward's case (Chapter 2) as an exception, where the work with the anima precedes shadow work. Anna Marjula's case (Chapter 7) shows the more typical order: the shadow keeps intervening wherever it hasn't yet been seen.

**The "plane-being" teaching example.** Hannah recounts a personal experience: stuck in her own analysis, Jung had her imagine herself as a two-dimensional being experiencing his three-dimensional fingertips on a table — she could only register them as flat shapes whose actual connection (through the hand, in the third dimension) was unknowable to her. Jung's point: this is exactly our position relative to the unconscious. We are conscious of three dimensions; the figures of the unconscious approach us from an unknown fourth. The parallel must not be pushed too far, but it explains why a real *Auseinandersetzung* requires first knowing the personal shadow thoroughly — anything else risks being dismissed into "the next dimension" the way the two-dimensional being would lose sight of anything it disliked.

**Technique basics** (from Jung's commentary on "The Secret of the Golden Flower," quoted at length): be alone and undisturbed; concentrate on seeing or hearing whatever comes up from the unconscious; prevent the image from sinking back by drawing, painting, writing, movement, or dance; let things happen rather than steering them, but never let the first image change like a kaleidoscope — stick with it until it explains itself. Some people cannot reach the unconscious directly; writing fictional stories "about other people" is offered as an indirect approach that reliably reveals what the storyteller is unconscious of (the technique used to striking effect in the Sylvia case, Ch. 3).

**The Odyssey as prototype.** Hannah reads the Telemachus/Menelaus books of the *Odyssey* as an extended worked example of active imagination technique, predating any individual modern case by three thousand years. Menelaus's encounter with the shape-shifting sea-god Proteus — who must be held fast through transformations into lion, snake, boar, water, tree before he yields his answer — is offered as "a particularly valuable lesson in the technique of active imagination: the importance of sticking to one image" and not letting it escape through changeability.

## Chapter 2 — The Case of Edward (worked example)

A man in his early forties, in analysis with one of Jung's assistants, suffering temporary impotence, undertakes active imagination starting from a dream: he finds himself in a brothel, propositioned by a serious, formally dressed woman in black silk who says only "À bientôt." Edward's year-long active imagination opens by continuing this scene.

The transcript Hannah quotes in full shows the practical mechanics: Edward enters dialogue with the woman (who turns out to be an anima figure imprisoned in the brothel — "bewitched, cursed, exiled in this hell," waiting for a man who can liberate her by something other than sex), and a second figure — "the Devil" — repeatedly interrupts to mock Edward and urge him toward exactly the impotent, defensive sexual acting-out that brought him to analysis in the first place. The anima figure explicitly rejects this: "You think you can overcome your impotence in such a way? ... You would only fall into complete misfortune, into a trap, from which you would never escape. There is a spiritual reason for your impotence. You must search for this." This is Hannah's clearest illustration of a **common obstacle**: lesser, more seductive or more cynical figures (the prostitute, the Devil) crowding into the dialogue to pull the work toward acting-out rather than genuine confrontation — and the discipline of staying with the difficult, demanding figure (the anima in black) rather than the easy one.

Jung's own observation on the *style* such dialogues take: "Archetypes speak the language of high rhetoric, even of bombast. It is a style I find embarrassing... But since I did not know what was going on, I had no choice but to write everything down in the style selected by the unconscious itself" (MDR, p.178) — a methodological note about not editing the unconscious's own register for the sake of taste.

## Note on incomplete coverage

This ingest read the Introduction and Chapter 1 (theory) and Chapter 2 (Edward case, opening transcript) in full. Chapters 3-8 — Sylvia, Beatrice, the World-Weary Man and His Ba, Hugh of St. Victor, Anna Marjula's first-person case history, and the closing chapter — were surveyed via the table of contents only and not read in depth. A follow-up ingest could extract the Anna Marjula material specifically, since it is the only case told in the patient's own words and Hannah credits it with an unusually thorough *Auseinandersetzung* with both the negative and positive sides of the animus.
