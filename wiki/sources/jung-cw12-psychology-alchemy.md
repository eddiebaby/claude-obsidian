# Psychology and Alchemy (CW Vol. 12)

Jung's 1944 magnum opus establishing the psychological interpretation of Western alchemy. 270 illustrations. The central argument: the alchemists were projecting the individuation process onto matter. The symbolism of the opus alchymicum — its stages, its goal (the lapis), its presiding spirit (Mercurius) — is the unconscious's own language for what Jung calls individuation.

---

## Structure

**Part I: Introduction to the Religious and Psychological Problems of Alchemy** (paras 1-43)

Jung's theoretical statement. Alchemy as the compensatory undercurrent to Christianity; the alchemists' unconscious as the psychic laboratory; the problem of projection and the path from projection to self-knowledge.

**Part II: Individual Dream Symbolism in Relation to Alchemy** (paras 44+)

The empirical heart of the book. 400+ dreams from an anonymous dreamer (a young man of scientific education, later identified as Wolfgang Pauli, the physicist). Jung analyzed approximately 355 of these dreams without personal contact with the patient. The dream series is read as an autonomous individuation sequence; Jung systematically parallels each major dream symbol with alchemical imagery from medieval texts.

Chapters:
- Ch. 1: Introduction (Material and Method; The Initial Dreams)
- Ch. 2: Initial Dreams
- Ch. 3: Symbolism of the Mandala (four sections)

**Part III: Religious Ideas in Alchemy** (paras 400+)

Historical survey. The lapis-Christ parallel from Zosimos of Panopolis through Raymond Lully, Tractatus aureus, Petrus Bonus, Aurora consurgens, to Paracelsus and later alchemists. Evidence that the lapis carried the same psychological charge as the Redeemer figure in Christianity.

Chapters:
- Ch. 1: Basic Concepts of Alchemy (Alchemical Process and Its Stages; Conceptions and Symbols of the Goal)
- Ch. 2: Psychic Nature of the Alchemical Work
- Ch. 3: The Work
- Ch. 4: Prima Materia
- Ch. 5: Lapis-Christ Parallel
- Ch. 6: Alchemical Symbolism in the History of Religion

## Part I: Key Arguments (paras 1-43, read in full)

### Alchemy as Compensatory Undercurrent (paras 1-26)

Christianity elevated spirit and denigrated matter. Alchemy worked in matter — with metals, furnaces, retorts, volatile substances. But the alchemical texts disclose an obsessive concern with what Jung calls "the divine image in matter": the hidden god imprisoned in the prima materia, who must be liberated by the opus.

Para 26 states the structural argument: alchemy was "like an undercurrent to the Christianity that ruled on the surface." It compensated for what official religion repressed, above all the chthonic, the feminine, and the body.

### Maria Prophetissa's Axiom (para 26)

The Axiom of Maria: "One becomes two, two becomes three, and out of the third comes the one as the fourth." Jung calls this the psychological leitmotiv of the whole of alchemy and traces it through the entire volume as the basic formula of the transformation process. The progression from unity through duality and trinity to quaternary wholeness is the structure of individuation.

### Projection and Its Withdrawal (paras 39-43)

The alchemists did not know they were projecting. They believed they were studying the objective properties of matter. But the language they used — the king who must die, the queen with whom he unites, the dragon, the black sun, the white rose — is the language of the unconscious.

Para 40: "What the symbolism of alchemy expresses is the whole problem of the evolution of personality described above, the so-called individuation process."

This implies the alchemists were doing unconscious psychological work without knowing it. Their failure to complete the opus was precisely their failure to withdraw the projection and recognize that the transforming substance was the psyche itself.

## Part II: The Dream Series (paras 44-57, partial read)

### The Anonymous Dreamer

A young man of scientific rationalist background. He does not know who will analyze his dreams (Jung arranged to receive the series without direct contact). This anonymity is methodologically significant: the absence of a transference removes one major variable. The dreams proceed without analyst influence.

### Circumambulation and the Center

From the opening of the dream series: the dreams do not move linearly but spiral. Each cycle returns to the same center from a different angle. Jung calls this circumambulation — the spiral movement around the Self that defines the individuation process. The longissima via (the longest way) is not the straight line but the serpentine path that keeps returning to deepen rather than advance.

### Mandala Dreams

The earliest mandalas in the dream series appear before the dreamer has any knowledge of mandala symbolism. They emerge autonomously. Jung traces the progressive development of circular motifs through the series, showing how the psyche's own symbolic production parallels medieval alchemical imagery: the squaring of the circle, the rotundum, the world-egg, the philosophical city.

## Part III: Religious Ideas in Alchemy (key sections read)

### Lapis-Christ Parallel (paras 452-469)

The lapis (Philosopher's Stone) carries functionally the same symbolic charge as Christ. Both are:
- Born of lowly matter (the lapis from prima materia; Christ from a virgin and the stable)
- Capable of unlimited transformation and multiplication
- A redeemer figure (the lapis redeems the metals from their imperfection; Christ redeems mankind from sin)
- Identified with the Anthropos, the primordial Man

Para 452: "Without knowing it, the alchemist carries the idea of the imitatio a stage further and reaches the conclusion we mentioned earlier, that complete assimilation to the Redeemer would enable him, the assimilated, to continue the work of redemption in the depths of his own psyche."

The alchemist's Christ analog is the stone, not himself — he sees the correspondence to the Redeemer in his wonderful stone. This is the unconscious projection at its most visible: the alchemist cannot yet say "I, regarded not as ego but as Self, have taken over the work of redeeming not man but God."

### Raymond Lully and the Earliest Lapis-Christ Text (para 453)

The oldest known text identifying lapis with Christ: Codicillus (Ch. IX) of Raymond Lully (1235-1315). "And as Jesus Christ, of the house of David, took on human nature for the deliverance and redemption of mankind... so likewise in our art that which has been wrongfully defiled by one thing is absolved by its opposite; cleansed, and delivered from that stain."

### Zosimos of Panopolis and the Anthropos (paras 456-461)

Zosimos (3rd-4th century CE), the earliest named alchemical writer, provides texts that show the Son of God explicitly identified with the Anthropos, the spiritual Man. The Anthropos is:
- Named Thoyth (Thoth) in his outer form; his inner name is Light (phos)
- Quaternal: "named symbolically after the four elements of the whole heavenly sphere"
- Functionally identical to the lapis as materia lapidis

Para 457: "To all appearances, Zosimos' Son of God is a Gnostic Christ who has more affinity with the Iranian conception of Gayomart than with the Jesus of the Gospels."

Para 460: "The alchemical parallel to this polarity is the double nature of Mercurius, which shows itself most clearly in the Uroboros, the dragon that devours, fertilizes, begets, slays, and brings itself to life again. Being hermaphroditic, it is compounded of opposites and is at the same time their uniting symbol."

### Aurora Consurgens (paras 464-469)

The alchemical treatise attributed (probably falsely) to Thomas Aquinas. Begins with Wisdom of Solomon and Proverbs. The Sapientia Dei (Wisdom of God) as the "regina Austri" — the Queen of the South who comes from the East like the rising dawn. The alchemical Sapientia is the feminine counterpart of the Logos: the anima mundi, the Sophia, the divine feminine in matter.
