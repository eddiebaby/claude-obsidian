# Jung on Active Imagination

A reader assembled by Jungian dance/movement therapist Joan Chodorow, gathering Jung's own writing on active imagination from across the Collected Works (the Tavistock Lectures, "The Transcendent Function," "The Technique of Differentiation," MDR excerpts, and other shorter pieces) with a long interpretive Introduction.

## Chodorow's Introduction — The Discovery and Its Risk

Active imagination grew directly out of Jung's own 1913-1917 "confrontation with the unconscious" (covered in [[Confrontation with the Unconscious]] and the MDR material already in [[Active Imagination]]). Chodorow's Introduction adds two pieces of framing not yet in the wiki:

**The Nietzsche cautionary parallel.** Chodorow places Jung's self-experimentation against the case of Nietzsche, whose unrooted, ungrounded confrontation with his own unconscious material (visible already in *Thus Spake Zarathustra*) ended in psychotic inflation rather than integration. Jung treated this as a structural warning, not a personality difference: the same material that individuates one person can shatter another if approached without an external anchor — Jung's anchor was his ongoing clinical practice, his family, and his discipline of always returning to write the experience down and try to *understand* it rather than simply living inside it. > [!contradiction] Nietzsche as cautionary case appears independently in both this source and in [[hannah-active-imagination|Barbara Hannah's account]] (Ch. 1) — the two sources converge almost word-for-word on this point, suggesting it was a standing teaching example in Jung's own circle, not Chodorow's individual framing.

**The Rainmaker of Kiau Tchou.** Chodorow gives the full Taoist parable Jung told to illustrate the goal of active imagination: a village in drought sends for a rainmaker, who asks to be left alone in a hut; after three days it rains and snows. Asked how he made it rain, the rainmaker says he didn't — "I am not responsible for it... I come from a place where people are in order; they are in Tao. But the weather was out of order, so I was also infected by it. I remained alone until I was once more in Tao, and then, of course, it snowed." The point: the **greatest use of active imagination is to put oneself into harmony with the Tao**, so the right things happen around you instead of the wrong — restoring inner order is how outer disorder resolves, not the reverse. (This exact story, told almost identically, recurs at the opening of [[hannah-active-imagination|Hannah's book]] — see that source page.)

## Jung's Own Two-Stage Model

Where the existing wiki page (built from MDR and von Franz's *Man and His Symbols*) describes active imagination procedurally, this source supplies **Jung's own theoretical scaffolding**, given in "The Transcendent Function" (CW 7/8, pars. 145-193):

1. **Letting the unconscious come up** — switching off conscious direction, giving fantasy material the opportunity to surface without forcing or steering it.
2. ***Auseinandersetzung*** — "coming to terms with" the unconscious material once it has surfaced. This is the harder, ethically serious half: not just receiving the image but entering negotiation with it, taking a real position, refusing to simply submit to or be flooded by it.

Later Jungians (von Franz, Dallett, Robert Johnson) elaborated this into four steps (emptying the mind / inviting the image / giving it expression / integrating it ethically), but Jung's own writing stays with the two-stage model — letting come, then having it out.

The product of holding the tension through stage two honestly is the **transcendent function**: not a "tertium non datur" (a logical stillbirth, an empty synthesis) but, in Jung's words, "a living birth that leads to a new level of being" (CW 7/8, par. 189). The "third thing" is not a compromise between the two positions but a genuinely new standpoint that contains and transcends both.

## Active vs. Passive Fantasy — "The Fiancée on the Ice"

From "The Technique of Differentiation" (CW 7, pars. 341-359), Jung distinguishes two kinds of fantasy that look similar on the surface but differ completely in effect:

- **Passive fantasy** simply happens to a person — it is involuntary, has a compulsive, intrusive quality, and the ego is essentially a spectator. It can be symptomatic of a psyche under unconscious pressure it cannot yet handle.
- **Active fantasy** is deliberately entered: the ego consciously concentrates on a starting point and then participates in what develops, holding its own position rather than being merely carried along.

Jung's illustrative case: a patient repeatedly produced a vivid, intrusive fantasy image of his fiancée out on thin ice, in danger of drowning — a passive fantasy that recurred and disturbed him without resolving anything. Jung's technique was to have the patient deliberately re-enter the same image, this time as an *active* participant: not just watching her risk drowning, but engaging the scene, asking what it wanted, responding within it. The shift from passive to active fantasy is the shift from being possessed by unconscious content to working with it — this is the operational definition of active imagination as a *technique*, as distinct from ordinary daydreaming or intrusive fantasy.

## Guardrails (Expansion)

This source sharpens the danger Jung attaches to the method beyond the existing wiki's general caution:

- Jung insisted active imagination is not for everyone and is contraindicated for patients close to or in psychosis — the technique deliberately lowers the threshold between conscious and unconscious, which is the opposite of what a fragile ego needs.
- The real danger, per both this source and Hannah's companion volume, is **panic** — not the unconscious material itself. A person who can stay calm in the presence of strange or frightening figures is rarely harmed by them; panic is what turns an encounter into a flood.
- The Nietzsche case (above) is the standing illustration of what happens when the method (or its informal equivalent — sustained, undisciplined inner monologue with one's own unconscious) is pursued without an external anchor or a genuine ethical *Auseinandersetzung*.
