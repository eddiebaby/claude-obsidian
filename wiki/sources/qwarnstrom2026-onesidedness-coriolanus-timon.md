# One-Sidedness and the Inferior Function in *Coriolanus* and *Timon of Athens*

[[Sofie Qwarnström]]'s 2026 *Journal of Analytical Psychology* essay reads two Shakespeare tragedies as matched clinical case studies in Jungian [[One-Sidedness|one-sidedness]] — the "fundamental tragic trait" A.C. Bradley (1905) identified in Shakespeare independently of, and decades before, Jung's typology gave it a structural name.

## The Argument

Jung: as a personality develops, one function (of the eight in [[Psychological Types]]) inevitably differentiates into dominance, and its diametric opposite is correspondingly repressed into the unconscious as the **inferior function**. This is not optional — "selection demands direction... this is bound to make the conscious orientation one-sided" (Jung, *Psychological Types* ¶694) — but sustained beyond a certain point it becomes dangerous: the repressed opposite accumulates compensatory pressure until it erupts, a flip Jung calls **[[Enantiodromia|enantiodromia]]**.

Classical *hubris* is read as the same structure under an older name: not arrogance, but "virtue gone mad" — Michelini's philological point that ancient texts apply *hubris* to plants suffering "superabundance of nurture," requiring pruning. Robust strength, ill-directed, becomes self-destructive. Bradley's own formula for Shakespearean tragedy: "a marked one-sidedness... a fatal tendency to identify the whole being with one interest, object, passion, or habit of mind."

## Coriolanus and Timon as Mirror Cases

Qwarnström builds an armillary-sphere model of Jung's eight functions (rational: Thinking/Feeling; irrational/perceiving: Sensation/Intuition; each introverted or extraverted) and places the two protagonists at opposite poles of the same axis:

- **Coriolanus** — dominant **Introverted Thinking**, inferior **Extraverted Feeling**. Rigid commitment to abstract principle ("Rome" as an idea) over the actual felt life of the community; starves the plebeians (literally and emotionally) exactly as he starves his own capacity for felt relation. His fear of the crowd is displaced fear of his own unintegrated feeling function — "not master in his own house," in Jung's phrase for complex-possession.
- **Timon of Athens** — dominant **Extraverted Feeling**, inferior **Introverted Thinking**. Boundless, undiscriminating generosity — feasting all of Athens — with no capacity for the judgment (Introverted Thinking) that would let him see his flatterers' self-interest. Apemantus, the cynic who refuses Timon's food, personifies the discarded judging function Timon has silenced in himself.

Both plays open on the same background image — a hungry crowd, and the hero's relation to feeding it — read as **the superior function's relation to its own starved opposite**: Coriolanus withholds nourishment from the plebeians as he withholds felt relation from himself; Timon overfeeds Athens as he floods himself with unexamined trust. Both call the crowd "slaves" — read as [[Shadow|shadow]] projection, since (per Augustine, cited in the essay) true slavery is enslavement to one's own unexamined drives, not to an external master.

## Von Franz on the Inferior Function's Capture

A key clinical mechanic, drawn from Marie-Louise von Franz's *The Inferior Function* lectures: the superior function doesn't just neglect the inferior one — it actively tries to **seize and administer it on its own terms**, "like an eagle seizing a mouse." Von Franz's example: an introvert who "explains away" the pull toward a real relationship by intellectualizing it as "just the anima" — using the superior function (Thinking) to pre-empt ever actually encountering the inferior one (Feeling) as itself. The escape route she names is not conquest but a **"mixtum compositum"** — an uncomfortable transitional state, "neither fish, nor flesh, nor good red herring," in which the dominant function must genuinely cede ground rather than annex the opposite one. Coriolanus never reaches this state; his one-sidedness holds until it breaks him.

## Endings as Enantiodromia

Both plays end in the same movement the essay names directly: sustained one-sidedness becomes unsustainable and flips. Coriolanus, having banished Rome, turns against it, is nearly talked back from destroying it by his mother, and is killed for the reversal. Timon, having loved indiscriminately, curses "the gods, mankind, and life itself" and dies by his own hand — undiscriminating love flipping to undiscriminating hatred, the same function (Extraverted Feeling) now running in reverse. See [[Enantiodromia]] for the general principle.
