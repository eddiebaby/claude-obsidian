# Man and His Symbols

Jung's final completed project — written for a general audience at the request of John Freeman after a 1959 BBC interview. Jung died in 1961 before it was published. Edited by Aniela Jaffé. Five contributors (Jung, Henderson, von Franz, Jaffé, Jacobi) each wrote a chapter; von Franz also wrote the Conclusion.

The book's organizing thesis: symbols mediate between the unconscious and consciousness, and modern man's severance from his symbolic life is a spiritual emergency with concrete psychological consequences.

---

## Part 1 — "Approaching the Unconscious" (C.G. Jung, pp. 1–103)

### Sign vs. Symbol
The fundamental distinction: a **sign** is an agreed abbreviation for a known thing (the skull-and-crossbones = danger). A **symbol** is the best possible expression for something unknown — a living psychic reality that cannot be replaced by any clearer formulation. When the unknown becomes known, the symbol dies and becomes a sign.

Implications: symbols cannot be invented consciously. They arise spontaneously from the unconscious when the psyche needs to express something beyond the reach of current conceptual language. A symbol that is "explained away" loses its power.

### Dreams as the Royal Road
Jung rejects both Freud's reduction (dreams as disguised wish-fulfillment) and the idea that dreams are random neurological noise. Dreams are autonomous productions of the unconscious psyche that compensate the one-sided attitude of consciousness. They do not deceive; they simply speak a language of images rather than concepts.

"The general function of dreams is to try to restore our psychological balance by producing dream material that re-establishes, in a subtle way, the total psychic equilibrium."

Prospective function: dreams also anticipate future psychic developments — not as prophecy but as the unconscious processing of emerging possibilities.

### Case: 8-Year-Old Girl's Dreams
A physician asked Jung to analyze his 8-year-old daughter's dreams (given as a Christmas gift). The dreams were cosmogonic and collective in character — full of archetypal imagery (Cosmic Man, sacrifice, the tree of life, animals) that the girl could not have derived from experience. Jung recognized them as a premonition of her early death. She died of infectious disease the following year.

This case established Jung's conviction that children can access collective archetypal material before the ego is fully formed.

### The Unconscious Psyche
- Personal unconscious: forgotten experiences, repressed contents, subliminal perceptions.
- **Collective unconscious**: deeper layer, inborn, not acquired. Contains the archetypes — structural predispositions common to all humanity.
- Archetypes are not images but tendencies to form images. The image is the archetype made visible in a specific cultural context.

"Just as the human body represents a whole museum of organs, each with a long evolutionary history, so we should expect to find that the mind is organized in a similar way."

### Cryptomnesia
Jung's 1905 case: a patient produced a vision he had read 45 years earlier in a book (Nietzsche's source being a ship's log from 1686). The unconscious retained and reproduced material the conscious mind had forgotten completely. This is why amplification is necessary — to distinguish original creative product from cryptomnesic reproduction.

## Part 2 — "Ancient Myths and Modern Man" (Joseph L. Henderson, pp. 104–157)

### Hero Myth: Universal Structure
Henderson surveys the hero myth across cultures. Its core pattern: the hero separates from the collective, undergoes trials, confronts the monster (chaos/unconscious), achieves a prize (consciousness/culture), and either returns or dies transformed. The psychological reading: the hero is the ego's drive to separate from the unconscious matrix and establish individuality.

**Winnebago Cycles** (Paul Radin): four successive cycles — Trickster, Hare, Red Horn, Twin — showing the progressive differentiation of consciousness from undifferentiated nature.

### Trickster
The most archaic layer of the hero spectrum. The Trickster (Coyote, Loki, Brer Rabbit, infant Hermes) is:
- Pre-moral: neither good nor evil
- Capable of miraculous feats and absurd failures
- A violator of all taboos
- Gradually evolving toward human likeness across the cycle

The Trickster represents the collective shadow of the tribe — the repressed chaos that must be acknowledged to maintain psychic balance. As he evolves through the Winnebago cycles, he becomes more human, less merely instinctual.

### Hero-Dragon Battle
The hero slays the dragon or monster = the ego conquers the regressive pull of the unconscious. The dragon guards treasure (consciousness) and devours sacrifices (unconscious demands). The anima appears as the "damsel in distress" rescued from the devouring mother — the masculine soul needing to be liberated from the unconscious.

### Initiation vs. Hero Achievement
A critical distinction: 
- **Hero**: willful achievement, individual triumph over external obstacles
- **Initiate**: submission, passive endurance of ordeal, surrender of individual will

Most individuation work is initiation, not heroism. Submission → containment → liberation. The initiate reconciles opposing forces; the hero defeats them.

Sacred marriage (Logos + Eros): the mythological expression of the conjunction of opposites after initiation. *Beauty and the Beast* = the female initiation pattern.

### Transcendence Symbols
The snake (Aesculapius's rod, caduceus) and bird as double symbols of chthonic and aerial forces, the below and above. Modern rockets as contemporary transcendence symbols — the collective psyche expressing the same upward aspiration in technological form.

## Part 3 — "The Process of Individuation" (M.-L. von Franz, pp. 158–229)

### The Self as Nuclear Atom
Von Franz's diagram: the sphere of the psyche, with the ego as the bright field at the center-surface and the **Self** as simultaneously the nucleus at the center AND the whole sphere. The Self is both the innermost core and the totality that contains everything.

Jung estimated 80,000 dreams across his clinical work; the recurring pattern was individuation — a meandering but purposeful design.

### Naskapi Indians and Mista'peo
The Naskapi of Labrador live in isolated family groups; they have no collective religion. Their spiritual practice centers on following the instructions of the **Mista'peo** ("Great Man"), the inner companion encountered in dreams. Hunters who ignore Mista'peo's guidance find their dreams become obscure and unreliable. Those who obey find them increasingly clear and accurate. This is individuation in its most immediate, unmediated form: follow the Self or lose contact with it.

### Chuang-Tzu's Oak Tree
The "useless" tree that survives to enormous size because it has never been useful enough to be cut down. Its secret: it fulfills its own destiny rather than serving human purposes. The earth-altar that dreamed of itself — the surrender to unconscious purpose. The contrast with "useful" trees that are cut down in their prime.

### Individuation Onset
The call to individuation manifests as:
- A wound, a failure, a crisis
- "Deadly boredom" — the sense that nothing matters
- The ego projecting the obstruction outward (blaming others, circumstances, society)

Fairy tale pattern: the king fallen ill / the barren couple / the monster threatening the kingdom = the onset symbol of a psyche ready for transformation.

### Shadow Realization
The shadow is the same-sex figure in dreams who carries what the ego has rejected. The introvert's shadow is extraverted; the extravert's shadow is introverted. Two aspects: power drive and sexuality. Political projection: the shadow of a nation is often projected onto neighboring peoples.

**Dream of 48-year-old** (exemplary shadow case): Big house with cellar representing an Egyptian underworld (the unconscious in its deepest historical layer); an old school friend = the lost extraverted shadow side; three riderless horses = instinctive drives without ego guidance.

Ethical problem: the shadow must be recognized and lived with, not destroyed. "The discovery of the unconscious is one of the most far-reaching discoveries of recent times" — because without it, shadow gets projected.

### Anima: Four Stages
Von Franz maps the development of the anima archetype in four stages (see full treatment in [[Anima and Animus]]):
1. **Eve** — biological woman, instinct, the mother
2. **Helen** — romantic/aesthetic, the "femme fatale" of the collective
3. **Virgin Mary** — spiritualized Eros, love that elevates
4. **Sapientia** — transcendent wisdom (Mona Lisa as example)

Global equivalents: Kwan-Yin, Shakti/Parvati, Fatima.

Key clinical function: the anima as "radio tuned to the Self" — when functioning positively, she mediates the messages of the deeper unconscious to the ego.

**45-year-old psychotherapist's church dream**: priest in his own inner church; nun as introverted anima functioning as guide; book of 16 (4×4) ancient pictures. The dream saying: serve the totality, not a partial function.

### Animus: Four Stages
Parallel four stages in women's animus development:
1. **Physical power** — brute strength, Tarzan, the athlete
2. **Planned action and initiative** — the "man of action" (Shelley, Hemingway as romantic-hero types)
3. **The Word** — the bearer of the authoritative word (Lloyd George; the preacher)
4. **Meaning** — the conveyor of spiritual meaning (Gandhi; the sage)

Negative animus: death-demon in fairy tales; Bluebeard; drives family into illness; "You are hopeless. What's the use of trying?" Uses collective speech: "one always..." "one should..." "you ought to..."

### Self as Cosmic Man
The Self archetype's most universal form: the primordial, bisexual Cosmic Man from whom creation emerges and to whom it returns. Manifestations: P'an Ku (China), Gayomart (Persian), Adam Kadmon (Kabbalah), Purusha (India), Anthropos (Gnostic), Christ (Christian). Appears in dreams as a royal couple.

**She-bear-and-lions dream** (47-year-old): she-bear polishing an oval black stone → it becomes a naked woman; lioness + lions; royal couple. Stone becomes mirror through polishing = shadow work enabling consciousness. Royal couple = individuation reaching conscious realization.

### Active Imagination
Jung's discovery: deliberately contact the unconscious without a conscious goal. Unlike Eastern meditation (which seeks emptiness or program-driven states), active imagination enters the unconscious to engage what is actually there — without predetermination.

The Self speaks in active imagination. The Self appeared to one practitioner as a deer: "I am the 'connecting animal'... I connect people, animals, and even stones with one another if I enter them. I am your fate or the 'objective I.'"

See [[Active Imagination]] for full method.

### Stone and Crystal as Self
The stone — especially the rough, natural stone — is one of the oldest symbols of the Self. Properties:
- Permanent, unchanging ("just-so-ness")  
- Pre-human in age
- Contains latent potential (crystal within rough stone)

Cultural instances: the Ka'aba (Islam), Jacob's Bethel stone, Christ as cornerstone, alchemical lapis. Morienus's formula: "This thing is extracted from you: you are its mineral, and one can find it in you."

The crystal as union of matter and spirit — the most transparent, structured, and "spiritual" form of matter.

### Synchronicity and Individuation
Synchronistic events cluster around crucial phases of individuation. Example: during a critical session, a patient described a dream of a golden scarab; at that moment, a rose-chafer (scarab) tapped at the window. Jung opened the window and handed it to her: "Here is your scarab."

The synchronistic event serves as confirmation and amplification: the outer world "agrees" with what is happening in the inner world. See [[Synchronicity]].

### Mandala
The mandala appears spontaneously in dreams and active imagination when psychic order is disturbed and seeking to restore itself. See [[Mandala Symbolism]].

**62-year-old's mandala dream**: golden disk + round stone table. King Arthur's Round Table as cultural mandala: equality in the circle, the center empty for the Grail = the Self as the unobtainable perfection that organizes the quest.

### Dark Side of the Self
The Self has a destructive as well as healing potential. The Bath Badgerd fairy tale: Hatim Tai encounters a ruined city petrified by a centerstone/diamond; the center of things turns everything to stone if approached wrongly. The barber's mirror = the faculty of true self-reflection, without which one cannot approach the Self. The parrot = the spirit of mere imitation that cannot substitute for genuine individuation.

### Religious Symbols and the Unconscious
Von Franz argues that all religious traditions originate in individual revelations from the unconscious. Black Elk's great healing vision at age 9, enacted as ritual at age 16, healed the tribe. The Eskimo eagle festival originated in one hunter's initiatory encounter with the eagle spirit. Over time, the living revelation calcifies into fixed doctrine, and the theologians defend the outer form while losing the living contact with the unconscious source.

The unconscious tendency in the modern West: add a fourth element (feminine, dark, earthly) to the Christian Trinity. The Catholic dogma of Mary's Assumption (1950) as a psychologically significant move in this direction.

## Part 4 — "Symbolism in the Visual Arts" (Aniela Jaffé, pp. 230–271)

### Stone Symbolism
Unhewn stones as the oldest form of deity-dwelling. Jacob's dream at Bethel — the stone mediates between the human and the divine. Stone sanctuaries (Carnac, Stonehenge). Zen rock gardens. Modern sculptors (Aeschbacher, Rosati, Max Ernst) continuing the primitive impulse to let the stone speak rather than impose a form on it.

Max Ernst (1935): "No human hand can do that. So why not leave the spadework to the elements, and confine ourselves to scratching on them the runes of our own mystery?"

### Animal Symbolism in Prehistoric Art
Paleolithic cave paintings (60,000–10,000 BCE): Lascaux, Trois Frères, Tuc d'Audubert. The painted animal as the animal's "double" — the soul in material form. Sympathetic magic: killing the image kills the animal in potentia. Fertility rites: male and female bison at the moment of mating.

Most significant: the shaman-figure of the Trois Frères cave — a human figure with antlers, horse's head, bear's paws, playing a flute. "Lord of the Animals." The mask as identity transformation: the wearer becomes the animal; the archetypal image is put on.

Animal symbols in world religions (Hindu gods, Egyptian gods, Greek mythology, the four Evangelists, Christ as Lamb/Fish/Lion/Serpent) demonstrate that "the subhuman as well as the superhuman is felt to belong to the realm of the divine." Christ born in a stable among animals = the perfect symbolic statement of this relationship.

### Mandala in Architecture
Every building or city with a mandala ground plan is a projection of the archetypal image of wholeness onto the outer world. Examples:
- **Rome as urbs quadrata**: Romulus dug the *mundus* pit (offerings to earth gods), plowed a circle with bull and cow, divided the city with two main arteries. The city = a mandala. The mundus covered with a "soul stone" — removed on certain days, releasing the spirits of the dead.
- Medieval cities: surrounded by circular wall, two arteries leading to four gates, cathedral at intersection. Inspiration: the Heavenly Jerusalem (Revelation 21).
- Washington D.C. as modern example.
- Angkor Wat: square temple with circular enclosure at four corners.
- Paris: Place de l'Étoile as modern urban mandala.

"Every building, sacred or secular, that has a mandala ground plan is the projection of an archetypal image from within the human unconscious onto the outer world."

### Christian Architecture: Cross vs. Mandala
The Latin cross (elongated vertical) replaced the Greek cross (balanced) as the standard church ground plan. The shift tracked the "upward" movement of medieval Christianity — the body and earth becoming forces to overcome, the promise of fulfillment moving to the beyond. Gothic cathedrals as the apex: setting laws of gravity at defiance.

The Renaissance reversed this: man turned back to earth, nature, the body. Circle replaced Latin cross in many churches. Baptisteries retained the mandala plan (font in center). St. Peter's in Rome (Bramante and Michelangelo): rare example where the altar stands at the center.

The alchemists worked this same territory: the *quadratura circuli* (squaring of the circle) = the true mandala = symbol of the union of opposites (male/female figures in the corners of the squared circle).

### Circle in Modern Art
In modern painting, the circle has been dethroned from its dominant position. It appears scattered, asymmetrical, disconnected from the square — an expression of psychic dissociation:
- Delaunay's sun disks: asymmetrical, scattered circular planes
- Matisse's *Still Life with Vase of Nasturtiums*: green sphere on black beam, circle separated from rectangle — "the two figures that from the beginning of time have formed a whole are in this painting torn apart or incoherently related. Yet both are there and are touching each other."
- Kandinsky: loose assemblies of circles drifting like soap bubbles
- Paul Klee: *Limits of Understanding* — simple sphere or circle placed above a complex structure of ladders and lines. "A true symbol appears only when there is a need to express what thought cannot think."
- Piet Mondrian: squares, no center, no circle — the "master of harmonious compositions with squares"

The separation of circle and square in modern art = "the psychic state of 20th-century man: His soul has lost its roots and he is threatened by dissociation."

### UFOs as Projections of Wholeness
Jung's interpretation: the "flying saucer" phenomenon (appearing en masse after World War II) is not primarily a perceptual phenomenon but a **visionary rumor** — a projection of the unconscious collective psyche's need to heal the split between East and West, rational and irrational. The round flying object = the mandala = the symbol of wholeness = what the collective unconscious is urgently trying to restore.

### Modern Painting as Symbol
The artist is "the instrument and spokesman of the spirit of his age." Modern art (from Kandinsky onward) is taken as a symptom of the collective psyche's condition — not for aesthetic evaluation but as psychological evidence.

Key arc:
- **Kandinsky** (1866–1944): pioneer; *Concerning the Spiritual in Art* (1911); forms between "great abstraction" and "great realism"
- **Malevich** (1878–1935): black square on white ground (1913) — the first purely abstract picture; "In my desperate struggle to liberate art from the ballast of the world of objects, I took refuge in the form of the square"
- **Duchamp** (1887–1968): bottle rack as *objet trouvé* — Bazaine: "torn from its utilitarian context and washed up on the beach, it has been invested with the lonely dignity of the derelict"
- **Schwitters** (1887–1948): "cathedral built for things" — ordinary rubbish assembled with such seriousness it became art; 10 years, three stories of house demolished

"The secret soul of things" (section heading): Kandinsky: "Everything that is *dead* quivers... Everything has a secret soul, which is silent more often than it speaks." The artists were projecting their psyche's unconscious contents onto matter — recovering the alchemical tradition of spirit in matter (Mercurius).

**Giorgio de Chirico** (1888–1978): *pittura metafisica*; self-portrait motto: "Et quid amabo nisi quod aenigma est" ("And what am I to love if not the enigma?"); two aspects of every object (common + metaphysical); the dreadful void = "God is dead" (Nietzsche). Faceless *manichino* (puppet) = man deprived of soul = "lonely wanderer who strives to discover the 'truth' about metaphysics."

**Marc Chagall** (1887–1985): counterpart to Chirico's despair; Eastern Jewish Hassidism; "has always kept one foot on the earth that had nourished him"; warm symbolism; never lost the human bond.

**Surrealism** (Breton, Dalí): sought to reconcile consciousness and unconscious through free association and automatic writing. Breton: "thought's dictation, independent of any aesthetic or moral preoccupation." Jaffé's critique: left to itself, without the human response of consciousness, the unconscious can destroy its own gifts. Consciousness alone can determine which unconscious images are significant for life *now*.

**Max Ernst's frottages**: series of graphite rubbings from tiled floors → *Histoire Naturelle* (1925). Ernst placed a ring or circle over or behind the frottages — the unconscious drive to oppose chaotic imagery with the symbol of psychic wholeness. "Psychic wholeness rules nature, itself meaningful and giving meaning."

**Marino Marini** (1901–1966): horse and rider series; over 12 years, the rider's posture shifts from hope to frozen terror. Marini: "I believe that we are approaching the end of the world. In every figure, I strove to express a deepening fear and despair. I am attempting to symbolize the last stage of a dying myth, the myth of the individual, victorious hero, of the humanist's man of virtue."

The victorious hero = symbol of individual consciousness. His defeat = the submergence of the individual in the mass.

**Healing turn**: mid-century, modern art begins to move toward the union of opposites. Alfred Manessier: "We have to reconquer the weight of lost reality... a new soul, in the measure of man." The Church becomes patron of modern art (Matisse chapel at Vence; Assy church; Audincourt). Pierre Soulages: behind cataract of huge black rafters, a clear, pure blue or radiant yellow. "Light is dawning behind darkness."

## Part 5 — "Symbols in an Individual Analysis" (Jolande Jacobi, pp. 272–301)

A complete nine-month analysis of "Henry" — a 25-year-old Swiss engineer, introverted, mother-bound, rationalist. 35 sessions, 50 dreams. Demonstrates Jungian method in action for a young person on the threshold of manhood (individuation in the first half of life).

See [[Jolande-Jacobi]] for background on the author. Key dreams and their interpretations:

**Initial dream**: Excursion to Zinalrothorn; group activity he cannot fully join; wise old woman shows the way; cog-wheel railway (right side = conscious, left = unconscious); bloated dead men in blue suits in cars = sterile intellectual formulas; rain/cloudburst = sacred marriage of heaven and earth; "Be fruitful!" The voice declaring the men dead = the Self speaking through a dream.

**Fourth dream**: Military service/long-distance race; always "déjà vu"; a doe (symbol of shy, innocent femininity) glimpsed and lost; three hybrid animals (half pig, half dog, kangaroo legs; faceless) = "confused mass of instincts," the alchemical *prima materia* from which individual ego can develop.

**The Saint and Prostitute dream**: Mountain road; prostitute in cave (Venus of Willendorf association; fertility goddess; touching buttocks = fertility rite); prostitute shifts to saint in crimson coat = dramatic reversal (enantiodromia); second cave = healing place of transformation. The coat as persona (Jung's term): the saint's coat combines colors associated with his fiancée and another girl, giving it "eroticized spirituality." Henry driven out of the cave = must succeed in outer life before spiritual sphere.

**Oracle dream**: South America journey; Chinese gatekeepers block passage; trapper (shadow) takes Henry's place; oracle consulted; Henry is saved. Leads to I Ching consultation: hexagram MENG = "Youthful Folly." The text directly applicable to Henry's condition: "For youthful folly, it is the most hopeless thing to entangle itself in empty imaginings." A synchronistic confirmation.

**Scarab beetles dream**: Black beetles from a hole in drawing table; Henry uses magic to drive them back; fire destroys their breeding ground. Henry's self-interpretation: "dark qualities awakened by analysis; fire of the Ark of the Covenant." The scarab: Egyptian solar symbol (golden = sun; black = devilish). Fire = transformation; phoenix.

**"Unlived life" / Old Man dying dream**: 40 people present (totality); the old man (Henry's "ruling principle") is dying; his daughter (anima as mediating principle) sends Henry to find the answer via fortune-telling cards; the nine of spades = "moral chains." Conclusion: "The unlived life is an illness of which one can die." The message: one needs more than reason as compass.

**Final dream (50th)**: Four-part mandala (Evening/Night/Noon/Morning); four friends + girl (anima) + President of France (undeveloped feeling function, abandons himself to instinct, urination = inexhaustible psychic libido) + old spinster with earth-colored child + the Negro (gorgeous, naked = chthonic shadow, real and true). Henry is first to give the Negro a gift: a box of matches (controlled fire = warmth, love, feeling, passion). All six male persons join in a communal feast. This is the masculine totality rounded.

Henry married nine months after the analysis began. The first half of individuation completed.

Jacobi's summary: "Henry's case reveals an accelerated maturation to an independent and responsible manliness. It represents an initiation into the reality of outer life... completion of the first half of the individuation process. The second half — which is the establishment of a right relationship between the ego and the Self — still lies ahead of Henry, in the second half of his life."

## Conclusion — "Science and the Unconscious" (M.-L. von Franz, pp. 304–310)

### Archetypes in All Fields
Archetypes appear as "dynamic background activity" in literature, art, history, anthropology, biology, physics. Not by reducing these fields to archetypal foundations, but by recognizing the archetypal patterns as underlay.

### Synchronicity and Biology
Wolfgang Pauli: evolution by purely random mutation would take much longer than the known age of the planet. Synchronicity — meaningful acausal events — may explain how "meaningful" biological adaptations occur in less time than pure chance would require. Darwin and Wallace's simultaneous independent discovery of evolution (1844/1858) = synchronistic event = "creatio continua" with the archetype as agent.

### Physics and the Unconscious
The deepest connection von Franz identifies: Jung (working closely with Pauli) discovered that analytical psychology was forced by its own investigations to create concepts strikingly similar to those physicists created when confronted with microphysical phenomena.

- Scientific concepts (atom, energy, field, particle) originated as intuitive, semi-mythological, archetypal ideas in Greek philosophy. They did not express "objective" facts so much as innate mental tendencies.
- **Heisenberg's uncertainty**: "When examining nature and the universe, instead of looking for and finding objective qualities, 'man encounters himself.'"
- **Bohr's complementarity**: light = particle AND wave; conscious/unconscious = complementary pair. Each new content from the unconscious is altered by being partially integrated into the conscious mind; dream interpretation enlarges consciousness and has immediate repercussion on the unconscious.
- Archetypes = Pauli's "primary possibilities" of psychic reactions. There are only "tendencies," not laws.
- **Natural numbers**: not consciously invented but spontaneous, autonomous products of the unconscious — archetypal symbols. They connect the sphere of matter to the sphere of psyche.
- **Unus mundus**: Jung's term for the one world in which matter and psyche are not yet discriminated. The archetype shows a "psychoid" (not purely psychic but almost material) aspect in synchronistic events — events where inner and outer facts are meaningfully arranged without causal connection.
- Mathematics as the meeting point: Gauss found a rule "by the Grace of God, so to speak. The riddle solved itself as lightning strikes." Poincaré watched his representations "colliding" until some "found a more stable connection." Van der Waerden: "the unconscious is not only able to associate and combine, but even to judge."
- Maria Mayer (Nobel Prize physics 1963): atomic nucleus as concentric shells; "magic numbers" (20, 28, 50, 82, 126) — the archetype of sphere and number manifest in physics.

### Closing Statement
Von Franz ends with a practical appeal: "Jung's ideas can serve in this way to find and interpret new facts in many fields of science (and also of everyday life), simultaneously leading the individual to a more balanced, more ethical, and wider conscious outlook. If the reader should feel stimulated to work further on the investigation and assimilation of the unconscious — which always begins by working on oneself — the purpose of this introductory book would be fulfilled."
