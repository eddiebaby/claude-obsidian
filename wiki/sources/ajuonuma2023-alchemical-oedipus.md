# The Alchemical Oedipus: Re-Visioning the Myth

**Author**: [[Reginald Ajuonuma]] (London, UK)
**Publication**: Journal of Analytical Psychology, 2023, 68(5), 807-827
**Keywords**: alchemy, analytical psychology, Laius, myth, Oedipal, psychoanalysis

## One-line summary

A Jungian amplification of the full Oedipus cycle — not just Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex* but Laius's backstory and *Oedipus at Colonus* — using the alchemical "king's renewal by his son" parable (specifically the *Visio Arislei*) as the interpretive template; the payoff is a reading of Oedipus as **Laius's redeemer** and as an embodiment of the Jungian **Self**, restoring the myth's generative, not merely pathological, meaning.

---

## The Problem the Paper Addresses

Freud built psychoanalysis on Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex*, and Jung — despite opening *Symbols of Transformation* by remarking on the myth's enduring power ("we realize with astonishment that Oedipus is still alive for us," 1911-1912/1952a, para. 1) — never produced a developed Jungian counter-reading. Jung's own engagement with Oedipus in CW 5 is cursory; he preferred other mythological expressions of the incest motif (the hero's rebirth from the symbolic mother). Post-Jungian [[James Hillman|James Hillman]] argues Jung reads Oedipus as "primarily a moral problem not a sexual one" (Astor 1998, p.705, as cited by Ajuonuma).

The result: the Oedipus myth's psychologically constructive elements have been ceded to reductive Freudian interpretation almost by default. Ajuonuma sets out to close that gap.

**Two moves make this possible:**
1. **Reinsert Laius.** Sophocles wrote Laius almost entirely out of the extant plays; classicist Silke-Maria Weineck (2010) notes "Ever since Sophocles banned him from the stage, Laius has remained the one we know by hearsay, the one we never see" (p.132). Psychoanalyst J.M. Ross (1982) argues the Oedipus myth is *also* the story of Laius, "the forgotten story." Without Laius's prehistory, Oedipus's story "might remain mysterious and apparently unmotivated" (Ross 1982, p.175).
2. **Reinsert the aftermath.** Bring *Oedipus at Colonus* — Oedipus's death and apotheosis at Athens — back into the frame alongside *Oedipus Rex*.

With the full arc restored (Laius's sterility and crime against Chrysippus → the crossroads killing → Oedipus's blinding and exile → Colonus and death), the myth reads as a story of **paternal rebirth**, not merely filial pathology.

## Laius's Backstory: The "Forgotten King"

- Ancient sources (the epic *Oedipodeia*, per classicist Karl Kerényi) differ sharply from Sophocles: Oedipus does not blind himself, and stays king after Jocasta's suicide. The origin of the family curse — omitted from Sophocles — is that Laius abducted and raped **Chrysippus**, son of King Pelops, who then killed himself; Laius and his descendants were cursed for it.
- **George Devereux (1953)** coins the **"complementary Oedipus complex"** — a parent's own filicidal fantasies and behaviours that *constellate* the child's Oedipus complex from the other direction. Ross (1982) calls the same pattern the **"Laius complex."** See [[Complementary Oedipus Complex]].
- Devereux: it is not Laius's attempted murder of the infant Oedipus that damns his reputation in antiquity but his "hybris" toward Chrysippus — "a violator of good manners" (1953, p.135).
- Oedipus, too, is prone to "fits of furious anger" (Kerényi 1959, p.93): he bites Laius's corpse at the crossroads (Aeschylus); he later curses his own two sons with death. Devereux attributes Oedipus's impulsiveness to inheritance from Laius's character; Weineck (2010) diagnoses a broader post-Freud tendency to "overidentify with the filial at the expense of the paternal." Ross's counter: Oedipus's anger is part of his nature which he nonetheless **transcends** — he becomes "the son who, unjustly victimized, blinded and maimed though he may be, openly betrayed by his father and his sons, will still find solace in his care for others and an informed redemption in old age and death" (Ross 1982, p.181).

## The Alchemical Template: The King's Renewal

Jung discusses the theme of a corrupt, sick, or sterile king renewed by his son across *Psychology and Alchemy* (1944), *Mysterium Coniunctionis* (1955-1956), and — outside alchemy proper — in *Answer to Job* (1952), where Yahweh is redeemed by his son Christ. See [[Kings Renewal Parable]] for the full treatment.

Key mechanics Ajuonuma draws on:
- The king's sickness (sterility, senescence, corruption) reflects the loss of "the dark, chthonic aspect of nature" (Jung 1955-1956, para. 427) — one-sided over-development of consciousness at the expense of the shadow. Jung: **"The more distinctly an idea emerges and the more consciousness gains in clarity, the more monarchic becomes its content, to which everything contradictory has to submit.... For these reasons, too, the king constantly needs the renewal that begins with a descent into his own darkness, an immersion in his own depths, and with a reminder that he is related by blood to his adversary."** (1955-1956, para. 471)
- Renewal proceeds via "dissolution and decomposition, extinction of his light in the darkness, incineration in the fire, and renewal out of the chaos" (1955-1956, para. 486) — the classic [[Nigredo]] sequence, associated with the planet Saturn as "dark, cold *maleficus*."
- Illustrated in the 1582 manuscript *Splendor Solis* ("Drowning King" — the crowned son rescuing the drowning old king), and named by the alchemists "the philosophical tree," whose goal is the *lapis* — which they identified with Christ. Dorn: the root of the philosophical tree is "ascribed to Saturn" — the corrupt old king is the necessary soil for the splendour of his son.
- The reborn king is the ***puellus regius*** (kingly boy), read as "an allegory of Christ" — see [[filius-regius]].

Ajuonuma's specific amplificatory tool is the ***Visio Arislei*** (Vision of Arisleus), a king's-renewal parable contained in the 1593 compendium *Artis Auriferae*. See [[Visio Arislei]] for the full cast and plot (Arisleus, [[rex-marinus|Rex Marinus]], Gabricus, Beya, Harforetus).

## The Mapping: Laius/Oedipus onto Rex Marinus/Gabricus-Beya

| Vision of Arisleus | Oedipus myth |
|---|---|
| Rex Marinus, sterile ("only like mates with like") | Laius — childless with Jocasta for years; his love for Chrysippus is read as the root of the sterility |
| Arisleus advises the mating of Gabricus and Beya | Delphic Apollo — sends Laius to the crossroads, sends Oedipus fleeing the same oracle |
| Gabricus dissolved in Beya's womb during intercourse | Laius killed by Oedipus at the crossroads — "the quarrelling that precedes their union" (Jung 1955-1956, para. 415); sex and power are "coexistent" drives (Jung 1946, para. 360) |
| Rex Marinus banishes Arisleus + Beya to the undersea hothouse (80 days, "every kind of terror") | Creon banishes Oedipus (with Antigone) from Thebes |
| Harforetus (hermaphrodite disciple of Pythagoras) feeds them the fruit of the tree of life, ending the torment | The Eumenides (the Furies in their benevolent aspect) receive Oedipus at Colonus — an [[Enantiodromia|enantiodromia]] from persecuting to redemptive |
| Beya births her brother, renewing Rex Marinus's kingdom; the son (*filius regius*) is unlike either parent | Oedipus's death is a blessing for Athens (Theseus) and a curse for Thebes (Polynices, Creon) — Vernant's "Oedipus is double" |

Because in alchemy "male" and "female" are ultimately both "nothing other than **Mercurius duplex**" (Jung 1955-1956, para. 416), and because *prima materia* and *lapis* — Mercurius's two forms — share names, Ajuonuma argues Laius and Oedipus are **consubstantial**: Laius as *prima materia*, Oedipus as *lapis*. Supporting textual detail: Jocasta tells Oedipus that Laius was "about your figure" (Sophocles, line 743); both men share a name connoting difficulty walking (Oedipus = "swell-foot," Laius = "left-sided" — Lévi-Strauss's structural reading of chthonic archaism); both display violence, "a sure sign of [the alchemical king's] morally defective state" (Jung 1955-1956, para. 365).

The androgyny extends further: some myth variants say Oedipus removes Laius's sword and belt after killing him — in ancient Greece "the undoing of a woman's belt is a preliminary to intercourse" (Devereux 1953, p.134) — a symbolic castration/feminization of the father that lets Ajuonuma also identify **Laius with Beya** (the renewing "womb") and Oedipus with Gabricus. Gabricus's death is "punishment for the incestuous ***coniunctio oppositorum*** [union of opposites]" (Jung 1944, para. 436) with his sister; Oedipus's self-blinding is read as the parallel consequence of his incest "in the womb of his father-mother." See [[coniunctio-oppositorum]].

## Pharmakon: Oedipus's Double Nature

Both the alchemical *filius regius* and Oedipus are **pharmakon** — Derrida's term for the Greek word meaning both remedy and poison (and, via *pharmakos*, "scapegoat"). Oedipus purifies Thebes as a *pharmakos* expelled by Creon; the *filius regius* is likewise expelled for its residual "nigredic" blackness. Classicist Helene Peet Foley calls this Oedipus's "extraordinary capacity to save and destroy." Derrida: the *pharmakon* "would be a substance ... if we didn't have eventually to come to recognize it as antisubstance itself" — which Ajuonuma reads against the alchemists' paradox of the lapis as "the stone that is no stone" (see [[lapis-exilis]]). Oedipus's death — the god's cry "Oedipus! Oedipus!... It is time: you stay too long" — marks him as **other-than-man**; in this final intimacy with the divine he becomes the *homo altus* (lofty man), another synonym for the lapis. See [[Pharmakon]].

## Conclusion: Oedipus as the Jungian Self

Equating Oedipus with the lapis identifies him with the **Self**. Theseus, witnessing Oedipus's apotheosis at Colonus, salutes both "heaven and earth" — Jung's Self as "a combination of opposites" (1958, para. 640). Against readings that see Oedipus as never achieving true consciousness (Inez Martinez 2012: he never learns "to father his children differently than he had been fathered"), Ajuonuma argues this view is anchored in a mundane conception of the Self — the Self "cannot be equated either with collective morality or with natural instinct, but must be conceived as a determining factor whose nature is individual and unique" (Jung 1942/1954, para. 394). Oedipus's suffering — "the dismembering of knowing" (Susan Rowland) — opens him to "what he has not known" (Kane 1975), redeeming Laius's hybris and earning him alchemy's sobriquet "**Saviour of all imperfect bodies and men**" (Waite 1678/1893, p.98).

The paper's closing move: Oedipus's achievement is only fully legible once Laius — devoid of insight, identified here with the **prima materia** — is restored as the necessary starting matter of his son's transformation.
