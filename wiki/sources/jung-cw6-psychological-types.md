## Overview

*Psychological Types* is Jung's most systematic work on individual psychological differences. Published in 1921, it represents his attempt to explain the sources of theoretical disagreement in psychology — particularly his split with Freud and Adler — by showing that different thinkers have different *orientations*. The introvert-extravert distinction and the four psychological functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition) are the twin axes of the typology.

The file contains Chapter X in full, which is the section most readers know as the "type descriptions" — the practical systematization after nine chapters of historical and philosophical survey. The text is the 1923 Baynes translation, not the revised Hull translation in the Collected Works.

For the [[Individuation]] process, typology matters because the work of individuation requires confronting one's inferior function and undeveloped attitude — the parts of oneself that have been left unconscious.

## Core Framework

### Two Attitude Types

The foundational axis is the *direction of libido movement* relative to the object:

| Attitude | Orientation | Biological analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Extravert** | Libido flows toward the object; outer world is determining | High fertility / broad propagation |
| **Introvert** | Libido withdraws from the object; subjective factor is determining | Individual self-protection / depth over breadth |

Key claim (p. 414): The distribution of types is not class-based or culturally learned — "two children of the same mother may at a very early age exhibit opposite types." The attitude is constitutional, with a biological precursor.

The extravert's normality depends on fitting objective conditions; his pathology is neglect of the subjective factor (the body, the inner life). The introvert's normality depends on the *Self* (not merely the *ego*) opposing the object; his pathology is ego-inflation that triggers compensatory enslavement to the very object he is trying to repudiate — **Psychoasthenia** is his typical neurosis.

### Four Psychological Functions

Jung divides psychic activity into two pairs:

**Rational (Judging) Functions** — those that evaluate:
- **Thinking**: orients by logical inference; asks "Is it true/false?"
- **Feeling**: orients by value; asks "Is it pleasant/unpleasant, acceptable/unacceptable?"

**Irrational (Perceiving) Functions** — those that simply register:
- **Sensation**: registers what *is*, via sense perception; the function of concrete reality
- **Intuition**: registers what *might be*, via unconscious perception; transmits images of possibilities and relations

Each function can operate in either attitude, yielding eight types (extraverted thinking, introverted thinking, etc.).

### Superior and Inferior Functions

Every person develops one function to primary ("superior") status — conscious, differentiated, under voluntary control. The function opposite in kind is correspondingly repressed and remains **inferior**: archaic, autonomous, contaminated by unconscious contents, prone to erupting as compulsive behavior, moods, or projections.

Feeling cannot serve as the auxiliary to Thinking (they compete for the same evaluative role). The auxiliary function must be of the *opposite kind* (rational / irrational) but not of the *opposite aim*. Valid pairings:

- Thinking + Sensation (practical intellect)
- Thinking + Intuition (speculative intellect)
- Feeling + Sensation
- Feeling + Intuition

The inferior function is the entry point for unconscious contents, and in dreams and fantasies appears in an "archaic, animal state."

### Individuation and Type

A forced reversal of type produces neurosis and physiological exhaustion. Therapeutic development should proceed *via the auxiliary function* (less violating to the conscious standpoint), not by attempting to directly develop the inferior function from the unconscious. Direct attack on the inferior function risks making the patient wholly dependent on the analyst (compulsive transference).

## The Eight Types (Chapter X summary)

### Extraverted Types

**1. Extraverted Thinking Type**: Governed by the object; thinking orients by objective facts and formula. When extreme, suppresses feeling into the unconscious — the repressed feeling erupts as infantile moralizing, secret sentimentality, or resentment.

**2. Extraverted Feeling Type**: Predominantly feminine psychology; feeling adapts to objective social value. Culturally creative (arts, sociability, fashion). When extreme, loses genuine warmth, becomes hysterical or merely performative.

**3. Extraverted Sensation Type**: Lives in concrete, sensuous actuality; reality = what is sensuously present. "Fulness of actual living" is his creed. The judging (rational) functions are subordinated and archaic. Typical hedonism with underlying superstitious morality.

**4. Extraverted Intuitive Type**: Restlessly moves from possibility to possibility; abandons situations the moment the intuitive potential is exhausted. Entrepreneur, prophet, catalyst — but no staying power for the developed project.

### Introverted Types

**5. Introverted Thinking Type**: Governed by subjective ideas; thinking moves inward toward primordial images. When severed from objective experience, the ideas become mythological and untrue for the present situation — the thinker is misunderstood as eccentric or mad.

**6. Introverted Feeling Type**: "Still waters run deep" — predominantly feminine; feeling runs deep but is not expressed. Hides behind a blank or childlike exterior. Has an unconscious influence on the environment it cannot account for. When extreme, appears cold, inaccessible.

**7. Introverted Sensation Type**: Orients by subjective intensity of sensory excitation; the inner psychic response, not the outer object, is primary. Appears aesthetically refined but practically ungrounded. Unconscious side is extraverted intuition.

**8. Introverted Intuitive Type**: The mystical dreamer, the seer, the fantastical crank, or the artist. Rich inner life of archetypal visions; poor outer adaptation. The "voice crying in the wilderness" — real experience, but incommunicable. Unconscious side is extraverted sensation, which can erupt as compulsive hypochondria or sensory obsession.

## Transcendent Function Note

The text mentions the transcendent function only obliquely (one reference to theosophy as a "transcendental" mode of thinking). The full treatment of the transcendent function as Jung's technical term for the bridge between conscious and unconscious is in [[jung-cw8-structure-dynamics-psyche]] (¶131–193, essay "The Transcendent Function") and is the basis for [[Active Imagination]].
