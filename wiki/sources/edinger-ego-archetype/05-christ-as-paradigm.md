# Christ as Paradigm of the Individuation Process
*Ego and Archetype, Part II, pp. 131–156*

## Central Theme
Edinger reads the Christ myth not as theology but as a symbolic drama that maps the individuation process in its essential structure. The life of Christ — incarnation, ministry, passion, death, resurrection, and the sending of the Paraclete — enacts in mythological form the archetypal sequence every individual must undergo in the relation between ego and Self. Christ is the preeminent Western symbol of the individuating Self made conscious, and his ethical teaching describes the psychological demands that the Self places on the ego once it has been encountered. The chapter moves from the acceptance of separateness (incarnation as the ego's acceptance of its finitude) through the ethical demands of Selfhood to the theological formulation of man as *imago Dei* — the image of God — which Edinger translates as the ego's unique capacity to make the Self conscious.

## Key Ideas
- **The Acceptance of Separateness** is the first demand of individuation: the ego must relinquish its unconscious identification with the transpersonal source (the Self, the unconscious, the pleroma) and accept the pain of being a separate, finite, mortal individual. Christ's incarnation is the mythological statement of this acceptance.
- The **Virgin Birth** as symbol: the ego that is to undergo individuation is not generated purely from personal, human parents but has a transpersonal dimension — it is "sired" by the unconscious (the divine father) and carried by personal human life (the human mother). This is the structural situation of any person called to conscious development.
- **The Ethical Teaching** of Christ is psychologically the demand of the Self on the ego. "Love your enemy," "turn the other cheek," and especially "take up your cross" are not moral imperatives issued from without but descriptions of what is required when the ego consciously cooperates with the individuation process — specifically the integration of the shadow and the acceptance of suffering as meaningful.
- **The Self-Oriented Ego** is Edinger's term for the ego that has established a conscious, living relation to the Self — oriented toward it as a compass toward north, without being absorbed by it. This is the ego Christ exemplifies: fully human, fully responsive to the transpersonal, capable of saying both "Not my will but Thine" and "Why hast thou forsaken me."
- The **Crucifixion** as psychological image: the ego stretched between opposites — human and divine, love and wrath, acceptance and rebellion — and holding the tension. The cross is the intersection of the vertical (spirit) and horizontal (matter/time) axes of existence.
- **Man as the Image of God** (*imago Dei*): Edinger follows Jung in arguing that man is the being through whom God (the Self, the totality of the psyche) becomes conscious. The individuation process is thus God's project as much as man's; the ego's consciousness is what the Self requires to realize itself.
- The **Paraclete** (Holy Spirit sent after Christ's departure) is psychologically the inner guide that emerges once the ego withdraws its projection from a concrete outer figure — the analyst, the teacher, the church — and takes up the work of inner development. "It is to your advantage that I go away" (John 16:7) means the personal form must be sacrificed to release the universal spirit.
- Christ's **"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me"** is interpreted as the ego's darkest moment of abandonment by the Self — the *nigredo* of the individuation process — which paradoxically is the precondition of transformation.

## Psychological Significance
This chapter addresses the **midpoint of the individuation process**: the ego has encountered the Self (Chapter 3) and has committed to the symbolic life (Chapter 4). Now it confronts the full ethical and existential demands of that commitment. The psychological task is to accept suffering as meaningful — not masochistically, but as the necessary friction of genuine development. The Gethsemane prayer ("Not my will but Thine") is the model attitude: full engagement of the ego's will, followed by conscious surrender to the larger pattern.

## Key Images / Symbols
- **The Cross** — intersection of the vertical (spirit/transcendence) and horizontal (time/matter) axes; the ego crucified between opposites, holding the tension.
- **The Virgin Birth** — transpersonal origin of the individuating ego; the Self as the "divine father" of conscious development.
- **Gethsemane** — the moment of voluntary acceptance; the ego's willingness to suffer what the Self requires.
- **The Paraclete / Holy Ghost** — the inner spirit that replaces the outer projection once the personal form of the symbol is relinquished.
- **The Empty Tomb** — not physical resurrection but the psychological reality that meaning (the Self) survives the death of any particular form through which it was expressed.

## Subsection Notes

**The Acceptance of Separateness.** Edinger opens by noting that incarnation — the descent of the divine into finite human form — is a symbolic statement about what the ego must endure: the painful awareness of being cut off from the wholeness it once unconsciously enjoyed. This is not a fall but a necessary precondition of consciousness.

**The Ethical Teaching.** The demands of Christ's ethics are not achievable by willpower alone; they describe the natural consequence of a genuine relation to the Self. The person who has experienced the Self as a living reality naturally extends its perspective — which sees and values everything — into his ethical relations. The "love of enemies" becomes possible when the shadow has been partially integrated.

**The Self-Oriented Ego.** Edinger is careful to distinguish this from ego-dissolution or mystical merger. The self-oriented ego remains a distinct center of consciousness; it is not swallowed by the Self but consciously serves it. The model is the responsible servant, not the ecstatic devotee.

**Man as the Image of God.** The *imago Dei* doctrine receives a psychological reading: humanity is the location where the unconscious totality achieves reflective self-knowledge. This gives the ego's existence a cosmic justification: its suffering and striving are not merely personal but serve the larger process of God becoming conscious in the world.
