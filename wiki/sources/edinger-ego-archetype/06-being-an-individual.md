# Being an Individual
*Ego and Archetype, Part II, pp. 157–178*

## Central Theme
What does it mean to be a genuine individual — not merely a member of a collective, not a fragment of an archetype, but a unique, irreplaceable center of consciousness? Edinger approaches this question through three interrelated concepts: the *a priori* existence of the ego (the idea that individuality is not an accident but has a transpersonal, pre-given ground), the Gnostic and Neoplatonic idea of the Monad and the Monogenes (the "only-begotten" or unique one), and the paradox of Unity and Multiplicity. The chapter argues that the individuation process culminates not in the absorption of the ego into the Self but in the realization of a particular, concrete ego whose uniqueness is itself the contribution of the individual to the wholeness of being.

## Key Ideas
- The **a priori existence of the ego** means that individual identity is not merely the product of heredity, environment, and chance but has a transpersonal foundation — a pattern laid down in the psyche before birth, analogous to the Platonic Idea or the alchemical *prima materia*. To discover one's individuality is to discover this pre-given pattern.
- The **Monad** (from Greek philosophy, particularly Leibniz via the Neoplatonists) is the ultimate indivisible unit — the Self in its absolute, unrelated aspect. Each individual psyche is a monad in this sense: a complete world unto itself, mirroring the whole but from an utterly unique angle.
- The **Monogenes** — "the only-begotten one" — is the Gnostic and early Christian term for Christ as the unique divine son. Edinger uses it to denote the aspect of the Self that is singular and non-interchangeable. Every individual who undertakes individuation is, in some sense, a Monogenes: the particular instantiation of the divine pattern that only he or she can realize.
- **Individuation does not dissolve individuality**: a common misreading holds that the goal is merger with the collective unconscious or the loss of ego. Edinger insists the opposite is true. The more deeply one individuates, the more distinctly one's particular character is constellated. The Self is not a homogenizing force but the ground of radical uniqueness.
- **Unity and Multiplicity as a paradox of the Self**: The Self is simultaneously the One (the wholeness that contains all opposites) and the many (manifested only through the multiplicity of individual egos). This paradox is expressed theologically in Trinitarian doctrine, philosophically in Neoplatonic emanationism, and psychologically in the relation between the collective unconscious (One) and individual psyches (Many).
- The **name written in heaven** (from Revelation and the Philosophical Stone text Edinger will revisit in Chapter 10) signifies the metaphysical uniqueness of the individual: one's personal identity has a transpersonal, eternally valid foundation. This is the experiential answer to alienation and unworthiness.
- The experience of the Self gives the ego an undeniable sense of the **right to exist** — not earned by achievement or conformity, but given as part of the original structure of being. This is the psychological cure for the existential shame or ontological insecurity that underlies many neurotic conditions.
- The chapter engages Leibniz's monadology: each monad perceives the whole universe but from its own irreplaceable vantage point. There is no communication between monads except through God (the Self). The parallel to Jung's model of the psyche is explicit.

## Psychological Significance
This chapter addresses the **late phase of individuation** — the phase in which the question is no longer "How do I become more conscious?" but "What is the meaning of my specific, irreplaceable existence?" The psychological task is the acceptance of one's own *thisness* — the particular configuration of shadow, gifts, wounds, and calling that constitutes this person and no other. The opposite of this acceptance is conformism (dissolving into collective expectations) or grandiosity (claiming a uniqueness borrowed from the archetype rather than earned through one's own development).

## Key Images / Symbols
- **The Monad** — the indivisible unit; image of the Self as both whole and utterly singular.
- **The Monogenes** — the Gnostic only-begotten; symbol of the irreplaceable uniqueness of each individuating ego.
- **Name written in heaven / Book of Life** — transpersonal registration of individual identity; the metaphysical ground of the right to exist.
- **The Bollingen Stone inscription** (quoted from Jung): "I am an orphan, alone; nevertheless I am found everywhere. I am one, but opposed to myself..." — the paradoxical self-description of the Philosophers' Stone/Self as simultaneously singular and universal.
- **The seed** — the *a priori* pattern of individual development, latent from the beginning, requiring experience to actualize.

## Subsection Notes

**The A Priori Existence of the Ego.** Edinger argues that each ego comes into the world with a predisposed pattern — not in the theological sense of predestination but in the psychological sense of an inherent developmental trajectory. Dreams and symptoms often express this pattern before consciousness has recognized it. The analyst's task is partly to help the patient discover the pattern that was there from the beginning.

**The Monad and the Monogenes.** Moving through Leibniz, the Neoplatonists, and Gnostic texts, Edinger maps the philosophical traditions that have tried to account for the relation between the One and the many. The Monogenes — the uniquely begotten — is the symbol of how the absolute particularizes itself without losing its unity. Psychologically this describes how the Self appears in the form of a specific, irreplaceable individual.

**Unity and Multiplicity.** The paradox is not resolvable by logic. The Self is simultaneously the ground of all individuals (the One) and exhausted by none of them (the Many still to come). Individual consciousness is necessary to the completeness of the whole: if any particular individual fails to individuate, something of the Self's self-knowledge fails to be realized. This gives the work of individuation a genuine urgency beyond personal welfare.
