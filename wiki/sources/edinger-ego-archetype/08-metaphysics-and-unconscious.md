# Metaphysics and the Unconscious
*Ego and Archetype, Part III, pp. 197–224*

## Central Theme
Part III opens with the boldest claim in the book: the unconscious, under certain conditions, produces images that are properly called metaphysical — images of ultimacy, of cosmological structure, of the nature of God and the soul that cannot be explained by personal biography or cultural conditioning alone. Edinger illustrates this through a series of thirteen dreams from a single patient — an unreligious, unphilosophical man near death — whose unconscious produced, without prompting, imagery that parallels the most sophisticated Gnostic, alchemical, and mystical traditions. The chapter distinguishes empirical metaphysics (the study of the metaphysical images the psyche actually produces) from speculative metaphysics (philosophical system-building), and argues that analytical psychology is in fact a form of empirical metaphysics. It closes with the "Completion of the Opus" dream series, showing the unconscious's urgent need, especially near death, to convey a sense of transcendent meaning and completion.

## Key Ideas
- **Empirical metaphysics** is Edinger's term for the approach of analytical psychology to the "big questions": rather than asserting metaphysical truths from authority or reason, it observes and describes the metaphysical images that the unconscious spontaneously produces. These images are empirical data; their psychological reality is not contingent on their theological truth.
- The **metaphysical dream** is a recognizable category: it carries an exceptional charge of numinosity, often involves themes of cosmic proportions (the structure of creation, the nature of the divine, the fate of the soul), and tends to produce a permanent shift in the dreamer's relation to existence.
- The **dream series of the dying man** demonstrates that the pressure of death can telescope the individuation process, producing in a compressed time-frame the imagery that ordinarily takes decades of inner work to constellate. The unconscious behaves as if it "knows" death is imminent and urgently transmits what was not transmitted in life.
- **Dream 10 (Completion of the Opus)**: A man is assigned a task by God — to remove the hands of another at the wrists, trim them, and unite them to make a hexagonal shape. The resulting symbol, mummified and preserved, is deposited in a public library. Edinger reads this as: the ego's will (hands) must be amputated (surrendered to the Self), the opposites united (hexagon = Solomon's Seal = *coniunctio*), and the result preserved as a permanent contribution to the collective treasury of human consciousness.
- **The hexagon / Solomon's Seal** as a symbol of completed *coniunctio*: two triangles, upward and downward, uniting fire and water, spirit and matter, masculine and feminine. The number six is associated with creative completion (Genesis: world created in six days; Christ crucified on the sixth day of the week).
- **Dream 11**: A luminous golden woman with a Mona Lisa face wears a necklace of turquoise cabochons set in reddish gold. Edinger identifies her as Sophia, Divine Wisdom — the *Sapientia Dei*, the sum of archetypal images in the mind of God. The necklace as a "celestial rosary" uniting diverse forms corresponds to the Zodiac uniting the signs of the year.
- **Dream 12**: The dreamer must uncover a hidden log, carve from it a circular piece, then give it and a tape recording of its meaning to a public library. The five-fold design is the quintessence, the final unity of the four elements. The deposit in the library suggests that individual psychological work produces a permanent increment to the collective treasury of meaning — corresponding to the alchemical *Treasure House* which Wisdom built upon a rock.
- **Dream 13**: A large stone square garden with brass poles ("Bird in Space") — the *coniunctio* of stone (feminine, matter) and pole (masculine, spirit) in an image of completed totality. This was one of the last dreams before the man's death.
- The **Return to the Beginning** motif: several dreams in the series carry an atmosphere of primordial origin — of going back to the source. This corresponds to the alchemical *circulatio* and to the mystical sense of completion as a return to the state that existed before the ego-Self separation that initiated development.
- The **Transcendent Dimension**: the dreamer's experience of these dreams produced a gradual change in his life attitude — a metaphysical initiation that released him from preoccupation with personal frustrations and gave his personality "a new level of depth and dignity."

## Psychological Significance
This chapter addresses the **telos of individuation** — what the process is moving toward and what it produces in its final phase. It also demonstrates the stakes of the work: the unconscious operates as if the psychological task has genuine metaphysical importance, as if the deposit of individual achievement contributes to something beyond personal survival. For someone facing death (literal or symbolic — any major ending), the chapter offers the possibility that the work of consciousness is not merely therapeutic but participates in the self-realization of the totality.

## Key Images / Symbols
- **The hexagonal symbol / Solomon's Seal** — *coniunctio* of opposites, completion of creative work, image of the Self as the union of fire and water.
- **Sophia / the golden woman** — Divine Wisdom, the *Sapientia Dei*, the feminine personification of the Self's knowing capacity.
- **Brancusi's "Bird in Space"** (the brass poles in Dream 13) — the *axis mundi*, the vertical connection between human and transpersonal worlds; also the Osirian *Djed* column of life and resurrection.
- **The Treasure House / public library** — the alchemical and mystical image of accumulated spiritual achievement deposited for the benefit of the collective; the "treasury of merits."
- **The mummification motif** — the preservation of a psychic achievement against deterioration; the embalming of what has been spiritually accomplished as a permanent deposit.
- **The benedicta viriditas / blessed greenness** — the alchemical principle of life, growth, and resurrection; the Soul of the World (*Anima Mundi*) as green; the Holy Ghost as creative principle.

## Subsection Notes

**Empirical Metaphysics.** Edinger carefully distinguishes the psychological approach from both theology and materialist psychology. The question is not "Is God real?" but "What images of God does the unconscious produce, and what do they mean for the development of consciousness?" This is the empirical stance — descriptive, not assertive.

**Metaphysical Dreams.** The chapter establishes criteria: numinosity, cosmic scope, life-changing impact, and a symbolic richness that exceeds personal associations. Such dreams call for amplification (cross-referencing to mythological parallels) rather than personal association.

**Return to the Beginning.** The alchemical *circulatio* and the mystical *regressus ad uterum* — both describe the completion of the *opus* as a return to origin on a higher level. This is not regression but the spiral's return to its starting latitude after an ascent. The primordial unity recovered is not the unconscious wholeness of the beginning but conscious wholeness at the end.

**The Transcendent Dimension.** Edinger closes by noting that the dreamer — despite never undergoing formal analysis — underwent what can only be called a metaphysical initiation through his dream life, mediated by the presence of the analyst. The numinosity was released in the analytic session, not beforehand, suggesting that the witness of consciousness is required for the full realization of the Self's images.

**Completion of the Opus.** The final dreams show the unconscious pressing toward a totality image — the hexagon, the circular carved piece, the square garden with poles — each a variation on the mandala. The opus, for this man, was completed in image even though his outer life never reflected individuation in the conventional sense.
