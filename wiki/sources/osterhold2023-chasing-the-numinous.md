# Chasing the Numinous: Hungry Ghosts in the Shadow of the Psychedelic Renaissance

Paper by Jungian-oriented clinicians [[Helge Michael Osterhold]] and [[Gisele Fernandes-Osterhold]] (San Francisco), *Journal of Analytical Psychology* 68(4), 2023, pp. 638-664. Uses Jung's diagnosis of "modern man" and the Buddhist [[Hungry Ghost]] archetype to examine the cultural shadow dynamics underneath the 2020s "psychedelic renaissance."

Navigation: [[depth-psychology]] | [[Numinous Experience]] | [[Hungry Ghost]]

---

## The Argument in Brief

The paper does not argue against psychedelic-assisted therapy — it explicitly honours the clinical promise (PTSD, treatment-resistant depression, substance/alcohol use disorders, end-of-life anxiety) and situates the current wave as a partial *recovery* of ancient plant-medicine knowledge suppressed by colonial-era prohibition, not a novel discovery. Its target is the **collective shadow riding along with the promise**: a psychedelic "gold rush" of patent races, venture capital, exotic retreat tourism, undertrained guides, and clinician/shaman abuse scandals, unfolding in parallel with (and largely unexamined by) the genuine therapeutic science.

The authors read this gold rush through C. G. Jung's diagnosis of the modern Western psyche and, in the paper's most original move, through the Buddhist [[Hungry Ghost]] (*preta*/*peta*) — a being that can never be nourished because it cannot metabolize what it takes in — as an archetypal amplification of the same dynamic.

---

## 1. The Psychedelic Renaissance — Overview

Cannabis legalization, the end of decades of prohibition, and an accelerating body of clinical research (psilocybin, MDMA, ayahuasca, ibogaine) have produced "a forthcoming seismic shift in the psychiatric and psychotherapeutic treatment of a number of mental health concerns" — PTSD, MDD, substance/alcohol use disorders, end-of-life anxiety (Siegel et al. 2021; Yaden et al. 2022). Peer-reviewed publications jumped from 31 (1988) to 722 (2018) (Petranker et al. 2020).

Genuine risks remain understudied: research populations are highly selected and exclude at-risk individuals (Bender & Hellerstein 2022); people of colour are underrepresented in trials (George et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2019); real-world (uncontrolled) settings will see different risk profiles once medicines move beyond research contexts (Bradberry et al. 2022).

**Entheogen**: an "analogous term for psychedelics referring to 'generating the God within,'" covering spiritual/mystical experiences of oneness, unitary consciousness, and encounters with ancestors, sacred figures, and God, cross-culturally documented in shamanic ritual (Winkelman 2019, 2005). This word is the paper's bridge from clinical psychiatry into [[Numinous Experience]] territory.

**The Psychedelic Gold Rush**: over 50 publicly traded companies related to psychedelic-drug development in the US, market projected to grow from $2B (2020) to $10.75B (2027) (Phelps et al. 2022). Investors include Peter Thiel, Rebekah Mercer, and Mike Tyson. Compass Pathways has sought patents amounting to "blanket coverage" of psilocybin as a depression treatment (McDaniel 2021); Philip Morris has applied to patent e-cigarette technology for DMT vape pens (Love 2021). Meanwhile underground guides for those without research-trial access often charge thousands of dollars with no vetted competency standard, and ethical scandals (sexual, physical, financial abuse) have been documented at both underground and international retreat settings (Brown 2020; Siebert 2022; Tillett Wright 2021-2022). Ayahuasca tourism to Peru grew from ~2 retreat centres in the 1980s to ~50 by 2017 (Peluso 2017), with secondary harms: dilution of local traditions, diminished local access to traditional medicines, and a trade in jaguar body parts marketed as ritual enhancers (Braczkowski et al. 2019).

## 2. Jung's Diagnosis of Modern Man

The paper's psychological engine is Jung's argument that the historic Western preference for rational, empiric, extraverted orientation — amplified by the scientific revolution and the erosion of the Catholic Church's role as symbolic/ritual container — left "modern man" disconnected from the "innate mythic needs and capacities" that container once channeled. Jung (1931, para 163): "modern man has lost all the metaphysical certainties of his medieval brother." Without a spiritual/philosophical orientation, the psyche's "religious instinct" (1946b, para 157) goes unmet, and "if we repress or suppress this instinct, we can fall ill just as surely as we do when we interfere with our physical appetite for food, or with our sexual instinct" (Ulanov 1997, pp. 302-303).

The dislodged psychic energy either floods back into the psyche (upheaval) or is projected onto substitutes: "political ideas, money, success, or other obsessions, compulsions, or addictions." The authors quote Nietzsche (1993/1872, *Birth of Tragedy*) at length on culture "without a secure and sacred primal site," "stripped of myth," "digging and scrabbling for roots" — the direct literary ancestor of the paper's Hungry Ghost diagnosis. See [[Cultural Complex]].

## 3. Jungian Reflections on the Psychedelic Renaissance

**The promise**: psychedelics may offer a "non-doctrinal, experiential possibility for psycho-spiritual inquiry and connectedness" — a channel back to the numinous ground that doesn't require assent to any creed, which is exactly what would appeal to the secular modern orientation Jung describes. Jung's own formula for cure in the second half of life required developing a "religious orientation" — an "attitude peculiar to a consciousness which has been changed by experience of the *numinosum*" (1940, para 9). See [[Numinous Experience]] and [[Religious Function of the Psyche]].

**Jung's own reserved stance**: Jung wrote little directly on psychedelics and was "generally reserved and cautious," citing his own lack of deeper knowledge (Jung & Adler 1976, p. 172). His concern, stated in a letter, was that the collective unconscious accessed abruptly and powerfully may be "more than some individuals could integrate":

> "I only know there is no point in wishing to know more of the collective unconscious than one gets through dreams and intuition. The more you know of it, the greater and heavier becomes your moral burden, because the unconscious contents transform themselves into your individual tasks and duties as soon as they become conscious. Do you want to increase loneliness and misunderstanding? Do you want to find more and more complications and increasing responsibilities?" (Jung & Adler 1976, p. 172)

And on the marketing claim that "one medicine session is like years of psychotherapy": "I am profoundly mistrustful of the 'pure gifts of the Gods'. You pay dearly for them ... it soon becomes dangerous to know more, because one does not learn at the same time to balance it through a conscious equivalent" (Jung & Adler 1976, p. 173). The paper stresses Jung's distinction between the *primary vision* and its *personal, philosophical integration*: "the personal value lies entirely in the philosophical achievement, not in the primary vision" (1928, para 229) — a direct challenge to the "one session = years of therapy" marketing narrative, since a philosophical achievement of that scale plausibly cannot happen in three integration sessions.

**Deflation and inflation as the two dangers**: see [[Inflation-Jungian]] for the general concept; this paper adds a psychedelic-specific case. Deflation: the client "crushed under the contents of the unconscious" (1928, para 221), overwhelmed or fragmented by material surfacing faster than it can be metabolized. Inflation: feeling "godlike" or "superhuman" (1928, para 227) — "an unmistakable and often unpleasant increase in self-confidence... they are full of themselves, they know everything" (1928, para 221) — observed clinically in psychedelic research as post-session "grandiosity" that can turn "even conservative individuals... wildly enthusiastic" (Anderson et al. 2020, p. 830), and culturally in celebrities who have "seen the light," self-taught guides and "self-proclaimed shamans" operating without training, and investors/marketeers "who promise years of therapy in a single session."

**Jung's naiveté-and-hubris warning**: likening modern man to Goethe's sorcerer's apprentice — who "evoke[s] the ghosts without the knowledge on how to make them disappear again" — Jung ties the same collective pattern that produced "radioactive clouds over Japan, Calcutta and Saskatchewan" to the risk of broad, insufficiently understood psychedelic access (Jung & Adler 1976, p. 173).

## 4. The Western Meta-Cultural Complex

Drawing on [[Cultural Complex]] theory (Singer & Kimbles 2004 and others), the authors name three intersecting orientations of the Western psyche that feed the fascination with psychedelics specifically:

1. **Outward/empirical epistemology** — what can be measured and proven in the external, material world is credited as real; even spirituality now needs neurological imagery and RCTs to be believed. "Modern man abhors faith... He holds them valid only so far as their knowledge-content seems to accord with his own experience of the psychic background. He wants to *know* — to experience for himself" (Jung 1931, para 171).
2. **Heroic conviction** — the belief, modeled on myth, that a treasure can be found and a dragon defeated "through a swift strike of the sword"; that solutions should be clear, complete, attainable, not ambivalent, partial, or messy. Psychedelics as the latest iteration of the "wonder-drug" narrative previously attached to Valium (1970s) and Prozac (1990s) (Kocsis 2009).
3. **Deficiency-solution orientation** — the neurotic sense that something essential is missing is resolved not through inner development but through acquisition or ingestion of something external (money, status, a pill, a retreat). "Happiness itself came to be a part of a new psychological standard of living" (Herzberg 2009, p. 234) — one can now "purchase triumphs and discoveries" instead of earning them.

## 5. Hungry Ghosts

Full treatment: [[Hungry Ghost]]. In Buddhist cosmology the *preta* (Skt.) / *peta* (Pali) is one of the six realms of cyclic existence (*saṃsāra*): a being with a stomach "the size of a whole country" and a throat "as narrow as a horse-hair," permanently starving and thirsty, unable to metabolize even what it manages to swallow (Rinpoche 1998). The karmic cause of rebirth as a hungry ghost is *mātsarya* (Skt.) / *macchariya* (Pali): "meanness" — miserliness, an unwillingness to give or share, plus jealousy and aggression toward the good fortune of others (Rotman 2021).

The authors read the hungry-ghost dynamic symbolically onto the current psychedelic gold rush at three levels: (1) the corporate/capital level — patenting, "locking out competitors," commodifying indigenous medicines; (2) the seeker level — thousands of Westerners "ever seeking and hungry for experiences of ascendance and transcendence," rummaging the spiritual retreat "buffet" without integrating what they find; (3) the guide/therapist level — inflation-driven abuse of power, status-seeking within the psychedelic community. The unifying diagnostic: **an inability to take in and digest** what is consumed — the mirror image of the Western psyche's disconnection from its own depth.

## 6. Conclusion

The paper closes by proposing that psychedelic therapy, depth psychology, and Buddhist practice may converge on the same underlying task — turning inward to relieve suffering by expanding consciousness to previously unseen material — but that sustainable healing still requires the slow work: "a re-connection with our own depth... it will likely continue to matter, to be in relationship with the deeper psyche and examine the shadows in longer-term, depth-oriented psychotherapy or embodied, relational and spiritual practice." Its closing image: individuation as "circumambulating the centre," walking the winding mountain path, "rather than taking a helicopter tour around its peak once, or again and again" — a direct image-level echo of the vault's existing [[Amplification]] concept (circumambulation as method) applied here to an entire cultural moment rather than a single dream image.
