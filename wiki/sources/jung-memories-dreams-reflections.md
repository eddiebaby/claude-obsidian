# Memories, Dreams, Reflections — C.G. Jung

**Author:** C.G. Jung (recorded and edited by Aniela Jaffé)
**Published:** 1962 (Pantheon Books, USA; Collins, UK)
**Format:** Autobiography / memoir; dictated and refined in the last years of Jung's life
**Source file:** `.raw/Dream-Jung/Books/Memories_Dreams_Reflections_-_CG_Jung.txt`

---

## Overview

Jung's autobiography in a strict sense is not one: he describes it in the opening line as "a story of the self-realization of the unconscious." The book moves through his childhood, his break with Freud, the pivotal years of inner crisis (1913-1917), his encounters with alchemy and Gnosticism, travels to Africa and the American Southwest, and his old age at Bollingen. The throughline is not career but psyche. Aniela Jaffé worked with Jung from 1956 onward; some chapters are dictated directly, others reconstructed from conversations and existing manuscripts.

The book is the primary autobiographical account of what became the conceptual foundation of analytical psychology. What the Collected Works presents as systematic theory, MDR presents as lived encounter.

---

## Opening Premise

The first sentence is programmatic: "My life is a story of the self-realization of the unconscious. Everything in the unconscious seeks outward manifestation, and the personality too desires to evolve out of its unconscious conditions and to experience itself as a whole."

This framing — life as the unconscious's project of self-expression — recasts ordinary biography. Events are not what happened to Jung; they are what the unconscious was trying to accomplish through him.

---

## Personality No. 1 and No. 2

Jung identifies from early childhood two distinct layers of himself: Personality No. 1, the ordinary boy living in time, anxious about school and social position; Personality No. 2, a vast, ageless figure at home in the pre-Christian world, experienced in moments of solitude and in contact with nature. The tension between them — "No. 1 wanted to free himself from the pressure or melancholy of No. 2" — is the earliest form of what he would later theorize as the ego-Self axis.

The duality is not pathology. It is the native condition. Personality No. 2 is not escapism but a deeper substrate of being, one that most adults lose access to as socialization solidifies. Jung's lifelong theoretical interest in the deep layers of the psyche is rooted in this personal discovery.

---

## The Break with Freud and the Turn Inward (1912-1913)

The separation from Freud, completed around 1912-1913 with the publication of "Symbols of Transformation" (CW 5), left Jung without the protective scaffolding of the psychoanalytic movement. He describes the aftermath as disorientation: "I had to find evidence for the historical prefiguration of my inner experiences."

The break was intellectual as well as personal. Freud's insistence on sexuality as the primary libido-content was, for Jung, a reductive mythology masquerading as science. Jung had already argued in CW 5 that libido was undifferentiated psychic energy that could be genuinely transformed, not merely displaced. The disagreement was not amendable.

## The Confrontation with the Unconscious (1913-1917)

The core chapter of the book and of Jung's psychological biography. Starting in the autumn of 1913, a flood of fantasy-images and visions overwhelmed his ordinary life. He describes standing helpless before an alien world, living in constant tension, sometimes feeling as if gigantic blocks of stone were tumbling down on him: "Often I felt as if gigantic blocks of stone were tumbling down upon me. One thunderstorm followed another."

His response was disciplined engagement rather than retreat. He committed himself to recording and attempting to understand whatever arose, working on these materials in the evenings after clinic hours, protecting his family life and professional practice as anchors against the chaos.

**Key events of this period:**

### The Descent Vision (late 1913)

Jung describes deliberately letting himself descend inward — making "several attempts to get to the very bottom." On one occasion he reached "the edge of a cosmic abyss. It was like a voyage to the moon, or a descent into empty space." He encountered a crater and the sense of being in the land of the dead. There he met two interior figures: an old man with a white beard (who identified himself as Elijah) and a young blind girl (Salome). A large black snake accompanied them.

He notes his immediate interpretive move: "Elijah is the figure of the wise old prophet and represents the factor of intelligence and knowledge; Salome, the erotic element. One might say that the two figures are personifications of Logos and Eros." But he warns himself against over-intellectualizing — "It is more meaningful to let the figures be what they were for me at the time."

The snake as hero-companion, Salome as [[Anima and Animus|anima]] figure, and Elijah as precursor to the [[Individuation#Wise Old Man|wise old man]] archetype — these are the first autonomous inner figures of the confrontation period.

### The Siegfried Dream (1913)

A decisive dream: Jung and an unknown brown-skinned man (a "savage") are on a mountain at dawn. Siegfried appears on the crest, riding at furious speed in a chariot made of bones of the dead. They shoot and kill him. Jung wakes filled with "disgust and remorse for having destroyed something so great and beautiful."

His interpretation: "I reflected that Siegfried, the hero, no longer suited me. Therefore it had to be killed." Siegfried represents the heroic-will-imposing ideal — the Germanic mode of imposing one's will on the world. "There are higher things than the ego's will." The dream signals the necessity of sacrificing the heroic ego-ideal in favor of something less consciously controlled. The secret identity with Siegfried, acknowledged in the grief, is a classic projection-recognition moment. Cross-reference: [[Psychological Sacrifice]].

### Elijah Becomes Philemon

Over time the Elijah figure evolved and was replaced by a new figure Jung called Philemon: pagan, winged, lame-footed, with an Egypto-Hellenistic atmosphere and Gnostic coloring. He first appeared in a dream of a blue sky across which a winged being sailed — an old man with the horns of a bull and the wings of a kingfisher.

Jung painted the figure. Then a kingfisher with iridescent blue-green wings appeared in his garden — an animal he had never seen there before and never saw again afterward. He notes the coincidence without forcing interpretation.

Philemon was not a fantasy figure to be analyzed but a presence to be engaged: "Philemon represented superior insight. He was a mysterious figure to me. At times he seemed to me quite real, as if he were a living personality. I went walking up and down the garden with him, and to me he was what the Indians call a guru."

The significance: Philemon embodied the discovery that "there are things in the psyche which I do not produce, but which produce themselves and have their own life." This is not merely a theoretical formulation — it is the empirical core of the concept of the autonomous unconscious. Philemon conveyed illuminating ideas that felt genuinely other, not products of Jung's will. A later Indian visitor, a friend of Gandhi, recognized the description immediately as a guru relationship.

After Philemon, a further figure emerged: Ka, a kind of earth or metal demon, earthly where Philemon was spiritual. The two together, spiritual aspect and earth-bound aspect, constituted a differentiated inner world.

### The Septem Sermones ad Mortuos (1916)

In 1916 Jung's household was pervaded by a ghostly presence — footsteps, apparitions, children reporting unusual experiences. Jung found himself in a state of intense inner pressure. The release came when he sat down to write: in three evenings he produced the *Septem Sermones ad Mortuos* (Seven Sermons to the Dead), a Gnostic-flavored text attributed to the 2nd-century Basilides. As soon as he took up the pen, "the whole ghostly assemblage evaporated."

He describes this as an example of autonomous psychic production — a phenomenon he associates in MDR with psychoid processes. He later regretted allowing it to be printed, calling it a "sin of his youth," but it contains early formulations of the polarity principle that runs through all his later work (the tension of opposites as the source of psychic energy). Cross-reference: [[CW8 Theoretical Foundations]].

## Recording the Fantasies: The Black Books and the Red Book

During the confrontation period Jung maintained parallel records:

- The **Black Books**: working journals in which he wrote down fantasies and visions as they arose, usually in imagistic, not yet literary, form.
- The **Red Book** (Liber Novus): a large folio bound in red leather, containing the same fantasies reworked in elaborate literary form, calligraphic Gothic script, and illuminated with paintings and mandala drawings.

He writes: "In the Red Book I tried an esthetic elaboration of my fantasies, but never finished it. I became aware that I had not yet found the right language, that I still had to translate it into something else. Therefore I gave up this estheticizing tendency in good time, in favor of a rigorous process of understanding."

The Red Book was not published in Jung's lifetime — it appeared posthumously in 2009 as *The Red Book: Liber Novus*, edited by Sonu Shamdasani. MDR's account of its genesis is the primary first-person description of how it came to be.

## The Mandala and the Discovery of the Self (1918-1920)

During 1918-1919, while stationed in Château d'Oex as commandant of British internees, Jung began drawing a small mandala every morning. He observed that each drawing reflected his inner psychic state: "I saw that everything, all the paths I had been following, all the steps I had taken, were leading back to a single point — namely, to the mid-point. It became increasingly plain to me that the mandala is the center. It is the exponent of all paths. It is the path to the center, to individuation."

The key shift: "Between 1918 and 1920, I began to understand that the goal of psychic development is the self. There is no linear evolution; there is only a circumambulation of the self."

A 1927 dream confirmed this — he represented it as the "Window on Eternity" mandala with a golden castle at the center (the "Golden Flower" mandala, linked to Richard Wilhelm's 1928 transmission of the *Secret of the Golden Flower*). Cross-reference: [[Mandala Symbolism]], [[Self (archetype)]].

## Richard Wilhelm and the Golden Flower (1928)

The end of the confrontation period's isolation came when Richard Wilhelm sent Jung the manuscript of the *Secret of the Golden Flower* (a Chinese alchemical-Taoist text) in 1928 with a request for a commentary. Jung writes: "The text gave me undreamed-of confirmation of my ideas about the mandala and the circumambulation of the center. That was the first event which broke through my isolation. I became aware of an affinity; I could establish ties with something and someone."

This coincidence (his inner mandala-work and the arrival of this Chinese text treating analogous symbols) prompted the desire to study alchemical texts more broadly. He commissioned a Munich bookseller to acquire alchemical material.

---

## Alchemy as Psychological Research

Jung's encounter with alchemical texts — beginning with *Artis Auriferae Volumina Duo* (1593) — was transformative in a specific way: the alchemists were doing psychology without knowing it. Their cryptic language about metals, furnaces, and the philosopher's stone was a symbolic, projected description of unconscious processes.

His own formulation: "The experiences of the alchemists were, in a sense, my experiences, and their world was my world. This was, of course, a momentous discovery: I had stumbled upon the historical counterpart of my psychology of the unconscious."

What alchemy provided that Gnosticism alone could not: "Alchemy formed the bridge on the one hand into the past, to Gnosticism, and on the other into the future, to the modern psychology of the unconscious." Gnosticism had been severed from the present by centuries; alchemy was continuous through the medieval period into the seventeenth century and thus offered a datable, citable chain of symbolic amplification.

The practical result: *Psychology and Alchemy* (CW 12, 1944), which Jung describes as reaching "the ground which underlay my own experiences of the years 1913 to 1917; for the process through which I had passed at that time corresponded to the process of alchemical transformation discussed in that book."

Cross-reference: [[jung-cw12-psychology-alchemy]].

Key claim about the feminine in alchemy: "In alchemy, the feminine principle plays a role equal to that of the masculine. Before I discovered alchemy, I had a series of dreams which repeatedly dealt with the same theme. Beside my house stood another wing, strange to me." The dream of the unknown annex — always there but never explored — anticipates his discovery of the alchemical library. The annex was alchemy; its feminine connotation reflects alchemy's central role for the [[Anima and Animus|anima]] and the *soror mystica*.

## Bollingen Tower

From 1923 onward Jung built a stone tower at Bollingen on the upper lake of Zurich. He built it in phases over decades, always following immediate concrete needs. He recognized only afterward that the structure was a mandala in stone — a symbol of psychic wholeness made material.

"At Bollingen I am in the midst of my true life, I am most deeply myself. Here I am, as it were, the age-old son of the mother."

The tower as individuation artifact: the building of it in dreams corresponded to the process of psychic integration. A "small central section which crouched so low, so hidden" revealed itself as himself — the ego no longer able to hide behind the projections. Cross-reference: [[Individuation]].

## Individuation: The Theoretical Summary

Late in the book Jung draws together his conclusions:

- Individuation is **not linear evolution but circumambulation** of the Self.
- "The individual is still relying on a collective organization to effect his differentiation for him; that is, he has not yet recognized that it is really the individual's task."
- Individuation at a late stage means "detachment from valuations and from what we call emotional ties" — not coldness but the withdrawal of projections so that genuine relationship (not fantasy projection) becomes possible.
- The danger of confusing individuation with ego-centredness: "If the individuation process is confused with the coming of the ego into consciousness and that the ego is in consequence identified with the self, this naturally produces a hopeless conceptual muddle. Individuation is then nothing but ego-centredness and autoeroticism. But the self comprises infinitely more than a mere ego."

Cross-reference: [[Individuation]], [[Self (archetype)]], [[Ego]].

## Relation to the Collected Works

MDR is not a CW volume; it is not systematic theory. Its function is to provide the biographical *why* behind the CW. Reading CW volumes after MDR transforms abstract theoretical claims into recognizable moves in a lived struggle:

- The individuaton theory (CW 9i, 9ii) is the conceptual crystallization of the 1913-1920 inner events.
- The alchemy project (CW 12, 13, 14) is the "historical counterpart" Jung found for those events.
- The psychic energy and compensation theories (CW 8) are the mechanics extracted from the inner dynamic he lived through.
- The Red Book / Liber Novus is the unedited primary material; MDR is the retrospective narrative.

## Source Quality

This is an autobiographical narrative, not a clinical treatise. Dates are sometimes approximate. Some passages were dictated late in life and reconstructed. Jaffé's editorial hand is present but generally unobtrusive. The account should be read as phenomenological testimony, not historical documentation. Where MDR's account of a theory diverges from the CW formulation, the CW takes precedence for theoretical purposes; MDR provides experiential context.
