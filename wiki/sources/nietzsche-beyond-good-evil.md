# Beyond Good and Evil

Friedrich Nietzsche, *Jenseits von Gut und Bose* (1886). One of Nietzsche's mature philosophical works: a sustained critique of philosophical dogmatism, Christian morality, and the herd instinct. Nine parts plus an epilogue. Foundational precursor to Jung's analytical psychology; Jung engaged Nietzsche intensively in his *Zarathustra Seminars* (1934-1939).

## Part I: On the Prejudices of Philosophers

Nietzsche opens by attacking the unexamined assumption that the will to truth is the highest value. Why does truth deserve unconditional pursuit? He argues that philosophers have systematically misread their own motives: what they present as disinterested pursuit of truth is really a drive to impose their values on reality.

Key charges:
- **Metaphysical dualism is psychological symptom**: the Platonic preference for the "real world" over the "apparent world" is a disguised devaluation of this world, of the body, of the drives. It mirrors the ascetic priest's hatred of life.
- **The value of opposites**: Nietzsche questions whether what we call "truth" and "falsehood" are actually opposed or whether falsehood (illusion, simplification) is necessary for life. "The falseness of a judgment is for us not necessarily an objection to it."
- **Will to power in cognition**: all intellectual drives, including the will to truth, are expressions of a fundamental drive Nietzsche calls the Will to Power. Knowing is an expression of commanding, not of passive mirroring.

**Psychological relevance**: The critique of philosophical self-deception directly anticipates Jung's insight that the persona (the face we show) and the philosophical system are often rationalizations of something the thinker refuses to acknowledge. The philosopher's "system" is the shadow in intellectual disguise.

## Part II: The Free Spirit

The "free spirit" is Nietzsche's name for the genuinely independent thinker: not someone who has adopted skepticism as a new orthodoxy, but someone who has genuinely freed themselves from the compulsion to be certain.

Characteristics:
- Can live experimentally, with hypotheses rather than convictions
- Does not need the herd's approval or the metaphysician's absolute ground
- Understands that every great philosophy is "an involuntary and unconscious memoir" of its author
- Is capable of commanding as well as obeying
- Hardened by suffering and solitude rather than softened by comfort

**Jung's engagement**: The free spirit anticipates Jung's description of the individuated person: not freed from the unconscious, but capable of engaging it consciously rather than being driven by it. Both figures require the sacrifice of comfort, consensus, and certainty.

---

## Part V: On the Natural History of Morals

Nietzsche introduces the genealogical method: rather than asking whether a moral claim is true, ask where it came from and what drives it. Moralities are symptoms of psychological states.

Key points:
- Morality is not universal or self-evident; it is always a local expression of particular drives
- "Herd morality" names the moral code that serves the preservation of the group: conformity, equality, compassion, anti-excellence
- The philosopher of the future must be a legislator of new values, not a passive observer
- Every ethics tacitly encodes a psychology: what does this morality assume about human nature? What does it fear?

## Part IX: What Is Noble

The culminating section. Nietzsche distinguishes two fundamental moral orientations:

**Noble/master morality**: The noble type creates values by self-affirmation. "Good" names what the noble is; "bad" names what is different, lower, weaker. This is not primarily a hostile judgment but a self-affirmation. The noble type acts from abundance, not resentment.

**Slave/ressentiment morality**: The slave type creates values through negation of the other. "Evil" (the enemy, the master) comes first; "good" is defined by contrast as not-evil. This morality is reactive, born from the impotence to act directly. It is the morality of resentment (*ressentiment*).

The slave revolt in morality (developed more fully in *On the Genealogy of Morals*): through Christianity and its secular descendants, the slave morality has triumphed. Excellence, hardness, solitude, pride are now "evil"; weakness, meekness, communal spirit are "good."

**The noble virtues**: self-overcoming, honesty, creation of values, the pathos of distance (the hierarchical instinct, the sense that not all people are equal in what matters), the capacity to command.

**Concerning nobility and the Will to Power**: "Every enhancement of the type 'man' has so far been the work of an aristocratic society - and so it will always be." Not political aristocracy but psychological: those who have the strength to bear the truth of existence without consolation.

## The Will to Power

Throughout BGE, the Will to Power is not a simple drive for domination over others but the fundamental tendency of all living things to discharge their strength, to grow, to overcome. It is more like an inner pressure to actualize capacity than a desire for conquest.

Key formulations:
- The Will to Power is not "wanting to be preserved" (that is reactive, Darwinian) but "wanting to grow, to spread, to discharge strength"
- Even the intellect's will to truth is will to power: an overcoming of the compulsion to comfort and illusion
- Self-overcoming (*Selbstuberwindung*) is the highest expression of will to power: not conquest of others but conquest of oneself

**Jung's engagement with Will to Power**: Jung was explicit that the Will to Power is the psychological content of the power complex. When a person cannot individuate (i.e., cannot relationally surrender to the unconscious), power fills the gap. The Adlerian drive for superiority and the Nietzschean Will to Power both describe the psychology of inflation: the ego asserting itself against the larger psyche.

## The Ubermensch (Overman)

BGE does not use *Ubermensch* as extensively as *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, but the concept underlies the account of the "philosopher of the future" and the noble type. The Overman is the person who has genuinely gone beyond conventional good and evil: not by becoming amoral in a destructive sense, but by having passed through morality and emerged with self-legislated values on the other side.

Characteristics:
- Has overcome the need for external validation, metaphysical consolation, or herd approval
- Creates values rather than inheriting them
- Capable of self-overcoming: the recurring sacrifice of one's current form in favor of growth

**The inflation danger (Jung's explicit critique)**: Jung addressed this at length in the *Zarathustra Seminars*. Nietzsche himself became inflated by identifying with his own creation. The Zarathustra figure - who speaks as the prophet of the Overman - is not a psychological program but a mythological figure. When a person literally identifies with the Ubermensch ideal, the ego has swallowed the Self rather than the Self enlarging the ego. The result is megalomaniac inflation, which is psychologically indistinguishable from possession by an archetype.

Jung's formulation: the Overman is an archetypal idea; it belongs to the collective unconscious, not to the ego. To "become" the Overman is to be possessed by an archetype. Nietzsche's eventual breakdown is Jung's exhibit A.

See [[Ubermensch and Individuation]] and [[Individuation]] for the full comparison.

## Master/Slave Morality and the Shadow

The master/slave polarity maps onto Jungian dynamics in two ways:

**Shadow projection**: The slave morality is built on the projection of the "evil" onto the master. What cannot be integrated (one's own aggression, vitality, hunger for power) is disowned and named "evil" in the other. The moralist is a failed shadow-worker: they have not integrated their own power but made a virtue of the incapacity to do so.

**The "good" as Shadow**: Conversely, what conventional morality calls "evil" (aggression, self-assertion, hierarchy-making, the will to command) is often shadow material in the Jungian sense - legitimate psychic contents repressed rather than integrated. Nietzsche's "transvaluation" and Jung's shadow-integration are parallel moves, though Nietzsche risks swinging to the opposite extreme (glorifying the shadow) while Jung aims at integration.

See [[Master-Slave Morality]] and [[Shadow]].

## Jung's Engagement with Nietzsche

Jung lectured on *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* in a seminar running from 1934 to 1939 (Bollingen Series XCIX, 2 vols.). He treated Zarathustra as a psychological document: Nietzsche's unconscious speaking through the prophet figure. Key points Jung made:

1. **Nietzsche as psychologist**: "Nietzsche's psychology is one of the most profound ever attempted." He saw further into the interior life than most analysts.

2. **Inflation as cautionary tale**: Nietzsche's eventual identification with Zarathustra (and through him, with Dionysus and the Crucified) is the paradigmatic case of psychic inflation - the ego consumed by an archetypal content.

3. **The shadow in the Overman ideal**: The Overman, taken literally as a personal ideal, is the ego's fantasy of transcending the human condition - which is exactly what individuation is not. Individuation does not transcend the human; it deepens and integrates it.

4. **The Will to Power as compensation**: Where Eros (relatedness, love, vulnerability) is lacking or repressed, Power fills the gap. Jung held that both Nietzsche and Adler had correctly identified a major dynamic - but that the Will to Power is not the primary drive; it is what happens when the capacity for genuine relationship has been blocked.

5. **The eternal recurrence**: In the *Zarathustra Seminars*, Jung interpreted eternal recurrence as Nietzsche's confrontation with the absolute necessity of accepting one's fate (amor fati) - equivalent to the Jungian acceptance of the totality of the psyche, including its suffering. In this light it is a genuine psychological insight about the attitude required for integration.

## Key Passages (Representative Quotes)

- "Supposing truth is a woman - what then?" (Opening question, BGE Preface - the will to truth as erotic/possessive rather than disinterested)
- "The falseness of a judgment is for us not necessarily an objection to it." (BGE §4)
- "Every philosophy is the confession of its originator, and a species of involuntary and unconscious autobiography." (BGE §6)
- "One must repay good and ill; but why precisely to the person who did good or ill to us?" (BGE §87 - the critique of reciprocity morality)
- "What is noble? ... The noble human being honors in himself the powerful one, him also who has power over himself." (BGE §260)
- "There is an instinct for rank, which more than anything else is already the sign of a high rank." (BGE §263)
- "That which is done out of love always takes place beyond good and evil." (BGE §153)

## Relevance to This Vault

This text is a secondary-domain source for the depth psychology project. Its relevance is primarily:

1. As intellectual precursor to Jung: Nietzsche diagnosed the unconscious drives behind morality and cognition before the psychoanalytic movement gave them clinical language.
2. As cautionary material: the Overman/inflation dynamic is a live danger in individuation work, especially for high-functioning, intellectually ambitious analysands.
3. As conceptual background for the Shadow concept: the master/slave distinction maps onto shadow projection and the need for integration rather than transvaluation.
4. As source for the Will to Power concept as it enters Jungian thinking (via Adler's "drive for superiority" and Jung's power complex).
