## What This Book Is

Aion is Jung's sustained investigation of the Self archetype as it appears in the history of Western religious symbolism - primarily Christian, Gnostic, and alchemical. It is the companion volume to CW 9i ([[jung-cw9i-archetypes-collective-unconscious]]), which surveyed the collective unconscious and its archetypal inhabitants in essay form. Aion takes one archetype - the Self - and traces its historical "incarnations" through two millennia of the "Christian aeon."

Jung's thesis, stated in the Foreword (directly read): "My investigation seeks, with the help of Christian, Gnostic, and alchemical symbols of the self, to throw light on the change of psychic situation within the 'Christian aeon.'" The title refers both to the Mithraic god Aion (lion-headed, serpent-wreathed figure depicted in the frontispiece) and to the Greek word *aion* meaning "age" or "aeon" - the Platonic month of Pisces, approximately 2,000 years, which coincides with the Christian era.

## Key Claims (directly read: Foreword + Chs I-V, paras 1-71)

### Foreword (directly read)

- Theme is the Aeon (Greek *Aion*): the book investigates psychic change within the "Christian aeon"
- Christian tradition is "saturated with Persian and Jewish ideas about the beginning and end of time" and "filled with intimations of a kind of enantiodromian reversal of dominants" - the dilemma of Christ and Antichrist
- The Pisces aeon is "the synchronistic concomitant of two thousand years of Christian development"
- Jung writes "as a physician... and not as a proselyte. Nor do I write as a scholar, for the scholar... would wisely barricade myself behind the safe walls of my specialism" (dated May 1950)

### Ch I: The Ego (paras 1-12, directly read)

- The *self* was formulated to denote a concept supraordinate to the *ego*. The ego is "the complex factor to which all conscious contents are related. It forms, as it were, the centre of the field of consciousness" (para 1)
- Ego rests on two bases: somatic (endosomatic perceptions) and psychic. The psychic basis falls into three groups of unconscious content: (1) temporarily subliminal, reproducible by will (memory); (2) unconscious contents NOT voluntarily reproducible; (3) contents not capable of becoming conscious at all (para 4)
- "The personality as a total phenomenon does not coincide with the ego... but forms an entity that has to be distinguished from the ego" (para 8)
- "I have suggested calling the total personality which, though present, cannot be fully known, the *self*. The ego is, by definition, subordinate to the self and is related to it like a part to the whole" (para 9)
- The unconscious, from the standpoint of the personality, divides into personal (contents are personal) and "extra-conscious" (contents are *impersonal and collective*) - the latter is "an omnipresent, unchanging, and everywhere identical quality or substrate of the psyche per se" (para 12)

### Ch II: The Shadow (paras 13-19, directly read)

- The archetypes "most clearly characterized from the empirical point of view are those which have the most frequent and the most disturbing influence on the ego. These are the shadow, the anima, and the animus" (para 13)
- "The shadow is a moral problem that challenges the whole ego-personality. To become conscious of it involves recognizing the dark aspects of the personality as present and real. This act is the essential condition for any kind of self-knowledge" (para 14)
- Shadow has an "emotional nature, a kind of autonomy, and accordingly an obsessive or, better, possessive quality" (para 15)
- On projections: "The more one is convinced that he is right, and if anybody is wrong it must be the other fellow" - projections isolate the subject from their environment (paras 16-17)
- Key distinction: shadow (same sex as the subject) vs. anima/animus (contrasexual); the shadow is closer to consciousness, can be recognized "with a little self-criticism"; anima/animus are "much further away from consciousness and in normal circumstances are seldom if ever realized" (para 19)
- "With a little self-criticism one can see through the shadow - so far as its nature is personal. But when it appears as an archetype, one encounters the same difficulties as with anima and animus... it is a rare and shattering experience for him to gaze into the face of absolute evil" (para 19)

### Ch III: The Syzygy: Anima and Animus (paras 20-42, directly read)

- The "projection-making factor" (what projects the anima) is what the East calls "Spinning Woman" - Maya, creator of illusion (para 20)
- The anima is NOT a substitute for the mother; rather, the mother-imago derives its numinous power from the collective archetype of the anima (para 27)
- Anima corresponds to the maternal Eros; animus corresponds to the paternal Logos. "Woman's consciousness is characterized more by the connective quality of Eros than by the discrimination and cognition associated with Logos" (para 29)
- "When animus and anima meet, the animus draws his sword of power and the anima ejects her poison of illusion and seduction" (para 30) - the well-known "love at first sight" phenomenon
- The anima and animus "as the foundation stones of the psychic structure... in its totality exceeds the limits of consciousness and therefore can never become the object of direct cognition" (para 40)
- Summary of the individuation sequence (para 42): shadow integration is "the first stage in the analytic process"; anima/animus recognition requires a partner of opposite sex because only in such relation do their projections "become operative"; the missing fourth element making the triad a quaternio is, in a man, "the Wise Old Man" and in a woman "the Chthonic Mother" - together these four form the "marriage quaternio"
- "The self... is a God-image, or at least cannot be distinguished from one" (para 42)

### Ch IV: The Self (paras 43-67, directly read)

- Inflation: when the ego assimilates the self, the "image of wholeness then remains in the unconscious" - it is "a psychic catastrophe when the *ego is assimilated by the self*" (para 45); the reverse (self assimilated to ego) is equally dangerous
- Mandalas are empirical symbols of the self, occurring in dreams "principally during times of psychic disorientation or re-orientation"; as magic circles they "bind and subdue the lawless powers belonging to the world of darkness" (para 60)
- "Unity and totality stand at the highest point on the scale of objective values because their symbols can no longer be distinguished from the *imago Dei*. Hence all statements about the God-image apply also to the empirical symbols of totality" (para 60)
- The post-Christian spirit is a "true *antimimon pneuma*, a false spirit of arrogance, hysteria, woolly-mindedness, criminal amorality, and doctrinaire fanaticism" (para 67)

### Ch V: Christ, a Symbol of the Self (paras 68-71, directly read)

- "Christ exemplifies the archetype of the self" (para 70, directly quoted) - he represents totality of a divine or heavenly kind, *sine macula peccati* (without stain of sin)
- As *Adam secundus* he corresponds to the first Adam before the Fall
- The *imago Dei* is imprinted on the soul, not the body (Origen); Augustine distinguishes the God-image that is Christ from the image implanted in man as a *possibility* of becoming like God

## Summary of Chapters VI-XV (established Jungian scholarship; not directly read)

**Ch VI-IX: The Fish Symbol (pp. 72-125)** - The fish was the primary symbol of Christ in early Christianity. Jung traces its astrological origin (the Piscean aeon), its pre-Christian roots in Babylonian and Jewish tradition, and its alchemical transformations. The fish embodies the paradox of Christ: savior from below (water, chthonic), not above.

**Ch X-XII: Fish in Alchemy (pp. 126-183)** - Extended survey of the fish symbol in alchemical texts (Medusa, the ordinary fish, Cathar fish symbolism). The alchemical fish is linked to Mercurius and the *prima materia* - the raw material of psychic transformation. This grounds the Jungian claim that alchemy was projecting psychological processes onto matter.

**Ch XIII: Gnostic Symbols of the Self (pp. 184-221)** - The Gnostic tradition (Naassenes, Valentinians, etc.) developed elaborate symbolic systems for the Self/Anthropos. Jung reads these as independent confirmation of the psychological archetype: the Gnostic Anthropos (Primordial Man), Sophia, and the Pleroma are mythological expressions of the same contents that appear in individual dreams.

**Ch XIV: Structure and Dynamics of the Self (pp. 222-265)** - The most technically dense chapter. Jung works out the structure of the self through various quaternio schemas: the anthropos quaternio, the shadow quaternio, the lapis quaternio, the Moses quaternio, and the "paradise quaternio." The self as a space-time quaternio (para 251-257). The chapter culminates in a diagram of how the self relates to its constituent polarities.

**Ch XV: Conclusion (pp. 266-270)** - Jung summarizes: depth psychology has shown that psychic contents are "real" in a sense that cannot be reduced to either materialism or metaphysics. The Christ symbol provided Western culture with a vehicle for Self-experience for 2,000 years; as it loses its living symbolic force, the contents it contained return as psychological phenomena requiring conscious integration.
