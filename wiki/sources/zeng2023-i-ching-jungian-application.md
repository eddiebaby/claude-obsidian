# The I Ching as a Potential Jungian Application: History and Practice

**Author**: [[Jingchao Zeng]] (Colchester, UK — University of Essex)
**Publication**: Journal of Analytical Psychology, 2023, 68(5), 913-932
**Keywords**: Carl Jung, China, divinatory practice, history, the I Ching, therapy

## One-line summary

A historical and critical study of Jung's lifelong practical engagement with the *I Ching* (Book of Changes) — begun in 1919, deepened through his 1920 friendship with translator [[Richard-Wilhelm|Richard Wilhelm]] — organized into three dimensions (physical, psychological, psychical), followed by an assessment of how three contemporary Jungian clinicians ([[Jean-Kirsch|Jean Kirsch]], [[Mengchao-Li|Mengchao Li]], [[Dennis-Merritt|Dennis Merritt]]) have carried I Ching divination into the consulting room, and where each imports unexamined cross-cultural conflations along the way.

## Why This Paper Matters for the Vault's Synchronicity Coverage

The existing [[Synchronicity]] page already records that Jung's own **astrological experiment failed statistically** (¶901: "merely chance") and that he was careful to treat synchronicity as a cautious empirical claim, not a metaphysical cosmology. This paper is not about astrology — it is about a *different* Jungian divinatory practice, the I Ching — and it extends rather than contradicts that caution. Jung never ran (or claimed to run) a statistical test of the I Ching's accuracy; his engagement was avowedly personal, clinical, and interpretive — closer to what Maggie Hyde calls **Synchronicity II** (the observer's participatory, meaning-making encounter with a symbol) than to **Synchronicity I** (an objective, testable correlation). ## Background: Yi-ology and the Two Schools

The *I Ching* (Book of Changes; classically the *Zhouyi*) has been studied in China under the umbrella term **Yi-ology** since at least the Zhou Dynasty (1046-256 BCE), when King Wen is credited with creating the 64 hexagrams by overlaying two of the eight trigrams first attributed to the legendary emperor Fuxi, and Confucius with writing the first commentaries (the *Yizhuan*). Two schools have run through the entire history of its study:

- **The image-number school** (*Xiang Shu*) — divinatory, working through the images, numbers, and numerological relationships of the trigrams and hexagrams themselves (interlocking trigrams, Yin-yang waxing/waning, the *Na Jia* heavenly-stems/earthly-branches system, hexagram timelines).
- **The philosophical school** (*Yi Li*) — literal/textual, treating the *Yizhuan* commentaries as the primary object of study and giving little credit to non-textual divinatory technique.

Zeng places Jung's own interest closer to the image-number school: practical, technique-oriented, uninterested in philology for its own sake.

## Jung's Engagement: A Timeline

- **1919**: Jung's own account (in a 1934 letter) dates his I Ching studies to this year, first introduced on a journey to visit Scottish psychiatrist Maurice Nicoll.
- **January 7, 1921**: Jung paints his first yin-yang glyph.
- **November 1920**: First meeting with [[Richard-Wilhelm|Richard Wilhelm]].
- **December 1921**: Jung's appointment-book notes on hexagrams begin; he invites Wilhelm to give a club seminar on I Ching divination (Dec 15, 1921). Wilhelm's German translation was not yet published, so Jung worked from James Legge's 1899 edition through 1924 — and was frankly unimpressed with it, noting Legge apparently never "bothered to put the method to a practical test" (Jung, 1950/1975, ¶965, note 2).
- **1924**: Wilhelm's German translation is published; Jung's engagement deepens accordingly, since — unlike Legge — Wilhelm wrote from direct practical experience with Chinese diviners.
- **1950**: Jung writes the foreword to the English translation (Wilhelm & Baynes, 1950/1977), the single richest text for his mature reflections on the method.

An early episode that frames Jung's whole relationship to the I Ching: in the mid-1930s (likely at Harvard's 300th-anniversary celebration, 1936) Jung asked the modernizing Chinese philosopher [[Hu-Shih|Hu Shih]] — John Dewey's student — for his opinion of the I Ching. Hu called it "nothing but an old collection of magic spells, without significance," then admitted, when pressed, that he had in fact consulted the oracle and gotten a sensible answer. Jung recorded his disappointment decades later: "In China, for instance, a philosopher like Hu Shih is ashamed to know anything of the I Ching, the profound significance of Tao has got lost, and instead people worship locomotives and aeroplanes" (Jung, 1976/1990, p. 322). The irony Zeng draws out: the Chinese scholar embraced Western-style modernization of Chinese thought (treating the I Ching purely as a logic text, discrediting its divinatory/practical side), while the European psychologist insisted on preserving the traditional, practical, divinatory dimension. See [[Hu-Shih]].

## The Three Dimensions of Jung's Practical Use

Zeng's central analytic contribution is a taxonomy of Jung's own practice, developed primarily from Jung's 1950 foreword to the I Ching (Jung, 1950/1975, ¶¶965-1014):

### 1. Physical Dimension — previewing future potentials of outer reality

Jung consulted the I Ching about his 1925 journey to Africa and threw hexagram 53 (Gradual Process), line 3: "the man goes forth and does not return." Some fellow passengers who planned to settle in Africa in fact died within months of arrival ([[Barbara-Hannah|Barbara Hannah]]'s biography, 1976, p. 166). Wilhelm's commentary on the same line ("If we do not let things develop quietly but plunge of our own choice too rashly into a struggle, misfortune results") also matched Jung's actual near-disaster crossing Uganda on foot against colonial officials' advice, rescued at the last moment by a passing truck (Bair, 2003, p. 352). A second case, recorded by [[Aniela-Jaffe|Aniela Jaffé]] from Jung's own German memoir: Jung asked Wilhelm to cast a hexagram on the state of the Psychological Club (a situation Jung knew and Wilhelm did not) — "the diagnosis that resulted was startlingly correct, and so was the prognosis" (Jung, as cited in Jaffé, 1989, p. 27).

### 2. Psychological Dimension — revealing one's own psychological state

Jung: "When I throw a handful of peas, they will roll in all directions. Try to interpret this, and you will understand the importance of the moment... you will see how remarkably well the meaning of the I Ching matches the psychological situation" (Jung, 1987/2008, p. 205). Clinically, he noted the trigram **K'an** (water/the abyss) "turned up with patients who were too much under the sway of the unconscious... and hence threatened with the possible occurrence of psychotic phenomena" (¶1005). Writing the 1950 foreword itself, Jung cast a hexagram and got K'an doubled — "abyss on abyss" — and read it as an accurate description of his own caution in writing about a subject prone to "limitless and uncritical speculation" (¶1003).

### 3. Psychical Dimension — the I Ching as an "animated being"

The most theoretically loaded dimension, and Jung's own addition beyond the Chinese textual tradition. Jung: "It is 'spiritual agencies,' acting in a mysterious way, that make the yarrow-stalks give a meaningful answer. These powers form, as it were, the living soul of the book" (¶975) — invoking the Chinese concept of ***shen*** (神, "spirit-like things," per James Legge's rendering). Jung illustrates with his own consultation on whether to publish the English translation at all: he drew hexagram 50, the **Cauldron** (*ding*, 鼎), and read it as the book describing itself as a vessel offering "(spiritual) nourishment" — "a cult utensil serving to provide spiritual nourishment for the unconscious elements or forces... that have been projected as gods" (¶982). Jung explicitly ties this to the etymology of *religio* itself — see [[religio]] and [[relegere]] — arguing that this careful, attentive regard for the numinous *is* the original meaning of religion. Zeng flags this as the dimension where Jung's own theorizing is most likely to have conflated Chinese cosmology with Jungian psychology rather than faithfully representing the former.

## Contemporary Jungian Clinical Practice

### [[Jean-Kirsch|Jean Kirsch]]'s Countertransference Case

A single worked case (a client's plan to leave for Jungian training in Zurich, triggering Kirsch's own unrecognized desire for his growth) that Zeng argues demonstrates *all three* of Jung's dimensions at once: psychical (treating the I Ching as an animated interlocutor she can address directly — "Respond, please, I Ching"), psychological (Wilhelm's commentary on hexagram 14, Dayou/"Possession in Great Measure," revealing her countertransference), and physical (the client did in fact go on to complete his Jungian training). See [[Jean-Kirsch]] and [[I Ching Divination]].

### [[Mengchao-Li|Mengchao Li]] and the Eight Heart Methods

Li, a Chinese Jungian-and-Kleinian psychiatrist, applies a psychologized version (developed by Heyong Shen, "the first Chinese Jungian analyst") of the **Eight Heart Methods of the I Ching** — a Yuan-dynasty interpretive system originated by the scholar Ba Bao — to therapeutic transference/countertransference material. See [[Eight Heart Methods of the I Ching]] for the full six-stage method and Li's worked case (a client's maternal projection, hexagram 2 Kun changing into hexagram 7 Shih).

### [[Dennis-Merritt|Dennis Merritt]]'s Two Clinical Formats

Merritt, researching the I Ching in analytic practice since around 1983, developed a three-hour single-consultation format (clarify the real question → yarrow-stalk casting as itself meditative/therapeutic → read across three English translations) and an ongoing format for I-Ching-literate clients (divination between sessions, brought back for discussion). His worked case: a young man's marijuana use, a dream of a stillborn baby delivered by a stoned doctor, and hexagram 12 (Stagnation) read as "getting high" as an escape from depressive feelings — the client quit smoking within four months. Merritt also links the Chinese holistic worldview to Jung's [[Unus Mundus]] and physicist David Bohm's implicate order. See [[Dennis-Merritt]].

## Zeng's Critical Reflections

Zeng does not simply celebrate this lineage of practice; each case draws a specific critique:

- **Kirsch**: unclear whether her quotations are from the original I Ching text or Wilhelm's commentary; conflates the Chinese *junzi* (君子, conventionally "superior man") with the Jungian archetype of the wise old man/sage — a translation slippage, since *junzi* does not carry the connotation of "superior" in Chinese.
- **Li**: creative and systematic, but blends Kleinian (projection, positions), Jungian (Great Mother, hero), and purely Confucian (moral-cultivation) elements from the Eight Heart Methods without marking the boundaries between them.
- **Merritt**: claims the I Ching "originates from the archetypal depths of the human psyche and the psychoid dimensions of the Self" (2001, p. 55) — a psychologizing move Zeng finds philosophically questionable against Yew Wai Ko's critical study *Jung on Synchronicity and Yijing* (2011), which argues the I Ching's native worldview "does not assume any transcendental realm of time" the way Merritt's framing implies. Merritt's *A Guide to the I Ching* source-text is separately noted as over-translating "inferior people" into Western therapeutic language ("anxieties," "fears").

Zeng's own verdict: the I Ching has genuine potential as a Jungian clinical tool, precisely because Jung himself modeled decades of serious, non-dogmatic practical use across all three dimensions — but "numerous issues of intercultural communication must be clarified to prevent misunderstandings" before it can be adopted responsibly.
