# The Origins and History of Consciousness — Erich Neumann (1949/1954)

Neumann's magnum opus. A comprehensive developmental psychology of consciousness framed through comparative mythology. Jung wrote the foreword. The thesis: the stages by which humanity achieved differentiated consciousness — traced in world mythology — are recapitulated by every individual ego in its development from unconscious merger to reflective selfhood. The phylogenetic and ontogenetic series are structurally identical.

Originally: *Ursprungsgeschichte des Bewusstseins* (Rascher Verlag, Zurich, 1949). English translation by R.F.C. Hull, 1954, Princeton/Bollingen.

---

## The Central Argument

Consciousness did not always exist in its present form. It had to be wrested from the unconscious through a long historical process that is simultaneously a personal developmental process. Neumann reads world mythology as the symbolic projection of this history. Every mythological sequence — creation myths, hero myths, transformation myths — encodes a stage in the evolution of human awareness.

The method is **stadial**: he identifies discrete stages of consciousness, each with its characteristic mythology, psychological structure, and symbolic figures. The stages are sequential but also archetypal — the later stages do not abolish the earlier but encompass them.

Key principle (explicit in the text): "The ontogenetic development of the individual repeats, in abbreviated and compressed form, the phylogenetic sequence of humanity's psychological history."

## Part A: Creation Mythology — The Stages Before the Hero

### Stage 1: The Uroboros

The primordial state. The world-serpent biting its own tail. Before any differentiation, all is one: conscious and unconscious are undivided; inside and outside are the same; self and world are merged. The uroboros is the primordial symbol of this pre-ego totality.

Key passages extracted:
- "The uroboros is properly called the 'tail-eater'" — self-sufficient, feeding on itself
- "The world is experienced as all-embracing" — in this stage there is no differentiated observer
- "The uroboros of the maternal world is life and psyche in one"
- "The uroboros arches over man's life" — it returns at the end, as the mystical dissolution sought in old age or at death

The uroboros is hermaphroditic (neither fully male nor female), presexual, and pre-individual. It has no inside/outside distinction. It is the oceanic state before birth as a subject.

**Psychological analog**: The infant's pre-ego state; states of deep sleep, absorption, or mystical dissolution in adults; the unconscious itself as a background condition.

### Stage 2: The Great Mother

The first differentiation out of the uroboros is not yet the ego — it is the Great Mother. As the unconscious begins to consolidate around the figure of the nurturing/devouring matrix, the maternal archetype dominates the psyche.

The Great Mother is double-faced:
- **Positive pole**: the nourishing, life-giving, containing earth mother — Isis, Demeter, Cybele, the Virgin Mary as protectress
- **Negative pole**: the devouring, castrating, deadly mother — Kali, Hecate, Medusa, Tiamat, the Terrible Mother

She is not a person but a structural reality of the psyche at this stage. The ego, barely forming, is wholly under her dominion. Her law is that everything born of her must return to her.

Key passages:
- "The Great Mother seems to be embodied in this stage of the uroboros"
- "The Great Mother takes the little child back into herself"
- "Matriarchal castration impends over an ego that has not yet broken its tie with the Great Mother"
- "Ego development leads to a stage in which the Great Mother no longer appears as friendly and good"

The matriarchal stage of mythology belongs here: mother goddesses, vegetation gods who die and are reborn, sacred marriages (hieros gamos) in which the male is the consort and servant of the goddess. Attis, Adonis, Osiris in his earliest form.

### Stage 3: The Separation of the World Parents

The first specifically heroic act: separating Heaven (Father) from Earth (Mother). The hero pries apart the merged World Parents and creates the space between them — the human world, light, differentiated existence.

This is the mythological expression of the principle of opposites. Before separation there is only the undifferentiated pleroma. After separation: light/dark, above/below, male/female, conscious/unconscious. Consciousness requires this polarity.

Examples: Maori Rangi (sky) and Papa (earth); Geb and Nut in Egypt; Heaven and Earth in Chinese cosmology. Marduk cleaving Tiamat.

## Part B: The Hero Myth — Stages of Ego Development

### Stage 4: The Birth of the Hero

The hero represents the ego-germ differentiating from the maternal matrix. His birth is always extraordinary: a divine father, a virgin mother, a miraculous or perilous circumstance of birth. This signals that the hero belongs to two worlds — the mortal (maternal) and the divine (transpersonal).

Key passages:
- "With the hero myth we enter upon a new phase of stadial development"
- "The hero is the archetypal forerunner of mankind in general"
- "The myths of the hero myth have become constituent elements in the personal development of every individual"
- "The hero's life completely detached from the Great Mother"

The hero has two fathers or two mothers as a central feature — one human, one divine. This dual nature is the seed of self-consciousness: the hero knows himself to be more than the mere child of the maternal world.

### Stage 5: The Dragon Fight (Slaying of the Mother)

The central mythological act of ego development. The hero faces the dragon — which is the uroboros, the Great Mother, the devouring unconscious — and must overcome it without being swallowed.

Key passages:
- "The dragon fight has three main components: the hero, the dragon to be conquered, and the captive (the treasure)"
- "The dragon fight forms a central chapter in the evolution of mankind as of the individual"
- "The dragon fight is the sun myth" — daily rebirth of solar consciousness from the darkness
- "The hero and the beast that kills him are very often identical" — the dragon and the hero share a root; the dragon is what the hero might become if swallowed back into the unconscious
- "The dragon fight is a transfiguration"
- "The dragon fight includes a change in his relation to the female"
- "The dragon fight of the first period begins with the encounter with the unconscious and ends with the heroic birth of the ego"
- "The dragon fight which brings infancy to a close"

The dragon fight is not merely an external combat. It is the ego's encounter with its own unconscious roots — the pull back to the uroboric state, to dissolution, to undifferentiation. The hero must resist it and yet not be cut off from it entirely. What he wins is not the destruction of the unconscious but its transformation: the captive (anima, treasure, new energy) is liberated.

### Stage 6: The Slaying of the Father

After winning against the devouring maternal dragon, the hero must also overcome the Father — the established patriarchal authority, the law, the old order. The Oedipal struggle in its positive form: not incest but emancipation.

The hero kills (or supersedes) the father not from hate but because development requires it. The son must become the father's equal or successor, not remain subordinate. This marks the emergence of genuine individual consciousness — no longer defined by either the maternal matrix or the paternal law, but self-grounding.

## Part C: The Transformation Myth

### Stage 7: The Captive and the Treasure

After the dragon fight, what was captive is liberated. The psychological content: the anima figure (the inner feminine), the treasure, the wisdom hidden in the unconscious. The hero's transformation is complete when he integrates what the dragon was guarding.

Two types of hero-treasure: extraverted (the captive princess) and introverted (wisdom, the grail, the philosopher's stone).

### Stage 8: Transformation — The Synthesis

The culminating stage: integration of the opposites that were separated in the earlier acts. The union of masculine (ego-consciousness, discrimination, logos) and feminine (the unconscious, eros, wholeness) in a higher synthesis. This is what Jung calls the hieros gamos at the psychic level — not marriage to another person but the internal sacred marriage of the differentiated ego with the re-encountered depths.

Neumann maps this onto the individuation process as Jung defines it: the goal is not merely a strong ego but a self-regulating psyche in which conscious and unconscious are in dialogue and mutually enriching.

## Part Two: The Psychological Stages of Personality Development

The second half of the book is more technical: Neumann applies the mythological sequence to the psychology of individual development — infancy, childhood, puberty, maturity, the second half of life.

Key concept: **centroversion** — the innate tendency of the psyche toward wholeness and self-regulation. Centroversion is the organizing principle behind all the stages; it works unconsciously at first, then more consciously as the ego differentiates.

Key passages:
- "Centroversion is the innate tendency of a whole to create unity within its parts and to synthesize their differences in unified systems"
- "Centroversion promotes the formation of ego consciousness as its specific organ"
- "Centroversion works unconsciously" in the early stages
- "The ego development is replaced by the development of the self" — at the culmination of the individuation process

Stages mapped to life phases:
- **Uroboric phase**: infancy, pre-verbal, pre-individual
- **Matriarchal phase**: early childhood, mother-dominated
- **Dragon fight (first)**: puberty and early adulthood — the break from the parental world, formation of individual identity
- **Dragon fight (second)**: midlife — the encounter with the unconscious as shadow, anima, the Self. What Jung calls the second half of life's work.
- **Integration**: old age or mature individuation — the ego in relation to the Self, not dominating or surrendering to it

The uroboros symbol returns at the end: "the uroboros appears as 'the ocean of Godhead'" — the dissolution sought in mystical experience, old age, death. Not regression but completion.

## Key Relationships to Jung

- Jung wrote the foreword and endorsed the book as a major advance in analytical psychology
- Neumann extends Jung's framework systematically: where Jung identified the archetypes and their patterns, Neumann maps their developmental sequence
- Jung's concept of the individuation process = Neumann's full mythological sequence
- Jung's Hero Archetype (CW 5) is Stage 5 in Neumann's schema
- Neumann adds explicit developmental stages that Jung's work implied but did not systematize
- The "second dragon fight" in midlife corresponds to Jung's second-half-of-life individuation work (see [[Individuation]], [[Hero Archetype]])

## Scholarly Context

- Published the same year as Jung's *Aion* (1951 German publication overlaps)
- Neumann was Jung's closest theoretical heir; died 1960 at age 55
- *The Great Mother* (1955) is the companion volume focusing entirely on the feminine archetypal principle
- The ontogeny/phylogeny argument has been criticized as too speculative historically, but remains valuable as psychological description
- The book's influence: James Hillman, Joseph Campbell (*Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949 — same year, complementary), Marion Woodman, Robert Moore
