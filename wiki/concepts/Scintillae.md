# Scintillae

**Latin**: *scintillae* — "sparks." Also called *oculi piscium* ("fishes' eyes") in the alchemical literature.

The alchemical name for the **multiple, scattered luminosities** that the alchemists believed to be latent within the *prima materia* — small autonomous points of light or consciousness shining out of the darkness of unworked matter, distinct from (and prior to) the single central light known as the *sol invisibilis* or Monas. See [[Lumen-Naturae]] for that singular, central form; this page treats the *plural* form.

---

## The Alchemical Sources

Heinrich Khunrath describes the scintillae as rays or sparks of the *Anima Catholica* — the universal soul, identical with the Spirit of God — dispersed throughout "the huge mass of all matter in the first lump," now scattered into the disjunct parts of the world but still constituting "the sparks of *one* universal world soul" (*Amphitheatrum*, 1604). He adds that the human mind itself is one of these sparks: *"Mens humani animi scintilla altior et lucidior"* ("the mind of the human spirit is a higher and more lucid spark").

The alchemists called these seeds of light in the chaos "sparks of the fire of the soul of the world, as pure *Formae rerum essentiales*" (essential ideal forms of things). Gerhard Dorn held to a doctrine of scintillae perceived by the "spiritual eyes," shining resplendently. Paracelsus's *lumen naturale* is based on a related conception — the light innate even in animals (their *"luminositas sensus naturae,"* per Agrippa von Nettesheim).

## Jung's Psychological Reading

Jung reads the scintillae as pre-scientific descriptions of the **archetypes of the collective unconscious**, which "must accordingly possess a certain inherent luminosity or autonomous latent element of consciousness" (*"On the Nature of the Psyche,"* §6, "The Unconscious as a Multiple Consciousness").

At the primitive level, he argues, consciousness is not yet a unity centered on a firmly structured ego-complex; it "flares up here and there, wherever outer or inner experiences, instincts, and affects call it up." Even the developed ego-complex should be thought of as surrounded by many small luminosities — a claim Jung supports from both the dreams of modern people and alchemical symbolism. These are the seedlike luminosities of the unconscious's contents, shining forth out of the darkness before they cohere into anything nameable.

> "Since consciousness is characterized from of old by expressions taken from the manifestations of light, the hypothesis that the multiple luminosities correspond to small phenomena of consciousness is not, in my view, too far-fetched. If the luminosity should appear as monadic, as a single star for instance, or as an eye, then it easily takes on mandala formation and is then to be interpreted as the Self." — Jung, "On the Nature of the Psyche"

The distinction matters: **many scintillae** = an early, pre-integrated field of small autonomous conscious points (a plurality); **one central light** (Monas, *sol invisibilis*) = the achieved, unified symbol of the [[Self (archetype)]], which typically appears in [[Mandala Symbolism|mandala]] form. Multiplicity precedes and yields to unity in the individuation process.

## In von Franz's Reading of Descartes

Von Franz ([[vonfranz-dreams-1998]]) applies this directly to the second of [[Rene-Descartes]]'s three 1619 dreams: the thunderclap and the "multitude of sparks" glowing in the room. She connects these fiery sparks to the melon of the first dream — a Manichaean "light-germ receptacle" that has now "burst open," releasing the luminosities directly before Descartes's eyes. Like the ghosts and tempest of the first dream, the sparks symbolize emotionally charged archetypal contents not yet contained within either the framework of the Church or Descartes's own intellectual conception of the world.

She reads Descartes's *lumen naturale* — his belief in a single, central "natural light" of reason from which all *semina scientiae* (seeds of science) and *veritates aeternae* (eternal truths) are drawn — as his *conscious, philosophical* translation of an experience that was, in its raw psychic form, plural and scattered (the many sparks), not yet the single unified Monas his rationalism required it to be. Descartes shuts and opens his eyes until the fear and the phenomenon both vanish; he rationalizes the plurality away rather than sitting with it, the same move that recurs across all three dreams (see [[Rene-Descartes]] §"The Multiplication of the Personality").
