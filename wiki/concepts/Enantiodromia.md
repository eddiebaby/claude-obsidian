# Enantiodromia

Jung's term, borrowed from Heraclitus, for the psychological law that a psychic attitude or value, pushed far enough in one direction, eventually flips into its opposite: "everything eventually changes into its opposite" (*Psychological Types* ¶112). Named directly in [[qwarnstrom2026-onesidedness-coriolanus-timon|Sofie Qwarnström's 2026 essay]] as the mechanism completing the arc of [[One-Sidedness]] — the general vault entry point for how this state builds.

## Mechanism

As one function or attitude is developed to dominance ([[Superior Function]]), its opposite ([[Inferior Function]]) sinks into the unconscious and accumulates unlived, compensatory pressure — "form[ing] a counterweight to the conscious orientation." The more extreme the conscious one-sidedness, the more this unconscious counter-position grows in force. Beyond a threshold the tension becomes unsustainable and the personality "flips" — enantiodromia — into a state markedly dissonant from its habitual style: "that which was formerly valued becomes worthless, and that which was hitherto thought good comes to be seen as bad" (¶453).

Jung frames this as a **fundamental psychological law**, a direct consequence of the psyche's self-regulating tendency toward balance — the same self-regulating principle behind [[Dream Compensation]], operating here at the level of a whole conscious attitude rather than a single dream.

## What Happens If the Flip Is Not Consciously Met

If the opposite attitude is never made conscious — never deliberately integrated — the flip does not stay internal. Jung: "when an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside, as fate... the world must perforce act out the conflict and be torn into opposing halves." Projection amplifies this: demeaning one's own repressed opposite onto another person or group reinforces both the dislike and one's own pull toward becoming more extreme oneself — a feedback loop that, left unaddressed, ends in breakdown, at which point enantiodromia occurs anyway, just without any conscious hand on it.

## Literary Case

Both protagonists in [[qwarnstrom2026-onesidedness-coriolanus-timon|Qwarnström's essay]] enact the unmet version of this flip. **Coriolanus** — rigid loyalist to Rome and to his own principle — turns against Rome itself once banished, and dies attempting the very destruction his whole prior identity was organized against. **Timon of Athens** — boundless, undiscriminating love for his fellow Athenians — flips to cursing "the gods, mankind, and life itself" and takes his own life once betrayed. In both cases the same function that defined the hero (Introverted Thinking's rigid principle; Extraverted Feeling's boundless trust) simply reverses polarity rather than integrating its opposite — enantiodromia as catastrophe, not growth, because neither hero ever consciously met his inferior function first.

## The Difference Between Enantiodromia and Individuation

Enantiodromia is not itself individuation — it is what happens *instead of* individuation when [[One-Sidedness]] is never consciously addressed. Individuation's version of the same movement is the deliberate, "mixtum compositum" cession of ground von Franz describes (see [[One-Sidedness]]) — an uncomfortable but chosen transitional integration, rather than an involuntary, often destructive flip. The presence of enantiodromia in a life or a dream series is diagnostic: it names exactly where one-sidedness has gone unmet for too long.
