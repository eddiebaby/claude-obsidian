# Rose Symbolism (Alchemical)

The **rose** — and specifically **rose-coloured blood** (*sanguis rosaceus*) — is alchemy's central image for the red tincture: the healing, whole-making effect of a particular kind of Eros, and the symbolic bridge by which the medieval mystique of the Rose (Mary, the Rosarium, courtly love) entered the alchemical tradition. Jung reads the rose, like its Eastern equivalent the lotus, as a form of **mandala** — a feminine totality-symbol standing beside the masculine cross/quaternity.

Source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]], Essay V, "The Philosophical Tree," §7, "The Rose-Coloured Blood and the Rose" (pars. 383–391). Discussed within [[Philosophical-Tree]].

---

## The Rose-Coloured Blood

Khunrath describes a "lion lured forth from the Saturnine mountain" possessing rose-coloured blood — the lion, "all and conquering all," corresponds to Zosimos's πᾶν (the whole, totality). Khunrath elsewhere names the "costly Catholick Rosy-Coloured Blood and Aetheric Water that flows forth Azothically from the side of the innate Son of the Great World" — by which all vegetable, animal, and mineral things are raised to their highest natural perfection.

The **"Aquarium sapientum"** correlates the *filius macrocosmi* (the lapis, "son of the great world") with **Christ as *filius microcosmi***, whose blood is the quintessence, the red tincture: "the true and authentic duplex Mercurius... the sole and perfect Healer of all imperfect bodies and men, the true and heavenly physician of the soul."

> [!key-insight] Not bad taste, but serious religious experience (par. 385)
> Jung insists these panegyrics were never intended to exalt the stone into a world-saviour by conscious design — the alchemists "simply found these qualities in their idea of a body composed of the four elements and capable of uniting all opposites, and were just as amazed at this discovery as anyone would be who had a singularly impressive dream and then came across an unknown myth which fitted it exactly." The stone was, for them, "nothing less than a **primordial religious experience** which, as good Christians, they had to reconcile with their beliefs" — the same *lapis-Christ* ambiguity treated in Essay II. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]].

## Dorn's *Putissimus Homo*

Because the stone represents the *homo totus*, and its soul resides in its blood, Dorn speaks of the arcane substance's bloody sweat as belonging to the ***putissimus homo*** — the "most pure man," entirely human, unadulterated by outside influence, who "will appear on earth only in the last days." This figure **cannot be Christ**, who has already redeemed the world by his blood; Christ is *purissimus* but not *putissimus* (pure silver, but also gold — not unalloyed). The alchemical figure is instead the ***servator cosmi*** (preserver of the cosmos) — the still-unconscious idea of the whole and complete man who will finish what Christ's sacrificial death left undone. Like Christ he sweats a redeeming blood, but "rose-coloured": not natural blood, but **"symbolic blood, a psychic substance, the manifestation of a certain kind of Eros"** that unifies the individual and the multitude and is therefore a panacea.

## Mechthild of Magdeburg and the Rose as Mandala

Mechthild of Magdeburg records the Lord showing her his heart: "a most beautiful rose with five petals covered his whole breast," and saying: "Praise me in my five senses, which are indicated by this rose." Jung reads the rose here, alongside the *hortus conclusus* and *rosa mystica*, as an **allegory of Mary** in the spiritual sense and of the beloved (the poets' *"fedeli d'amore"*) in the worldly sense — Mary allegorized elsewhere as the *medium terrae* (St. Bernard), the "city" (Rabanus Maurus), the "fortress" and "house of divine wisdom" (Godfrey of Admont), the *acies castrorum* (Alan of Lille). "So the rose has the significance of a **mandala**, as is clear from the heavenly rose in Dante's *Paradiso*." Like its equivalent the Indian lotus, the rose is decidedly feminine; in Mechthild's vision it functions as **a projection of her own feminine Eros upon Christ**.

## Love Alone Is Not Enough (par. 391)

Jung's closing judgment ties the rose mystique directly to the Rosicrucian motto ***per crucem ad rosam*** (through the cross to the rose) and to Goethe's poem "Die Geheimnisse" — and generalizes it into a psychological principle:

> "Love alone is useless if it does not also have understanding... The blinder love is, the more it is instinctual, and the more it is attended by destructive consequences, for it is a dynamism that needs form and direction. Therefore **a compensatory Logos has been joined to it as a light that shines in the darkness**."

Rosicrucianism and the Christian ideal of charity both compensate a real social defect (cruel, unreliable law; feudal disregard for human dignity), but "solicitude for the spiritual welfare of the erring sheep can explain even a Torquemada" — love without insight is blind and dangerous. Christianity's deeper cultural achievement, in Jung's reading, was not simply commanding love of neighbour but **educating man to a higher consciousness and responsibility** — a love combined with understanding, illuminating what is still dark both in the outside world and within the psyche.

## Rose vs. Cross: Two Halves of One Mandala

Read alongside [[Tetrasomia]] and the cross-as-quaternity material from the same essay, the rose completes the picture: where the **cross** is the masculine, differentiated, quaternary symbol of wholeness (four arms reaching the four quarters), the **rose** is its feminine counterpart — undifferentiated, circular, Eros-centered. *Per crucem ad rosam* names the whole coniunctio in miniature: the union of the two symbolic registers is the goal, not the triumph of either alone.
