# Spiritus Vegetativus

**Spiritus vegetativus** ("vegetative spirit") is the animating principle of plant life — the specific life-force of growing things. In Jung's reading of Essay IV, it becomes the alchemical name for the [[principium individuationis]] as it is hidden in the roots of the tree: the Spirit in the Bottle is the *spiritus vegetativus* of the great oak.

Primary CW 13 source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay IV §1, par. 243.

---

## The Term's Background

In Aristotelian-Scholastic psychology, the *spiritus vegetativus* (or *anima vegetativa*) is the lowest of the three levels of soul — the principle of growth, nutrition, and reproduction that plants and all living things share. It has no sensation, no movement, no reason; it simply *grows*. In the alchemical and Paracelsian tradition, the *vegetativus* spirit was also identified with the *spiritus mundi* as it expresses itself in nature's growing things — the invisible life-force behind the visible plant.

---

## Jung's Use: Spirit in the Tree's Roots (par. 243)

The Spirit in the Bottle (Grimm fairytale) is imprisoned not in the tree itself but **under its roots** — a detail Jung treats as precise:

> "The spirit = *spiritus vegetativus* of the tree = **'*principium individuationis*'** — 'the spiritual quintessence abstracted from it.' 'The tree would then be the outward and visible sign of the realization of the self.'"

The identification is:
- *Spiritus vegetativus* of the oak = the living principle of the tree's growth
- = *Principium individuationis* = the ordering intelligence of the psyche
- = The Self as process of individuation in visible form

The secret is hidden in the **roots**, not the crown. The vegetative spirit is the Self at its most unconscious, most instinctual, most bodily level — not in the heights of spirit but in the earthward-going roots of the organism.

---

## The Two Directions: Roots and Branches

The opposition between roots and crown is developed further in Essay V on the [[Philosophical-Tree]] (par. 333):

> "A person whose roots are above as well as below is thus like a tree growing simultaneously downwards and upwards. The goal is neither height nor depth, but the **centre**."

The *spiritus vegetativus* designates the downward-growing principle — the roots that anchor in body, instinct, matter. It is the aspect of the Self that is not airy or spiritual but heavy, earthy, hidden underground.

This is precisely what gets suppressed in the Christian or rationalist worldview: the body, the instinct, the dark vegetative life is declared "lower" and relegated to the unconscious, where it becomes the imprisoned spirit awaiting liberation.

---

## Connection to Paracelsus's *Spiritus Seminalis*

In Essay III (Paracelsus), the *spiritus vegetativus* appears as *spiritus seminalis* — the seed-spirit, the generative principle. The *Iliaster* = life-substance = *spiritus mercurii* contains this seed-spirit as its driving force. Mercurius as *anima media natura* ("spirit and soul of the bodies," par. 260) also carries the *spiritus vegetativus* in his aerial aspect: originally a wind god (Hermes) and an Egyptian breath-soul (Thoth "makes the souls to breathe").

The trajectory from Paracelsus to the alchemical Mercurius to the Spirit in the Bottle traces the same concept across different frameworks: the basic animating life-principle that precedes and underlies consciousness, hidden at the root level of the organism.

---

## Psychological Significance

For Jung, the *spiritus vegetativus* is psychologically significant because it locates the Self's deepest root not in spirit (above) but in the body (below). The individuating person must descend to the roots — enter the vegetative level — to encounter the *principium individuationis* in its unmediated form. This is the logic of the Nekyia (night-sea journey), the descent to the underworld, the encounter with what is most primitive and instinctual.

The oak's *spiritus vegetativus* drives the tree toward the light from its roots in the dark. Psychologically: the individuation drive (*principium individuationis*) arises from the body and the instincts, not from the conscious spiritual striving of the ego.
