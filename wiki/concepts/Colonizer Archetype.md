# Colonizer Archetype

The colonizer archetype is [[Divine Charura|Charura]] and [[Stephen Bushell|Bushell]]'s (2023) name for the deep psychic pattern that manifests in all forms of oppression and inequality, focused on the polarized opposites **colonizer – colonized**. Historical colonialism is a manifestation of this archetype, not the archetype itself — the pattern is structural and pre-exists any particular historical instance of conquest.

Source: [[JOAP-68-1-Charura-Bushell-Duoethnographic-Colonialism]] (*JAP* 68/1, 2023, pp. 27–47).

---

## Definition

"By 'the colonizer archetype', we name a pattern that manifests in all forms of oppression and inequality, and we focus on the polarized opposites of colonizer – colonized, which, we argue, seems to be shifting by way of increasing consciousness of the shame and pain in the colonized and colonizer." Archetypes generally are "understood to be the deep patterns of the collective unconscious that shape perception and so shape the world we perceive" — the colonizer archetype is this general definition applied specifically to domination/subjugation dynamics. See [[Archetype]] for the base theory.

---

## The Self as Archetype of Diversity

The authors ground the colonizer archetype in a reading of the [[Self (archetype)|Self]] as "the archetype of diversity": the Self, as totality and unity of all opposites, "contains the potentiality for all forms of human cultural expression, and within this the tendency for oppositional positions to polarize." Polarization (e.g. black–white) is not itself pathological — Jung (1916/1957) saw it as necessary for activating the [[transcendent function]], the innate capacity for opposites to be reconciled through symbolic images arising from the unconscious. The colonizer archetype is what happens when that polarization calcifies into domination instead of moving toward reconciliation.

---

## How It Operates Intrapsychically

The paper extends the archetype past the social-historical register into the structure of the individual psyche:

- **Ego formation as a colonizing act.** When an infant separates from the mother, "the activation of the colonizing archetype provides psychic energy for the strengthening of this separation, such that the sense of being a separate and distinct 'I' comes to colonize the internal space of consciousness." Colonizing is framed as "a force within nature," which is why it is so pervasive.
- **Complexes vying for dominance.** Jung's Word Association Test (1909, CW 2) showed that stimulus words provoke complexes attempting to enter consciousness. The authors read this as complexes "vying to move into a more dominant (colonizing) position" — observable in any concentration exercise (e.g. holding attention on a candle, where intruding thoughts "invade/colonize" the held space). Meditation practice is "initially about noticing this colonizing phenomenon."
- **The ego itself as a complex.** Following Jung (1921, CW 6), the ego is not a reified thing but a complex that organizes conscious perception through the four functions (sensation, intuition, thinking, feeling).
- **Psychosis as the limit case.** When an archetypally-driven complex fully determines speech and behaviour, "consciousness has become colonized by the activated archetypes."

## In the Consulting Room

Because the client is met as an "other," the authors argue *ipso facto* the colonizing archetype is prone to constellate in the therapeutic dyad, regardless of conscious intent. Named manifestations:

- Implicitly demanding the client see their intrapsychic world through the therapist's modality/language
- Holding onto interpretations that fail to resonate
- Interrupting, invading the client's narrative space
- Fixing the client into ready-made positions ("he is caught in the mother complex")
- Supervisor/supervisee dynamics that impose interpretive narratives on client material instead of working from the shared imaginal field (see [[mundus imaginalis]])

**Clinical task**: the moment a negative thought or feeling about a client arises, the analyst must track its origin and how far it is being projected. This alerts the analyst to a possible colonizer–colonized enactment. The therapeutic art is to "hold the poison, know it and yet not use it to harm the other" — recognizing it as psychic energy toned by the archetype, and placing that energy in the **inner *vas* (vessel)** of conscious awareness, where it breaks down to its original energetic component. This liberates the energy from the archetype's dominating patterning — an explicitly alchemical transformation image (cf. [[Vessel-Symbol]]).

---

## Emancipatory Encounter

Healing requires the colonizer archetype to *emerge and become conscious* — it cannot be bypassed. When the pain held by the colonized meets the shame held by the colonizer, each is found to conceal the other: "within the colonizer's shame dwells a colossal pain of blind inter-generational wounding... in the pain of the colonized, lies the inter-generational shame of the loss of the dignity of one's life and culture." Recognizing this shared substrate — "we each hold something of the other" — is "the gift of the transcendent function." The authors flag a residual risk: treating the encountered other as "a commodity to enhance our own growth" is "another kind of colonialism."

## Relation to Ethnic Shadow

The colonizer archetype is the structural *pattern* (colonizer–colonized polarity); the [[Ethnic Shadow]] is the specific *content* — a culture's or individual's split-off material about race and domination — that the pattern organizes and that must be faced for the archetype to be worked through rather than acted out. See [[Ethnic Shadow]] for the shadow-side treatment.
