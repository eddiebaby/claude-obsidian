# Self (archetype)

The Self is Jung's term for the totality of the psyche - the whole of which the ego is only a part. It is both the center and the circumference of the personality, encompassing both conscious and unconscious. The Self is the archetype of wholeness and order, the organizing principle behind the psyche's movement toward integration in [[Individuation]].

Primary source: [[jung-cw9ii-aion]] (Ch IV, paras 43-67, directly read). Also treated in [[jung-cw9i-archetypes-collective-unconscious]] (Mandala Symbolism essay, individuation).

---

## The Ego-Self Distinction

The foundational distinction in Aion (Ch I, directly read):

- **Ego**: The complex factor to which all conscious contents are related. The center of the field of consciousness; the subject of all personal acts of consciousness. Never more nor less than consciousness as a whole (para 7).
- **Self**: "The total personality which, though present, cannot be fully known" (para 9). It includes the ego as a part includes the whole; the ego is "subordinate to the self and is related to it like a part to the whole."

The Self is always partly unconscious. Because the unconscious cannot be fully known by consciousness, the Self can never become a complete object of cognition. What we experience of the Self is always partial - glimpsed in dreams, symbols, and transformative life events.

## The Self as God-image

Jung draws a psychological equivalence between the Self and the *imago Dei* (God-image):

"Unity and totality stand at the highest point on the scale of objective values because their symbols can no longer be distinguished from the *imago Dei*. Hence all statements about the God-image apply also to the empirical symbols of totality." (para 60, directly read)

This is a psychological claim, not a theological one. Jung is not asserting that God exists or that the Self *is* God. He is observing that the spontaneous symbols produced by the psyche for its own totality (mandalas, circles, quaternities) are formally identical with the symbols cultures use for the divine. Psychologically, the experience of the Self has the character of a *numinosum* - something overwhelming, other, and absolutely compelling.

> [!note] Scope of this claim
> Jung consistently insists he speaks as a physician of the psyche, not as a theologian or metaphysician. "I write as a physician... and not as a proselyte" (Aion Foreword, directly read). The Self-God equation is a phenomenological observation about the *character* of the experience, not a statement about ultimate reality.

## Inflation and the Ego-Self Problem

Two failure modes in the relationship between ego and self (Ch IV, paras 44-47, directly read):

**Inflation (ego assimilated by self)**: "It must be reckoned a psychic catastrophe when the ego is assimilated by the self" (para 45). The image of wholeness descends into the unconscious; the ego inflates, convinced of its special significance, identification with God or some cosmic role. The symptom: growing disinclination to take note of reactions from the environment.

**Psychologism (self assimilated to ego)**: The world of consciousness is "levelled down in favour of the reality of the unconscious." The reverse inflation: consciousness is demolished to make room for the dream-state. Also requires moral defeat.

The correct relationship: the ego maintains its stable position in conscious reality, while granting the figures of the unconscious (shadow, anima/animus, self) "relative autonomy and reality" (para 44). This requires the virtues of attention, conscientiousness, and patience.

## How the Self Appears

The Self does not appear directly. It manifests through symbols:

**Mandalas**: Circular symbols of wholeness and order, occurring "principally during times of psychic disorientation or re-orientation. As magic circles they bind and subdue the lawless powers belonging to the world of darkness" (para 60, directly read). They arise spontaneously in dreams of people with no cultural knowledge of them. See [[Mandala Symbolism]].

**Quaternary structures**: The four-fold pattern is the Self's signature in symbolic material - four functions, four directions, four elements, four Evangelists, the Christian cross, the alchemical squaring of the circle. The quaternio motif appears throughout Aion's historical survey.

**The "Christ figure"**: In Aion, Jung argues that the Christ figure in Western culture historically functioned as the primary symbol of the Self. "Christ exemplifies the archetype of the self" (para 70, directly read). The Christ symbol provided 2,000 years of psychic container for Self-experience; as it loses living symbolic force, the contained psychic energy returns in other forms.

**The number four**: "Although 'wholeness' seems at first sight to be nothing but an abstract idea (like anima and animus), it is nevertheless empirical in so far as it is anticipated by the psyche in the form of spontaneous or autonomous symbols. These are the quaternary or mandala symbols" (para 59, directly read).

## The Self in the Individuation Sequence

The Self is the telos of [[Individuation]] - the goal toward which the process moves, though never fully reached:

1. Shadow integration: recognizing and assimilating the dark/inferior side of the personality
2. Anima/animus integration: withdrawing projections onto the opposite sex; making the contrasexual dimension conscious
3. Encounter with the Self: the symbol of totality that transcends and encompasses ego, shadow, and syzygy

This is not a tidy linear sequence. The Self is present throughout - it is what drives the process of individuation - but conscious encounter with it becomes possible only after substantial work on shadow and syzygy.

The marriage quaternio (para 42, directly read): the four figures (ego/persona, shadow, anima or animus, Self/Wise Old Man or Chthonic Mother) form the complete structural map of the psyche's inner cast. The Self is the fourth, the transcendent element that cannot be integrated but can be related to.

---

## The Self and the Problem of Evil

One of Aion's most important arguments: the Christian symbol of the Self is incomplete because it is *privatio boni* - evil conceived as merely the absence of good. Psychologically this fails: the shadow is real, not merely a defect. The alchemical symbol of the Self (the lapis, the round fish, the coniunctio oppositorum) explicitly unites opposites - light and dark, Christ and Antichrist - and is therefore a psychologically truer symbol of totality.

This is the bridge to the Gnostic and alchemical material in Chs XIII-XIV. Gnosticism, for all its excesses, recognized the problem of evil in the Self more honestly than mainstream Christianity.
