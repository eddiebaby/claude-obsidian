# Peircean Semiotics

[[Charles Sanders Peirce|C. S. Peirce's]] 1903 trichotomy of representational categories — icon, index, symbol — introduced to the vault as a comparison framework against Jung's sign/symbol dichotomy (see [[Sign and Symbol]]) in [[Raya Jones|Jones's]] 2023 essay.

## The Trichotomy

| Category | How it represents | Peirce's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Icon** | By virtue of its own characteristics, even if its object does not exist. "A pure icon does not draw any distinction between itself and its object. It represents whatever it may represent." | A statue of a centaur |
| **Index** | By virtue of characteristics it would not have if its object did not exist, and which hold irrespective of whether it is interpreted. | An old-fashioned hygrometer: its physical reaction to moisture persists whether or not anyone reads it — though if nobody understands the instrument, it ceases to *convey information* |
| **Symbol** | Regardless of any similarity or analogy with its object — "solely and simply because it will be interpreted to be a representamen." | Any general word, sentence, or book |

Peirce also gives a fourth informal case relevant here: an index can be "any mere landmark by which a particular thing may be recognized because it is as a matter of fact associated with that thing, a proper name without signification, a pointing finger" — his example, a signpost reading "Here!" fixed on a historic battlefield. Move the sign, and it no longer directs anyone to the site; and even correctly placed, it is useless to someone who cannot read the language it's written in.

## Why It Doesn't Map Onto Jung

Peirce's question is *formal*: how do existing things (words, pictures, objects) convey information through their properties? Jung's question is *psychological*: how or why do certain symbolic representations arise and carry disproportionate psychic weight? The two trichotomies/dichotomies cross-cut each other rather than aligning:

- The phrase "Heavenly City" is a **symbol** in Peirce's sense (it represents purely by convention/interpretation) but not necessarily a **symbol** in Jung's sense — a non-Christian may understand the trope's meaning perfectly well (satisfying Peirce) while it carries no unconscious/numinous charge for them (failing Jung's test).
- A picture of a seven-headed dragon is an **icon** in Peirce's sense (representing by resemblance, even though dragons don't exist) but a **symbol** in Jung's sense (standing for something psychologically unknown — Rome's oppressive temporal power, per Jung's 1952 reading of Revelation).
- Jung makes essentially the same point from the other direction: "A bull-headed god can certainly be explained as a man's body with a bull's head on it. But this explanation can hardly hold its own against the symbolic explanation" (Jung, 1921, para. 819) — a formal/iconic description (Peirce's level) is available but psychologically inadequate (Jung's level).

## Archetype Theory as a Kind of Index

Jones takes this a step further: archetype theory itself functions like a Peircean index. Its signpost analogy: "Here!" on a battlefield only directs someone who can already read the sign — by analogy, one must "speak Jungianism" (be trained in the interpretive vocabulary of analytical psychology) to see that a given myth, scripture, or image indicates "Archetype A" rather than "Archetype B." This reframes the perennial complaint that archetypal interpretation looks arbitrary or unfalsifiable to outsiders: it isn't that the sign is meaningless, but that it is only legible within a specific interpretive community — precisely the tension Jones frames via [[Utterance (Bakhtin)|Bakhtin's]] centripetal/centrifugal forces.
