# Lumen Naturae (Light of Nature)

The *lumen naturae* ("light of nature") is Paracelsus's name for the second, independent source of human knowledge — distinct from and equal to divine revelation. It is the natural light of the cosmos itself, transmitted through the stars and dwelling in the human body as an innate faculty of intuitive knowing. Jung reads it as the alchemical precursor to the concept of the collective unconscious: the spontaneous, non-rational intelligence of nature reaching into human awareness.

Primary source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay III, §1 (pars. 148–163). Primary figure: [[Paracelsus]].

---

## The Two Sources of Knowledge

Paracelsus inherited a world with one official source of knowledge: divine revelation, mediated by Scripture and Church. His decisive contribution — and the source of his perpetual conflict — was insisting on a second, fully independent source: the light embedded in nature itself.

From his *Philosophia sagax*:
> "There are, therefore, two kinds of knowledge in this world: an eternal and a temporal. The eternal springs directly from the light of the Holy Spirit, but the other directly from the Light of Nature."

He considered the knowledge from the light of nature "not from flesh and blood, but from the stars in the flesh and blood. That is the treasure, the natural Summum Bonum." Both lights ultimately derive from God — "there is nothing that does not have its origin in God" — but the natural light was accessible to pagans and even animals.

Paracelsus framed this distinction as a theological puzzle rather than a conflict: "If Father and Son are one, how then can I honour two lights? I would be condemned as an idolater: but the number one preserves me."

---

## The Astrum: Where the Light Comes From

The *lumen naturae* comes from the **Astrum** — the stellar principle, the astral body of nature:

> "Nothing can be in man unless it has been given to him by the Light of Nature, and what is in the Light of Nature has been brought by the stars."

Paracelsus says: "The light of nature is the *quinta essentia*, extracted by God himself from the four elements, and dwelling 'in our hearts.' It is enkindled by the Holy Spirit."

The *Astrum* is not the visible sky but the invisible stellar world embodied within human nature — the "corpus astrale" or astral body that is the invisible form of the natural world. Birds' prophecies come from it: "the auguries of birds are caused by these innate spirits... All this comes from the innate spirit and is the Light of Nature... it is the astral spirit which is the invisible body of nature."

---

## What It Knows

The *lumen naturae* is fundamentally an **intuitive** faculty, not rational:

> "The light of nature is an intuitive apprehension of the facts, a kind of illumination."

It has two channels:
- A **mortal** source (the natural endowment, the animal-like prophetic faculty)
- An **immortal** source, which Paracelsus calls "angels": "Man is also an angel and has all the latter's qualities. He has a natural light, but also a light outside the light of nature by which he can search out supernatural things."

This intuitive knowing is what guides:
- Prophets ("He who is chaste is a good prophet, natural as the birds")
- Physicians (Paracelsus's students based medicine on direct experience of nature, not Aristotle)
- Magicians (magic derives from the *lumen naturae*, not from the Devil or the Holy Spirit: "he speaks from the innate spirit of the invisible body which teaches *Magiam*")

## Agrippa and the Tradition

The concept probably derives from Agrippa von Nettesheim's *Occulta philosophia* (1533), which speaks of a *luminositas sensus naturae* that "extends even to the four-footed beasts and enables them to foretell the future." Paracelsus adopts this and radicalizes it into a full epistemological principle.

In alchemy, this idea was ancient. The "Tractatus Aristotelis" says: "See therefore that the light which is in thee be not darkness." In the *Tractatus aureus*, Mercurius speaks: "My light excels all other lights, and my goods are higher than all other goods. I beget the light, but the darkness too is of my nature."

The aim of alchemy was "to beget this light in the shape of the *filius philosophorum*" — to extract and embody the light hidden in matter. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]].

---

## The Light and the Filius (par. 161–162)

In philosophical alchemy, the *lumen naturae* coincides with the *Sapientia* (Wisdom) and the *Scientia*:

> "We can safely call the light the central mystery of philosophical alchemy. Almost always it is personified as the *filius*, or is at least mentioned as one of his outstanding attributes. It is a δαιμόνιον pure and simple."

Khunrath (following Paracelsus): "This [the *filius philosophorum*], the Son of the Macrocosm, is God and creature... the one conceived in the womb of the Macrocosm, the other in the womb of the Microcosm, and both of a virginal womb."

The identification of the natural light with the divine son sets up a structural inversion of Christianity: in Christ, God became man; in the *filius philosophorum*, man extracts the divine from matter. "Man takes the place of the Creator." This is what Jung calls the shadow of alchemy — its shadow of hubris, and simultaneously its shadow of truth.

---

## Historical Consequences (par. 149, 163–164)

Paracelsus's *lumen naturae* principle "opened the way for the scientific investigation of nature and helped to emancipate natural science from the authority of tradition." This emancipation was simultaneously liberating and spiritually dangerous:

> "Though this liberating act had the most fruitful consequences, it also led to that conflict between knowledge and faith which poisoned the spiritual atmosphere of the nineteenth century in particular."

The trajectory runs: Paracelsus (*lumen naturae*) → Goethe's Faust ("God under me") → Nietzsche's Zarathustra ("God is dead") → modern science's conquest of nature. At each step the natural light grows more powerful and the divine light more remote.

"Science and technology have indeed conquered the world, but whether the psyche has gained anything is another matter."

## Psychological Reading

Jung reads the *lumen naturae* as a prescientific name for what analytical psychology calls the **spontaneous activity of the unconscious**:

- The innate prophetic faculty of animals = instinct
- The light from the stars in the human body = the collective unconscious, archaic and universal
- The "angel" that carries supernatural light = the Self as an autonomous source of meaning beyond the ego

The light that comes "from below" — from the body, from nature, from the stars in the flesh — is the light that consciousness cannot manufacture by effort. It arrives as dream, vision, intuition, or (in Paracelsus's world) magical perception. It is the same light the alchemists sought to extract from matter.

> [!key-insight] Lumen Naturae and the unlived life
> Every drive toward direct experience of reality — away from received doctrine and authority — draws on the *lumen naturae*. This is also what drives the individuating person: not the inherited God-image, but the God-image that emerges from direct encounter with one's own nature. Paracelsus's tension between the two lights is the tension every serious Jungian faces between collective tradition (persona/dogma) and authentic inner experience (the Self).

## The Light of the Darkness (§A, pars. 197-199)

Near the end of Essay III, Jung returns to the *lumen naturae* for his deepest treatment. The key distinction: two different lights are at work in the spiritual life, and the *lumen naturae* is NOT the light from above.

> "The light that is lighted in the heart by the grace of the Holy Spirit... is more important to them than the great light which shines in the darkness and which the darkness comprehended not. They discover that in the very darkness of nature a light is hidden, a little spark without which the darkness would not be darkness." (par. 197)

The formulation is precise and paradoxical: the *lumen naturae* is a light that cannot be separated from the darkness that contains it. Without that spark, darkness would not even register as darkness — the light defines what darkness is. The light from above (revealed truth, grace, the Holy Spirit) "made the darkness still darker" by contrast; but the *lumen naturae* "is the light of the darkness itself, which illuminates its own darkness, and this light the darkness comprehends."

This is John 1:5 inverted: "the darkness comprehended it not" — but here, the darkness DOES comprehend the light, because it is the darkness's own light.

Paracelsus's position: his ethics and professed faith were Christian, but "his most secret, deepest passion, his whole creative yearning, belonged to the *lumen naturae*, the divine spark buried in the darkness, whose sleep of death could not be vanquished even by the revelation of God's son." (par. 197)

**The alchemical operation of the *lumen naturae***: "it turns blackness into brightness, burns away 'all superfluities,' and leaves behind nothing but 'faecem et scoriam et terram damnatam' (dross and scoriae and the rejected earth)." The *lumen naturae* does not produce radiant glory — it burns away everything false and leaves the rejected residue. The calcination is its signature.

**Where it lives**: "Just as this prayer has come down to us embedded in a mass of magical recipes, so does the *lumen naturae* rise up from a world of kobolds and other creatures of darkness, veiled in magical spells and almost extinguished in a morass of mystification." (par. 199)

The Great Magic Papyrus of Paris — a late-antique prayer embedded in magical recipes — is quoted in full as an example of the *lumen naturae*'s environment. The "Secret Inscription" begins:

> "Greetings, entire edifice of the Spirit of the air, greetings, Spirit that penetratest from heaven to earth, and from earth, which abideth in the midst of the universe, to the uttermost bounds of the abyss... O great, greatest, incomprehensible fabric of the world, formed in a circle! Heavenly One, dwelling in the heavens, aetherial spirit, dwelling in the aether, having the form of water, of earth, of fire, of wind, of light, of darkness, star-glittering, damp-fiery-cold Spirit!"

This prayer appears in the same sources as magical recipes for love potions and curses — yet it rises above its context as genuine religious feeling. The *lumen naturae* is always like this: embedded in superstition, touched by coarseness, yet luminous within the mixture.

**The symbol as tertium** (par. 199): When trying to understand what unites the opposites (light and darkness, natural and spiritual), rational concepts fail — they only re-open the opposition. "Here only the symbol helps, for, in accordance with its paradoxical nature, it represents the 'tertium' that in logic does not exist, but which in reality is the living truth."

> "The protean mythologem and the shimmering symbol express the processes of the psyche far more trenchantly and, in the end, far more clearly than the clearest concept; for the symbol not only conveys a visualization of the process but—and this is perhaps just as important—it also brings a re-experiencing of it, of that twilight which we can learn to understand only through inoffensive empathy, but which too much clarity only dispels."

The right response to the *lumen naturae* is not analysis but empathy — not conceptual clarity but participatory re-experiencing. The very language of archaic alchemy (nettle, May, Aniada, heavenly marriage) conveys "a vision and experience of a climax whose significance could be amplified only by the finest flights of the poets. But the clear and unambiguous concept would find not the smallest place where it would fit."

**The goal of alchemy as *unio naturae***: "Not separation of the natures but union of the natures was the goal of alchemy. From the time of Democritus its *leitmotiv* had been: 'Nature rejoices in nature, nature conquers nature, nature rules over nature.'" (par. 198) The Church separated nature from spirit; alchemy sought to unite them. Nature IS transformation — "it strives not for isolation but for union, for the wedding feast followed by death and rebirth." Paracelsus's "exaltation in May" = the hierosgamos of light and darkness, Sol and Luna.

> [!key-insight] The light of the darkness vs. the light from above
> The *lumen naturae* must not be confused with revelation, grace, or inspiration in the Christian sense. It is specifically the light that the darkness produces from within itself — the spark inside the nigredo, the phosphorescence of matter. This is why individuation begins in darkness (the shadow) rather than in illumination: the first light is always the faint gleam of the nigredo recognizing its own nature. One cannot skip to the light from above without first finding the light from below.
