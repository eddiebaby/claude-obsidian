# Coniunctio

> **One-line definition:** The alchemical union of opposites (classically Sol and Luna, King and Queen) — the central operation of the alchemical opus and, psychologically, the symbolic language for the union of consciousness and the unconscious that drives individuation toward the Self.

---

## Origin and Scope

The coniunctio (Latin: conjunction, union) is the climactic operation of the alchemical work — the marriage, fusion, or chemical wedding of two opposed substances or principles into a third. Jung treats it as alchemy's most psychologically loaded symbol because the texts consistently describe it not as a mechanical mixing but as a *hierosgamos* (sacred marriage): something that must die before it can be reborn as a unity.

This page synthesizes coniunctio material across all three of Jung's major alchemical volumes. [[jung-cw12-psychology-alchemy]] (1944) introduces the basic vocabulary (Maria Prophetissa's axiom, Sol-Luna marriage, the *fons mercurialis*). [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] works through the operation in individual texts (the Rosarium, Paracelsus). [[jung-cw14-mysterium-coniunctionis]] (1955-56), Jung's last major work, is devoted to the coniunctio in full — this page's primary source.

---

## The Basic Formula: Maria Prophetissa's Axiom

From CW 12: "One becomes two, two becomes three, and out of the third comes the one as the fourth." Jung calls this the psychological leitmotiv of the whole of alchemy. The coniunctio is the operation that enacts this progression — duality (the opposites) generates a third (the union), and the achieved fourth is psychological wholeness. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]] for the goal-state this process produces.

## The Opposites (CW 14, Ch I)

Jung catalogues the dualistic pairs that the coniunctio unites: humidum/siccum (moist/dry), frigidum/calidum (cold/hot), and centrally **Sol and Luna**. These appear arranged in a quaternio and are often rendered theriomorphically — eagle and toad, two birds or dragons, stag and unicorn. Jung describes the opposites as "transconscious" and calls the whole structure "the phenomenology of the paradoxical self" (CW 14, par. 1-4).

**Mercurius as mediator**: standing between the pairs of opposites is Mercurius — "the true arcane substance, a spirit" that "penetrates all things," the *aurum philosophorum*, the germ of unity. Jung draws the Ephesians 2:13 parallel: Christ as peacemaker who "breaks down the dividing wall of hostility" performs the same structural function alchemically. See [[Mercurius]].

**The orphan, the widow, and Luna** (CW 14, Ch I §3): the prima materia accumulates an enormous range of feminine synonyms before the union — mater, matrix, Venus, regina, femina, virgo, Luna, meretrix, vetula — folding in the Isis/Osiris myth and Cabalistic material (Malchuth/Luna as the "widow," Tifereth as son/King). Both Mercurius and Luna are noted as *duplicitous* mediators — the union they make possible is not simple or safe. See [[Prima-Materia]].

## Sol and Luna: The Union and Its Psychology (CW 14, Ch III §4)

The fullest single statement of the coniunctio's psychological meaning in Jung's corpus.

The moon (Luna) is Sol's feminine counterpart and the universal receptacle of the sun — the "bountiful nurse of the dew." The Tabula Smaragdina's "Its father is the Sun, its mother the Moon" is the classical formula. The Rosarium's "Unless ye slay me" passage — Sol-Rex slain by ten men, the coniugium of dragon and woman — depicts the mortificatio that precedes the union; the death of the king is not destruction but the necessary first stage.

> **Par. 172** (key): "Psychologically, this means that the union of consciousness (Sol) with its feminine counterpart the unconscious (Luna) has undesirable results to begin with: it produces poisonous animals such as the dragon, serpent, scorpion, basilisk, and toad..." — the union of conscious and unconscious is not initially harmonious. It first releases what was repressed or split off.

> **Par. 217** (key): "The moon with her antithetical nature is, in a sense, a prototype of individuation, a prefiguration of the self: she is the 'mother and spouse of the sun, who carries in the wind and the air the spagyric embryo conceived by the sun in her womb and belly.'"

> **Par. 219** (key): "Luna is really the mother of the sun, which means, psychologically, that the unconscious is pregnant with consciousness and gives birth to it." — a direct reversal of the naive view that consciousness simply illuminates a passive unconscious. The unconscious is generative, not merely receptive.

### Jung's Logos/Eros Formulation (par. 224-226)

Jung explicitly maps Sol/Luna onto his earlier gendered-function vocabulary, then revises his own preference:

> **Par. 224** (key): "For purely psychological reasons I have, in other of my writings, tried to equate the masculine consciousness with the concept of Logos and the feminine with that of Eros. By Logos I meant discrimination, judgment, insight, and by Eros I meant the capacity to relate."

Jung goes on (par. 225-233) to say he now prefers the archetypal images Sol/Luna to the "pedestrian" abstractions Logos/Eros, because the alchemical pair carries the full ambivalence (poison and medicine, death and rebirth) that the abstract terms flatten. He extends the analysis to lunar anima in men and solar animus in women, "dark sun" (*Sol niger*) feminine psychology, and the father-imago/animus link, closing on "the disastrous repetition of the family pattern... the psychological original sin."

> [!note] Read with [[Anima and Animus]]
> This section is the primary-source location for Jung's claim that Sol/Luna are *better* archetypal carriers of the masculine/feminine principle than his own earlier Logos/Eros terminology — worth cross-referencing directly against the Anima and Animus page's treatment of the syzygy.

## Rex and Regina: King and Queen as Coniunctio Figures (CW 14, Ch IV §§1-3)

The king is the carrier of collective-unconscious myth — crown (sun rays), mantle (starry firmament), orb (world), throne. Egyptian kingship theology supplies the richest material: Pharaoh as incarnation/son of God, the *ka*, a double trinity (divine: father-god/divine son/*ka-mutef*; human: father-god/Pharaoh/Pharaoh's ka). Jung explicitly parallels the Egyptian trinity amulet (Horus, Hathor, winged serpent) to "the illustrations of the coniunctio in the Rosarium, showing King, Queen, and the dove of the Holy Ghost" — Rex and Regina are the human-scale, dramatized form of the same union pattern as Sol and Luna.

### The King's Death and Transformation — the "Allegoria Merlini"

A king drinks water "closest to his heart," swells and dies, is dismembered, dried, and reconstituted (Egyptian then Alexandrian physicians, sal ammoniac and nitre), and is finally resurrected, crying "Where are my enemies?" Jung reads this as "the primitive motif of the murder or sacrifice of the king for the purpose of renewing his kingly power" — connected to the *aqua permanens* ("It kills and vivifies") and the Egyptian Heb-Sed festival as "humanized regicide."

### The Spagyric Marriage

Dorn's term for the union of the purified, self-known mind (*unio mentalis*) with the body — the coniunctio's second degree (see below).

> **Par. 364** (key): "The dissolution of the heart in water would therefore correspond to the union of the male with the female, and this in turn to the union of conscious and unconscious, which is precisely the meaning of the 'spagyric marriage.'... The spagyric union produces an embryo whose equivalents are the homunculus and the lapis. **The lapis, of course, is a symbol of the self.**"

This is the load-bearing sentence connecting Rex/Regina symbolism directly to [[Self (archetype)]]: the king-and-queen marriage, worked through psychologically, produces the symbol of totality.

## Dorn's Three Degrees of Conjunction (CW 14, Ch VI)

Jung's final and most systematic statement of the coniunctio comes from his reading of Gerhard Dorn, who breaks the operation into three successive stages — and identifies active imagination as the modern equivalent of the first.

**First degree — *unio mentalis*.** The union of the warring contents of one's own mind; psychic self-knowledge. Jung translates Dorn's alchemical recipe directly into clinical method:

> **Par. 749** (key): "Take the unconscious in one of its handiest forms, say a spontaneous fantasy, a dream, an irrational mood, an affect, or something of the kind, and operate with it... the alchemical operation seems to us the equivalent of the psychological process of active imagination." See [[Active Imagination]].

**Second degree — the spagyric marriage.** The union of the now-unified mind with the body — conscious with unconscious in the fuller, embodied sense. "We would say that with this production of a physical equivalent the idea of the self had taken shape" (par. 758).

**Third degree — union with the *unus mundus*.** The union of the whole man (mind and body, now one) with the undivided ground of being itself — Dorn's *res simplex*, "the potential world of the first day of creation... when nothing was yet 'in actu'... but was still one" (par. 760). This is the coniunctio's outer limit and is treated in full on the [[Unus Mundus]] page.

> [!note] Three degrees, one operation
> The coniunctio is not a single event but a *graded* process: psychic integration (degree 1) → embodied integration (degree 2) → cosmic participation (degree 3). Most clinical individuation work operates at degree 1 and approaches degree 2; degree 3 is, for Dorn and for Jung, a limit-concept rather than an ordinary therapeutic achievement.

## The Lapis as Coniunctio's Product

Across all three volumes the coniunctio's successful outcome is named the lapis (the philosopher's stone) — "considered psychologically," Jung writes, the lapis "describe[s] the archetype of the self" (CW 14, par. 776). The squared circle / mandala is its visual phenomenology. See [[Lapis-Philosophorum]] and [[Self (archetype)]].
