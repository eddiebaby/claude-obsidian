# One-Sidedness

Jung's term (*Einseitigkeit*) for the over-development of one psychic function or attitude at the expense of its opposite. Not a flaw to be avoided but a structural inevitability: "selection demands direction. But direction requires the exclusion of everything irrelevant. This is bound to make the conscious orientation one-sided" (*Psychological Types* ¶694). The question is never whether one-sidedness happens, but how extreme it becomes and whether it is ever consciously offset. Named in [[qwarnstrom2026-onesidedness-coriolanus-timon|Sofie Qwarnström's 2026 essay]] as the exact structural root of Shakespearean tragedy — A.C. Bradley called it "the fundamental tragic trait" independently of Jung.

## Virtue Gone Mad, Not Arrogance

Classical *hubris* is usually misread today as boisterous pride. Its older sense is closer to one-sidedness itself: ancient texts apply *hubris* to plants suffering "superabundance of nurture" that must be pruned (Michelini). Robust strength, left undirected, becomes self-destructive. "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied" (*Romeo and Juliet*, II.iii). This reframes pathological one-sidedness as an excess of a real strength, not a moral failing — the same strength that made someone effective is what, unchecked, breaks them.

## Mechanism

As a personality develops it differentiates a **superior function** (see [[Superior Function]]) into conscious dominance; the function diametrically opposite in kind correspondingly sinks into the unconscious as the **inferior function** (see [[Inferior Function]]), where it accumulates compensatory pressure. Sustained beyond a threshold, this pressure discharges as **[[Enantiodromia|enantiodromia]]** — the sudden flip into the opposite.

Marie-Louise von Franz's clinical elaboration: the superior function doesn't just neglect its opposite, it actively tries to **capture and administer it on its own terms** — "like an eagle seizing a mouse." An introvert who intellectualizes away a real pull toward relationship ("she's just my anima projection") is using the superior function to pre-empt ever actually meeting the inferior one. The alternative is not conquest but a **"mixtum compositum"** — an uncomfortable transitional state, "neither fish, nor flesh, nor good red herring," in which the dominant function genuinely cedes ground.

## Literary Case: Coriolanus and Timon

Two mirror-image tragedies of the same axis: **Coriolanus** (dominant Introverted Thinking, inferior Extraverted Feeling) starves his own capacity for felt relation exactly as he starves the plebeians he governs; **Timon of Athens** (dominant Extraverted Feeling, inferior Introverted Thinking) floods himself and Athens with undiscriminating generosity, having silenced the judging function (personified by Apemantus the cynic) that would let him see his flatterers clearly. Both plays open on the same image — a hungry crowd and the hero's relation to feeding it — as the visible register of an inner starvation. Both endings enact enantiodromia directly: Coriolanus's rigid loyalty flips to attempted destruction of what he loved; Timon's undiscriminating love flips to undiscriminating hatred and suicide.

## Two Kinds of Danger, Not One

The essay is careful that one-sidedness isn't a single failure mode. Coriolanus's danger is closing off — rigidity, exclusion, contempt for the group's felt values. Timon's danger is dissolving — boundlessness, no capacity to withhold or judge. Naming *which* one-sidedness is active matters: the corrective for one (soften, relate, feed the group) is precisely the danger of the other.
