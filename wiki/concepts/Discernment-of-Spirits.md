# Discernment of Spirits

The Ignatian method for distinguishing which inner movements come from a constructive/transformative source and which from a destructive/hindering one — imported by [[Eckhard Frick|Frick]] ([[frick2025-spiritual-path-transformation|2025]]) into Jungian practice as the discipline that spiritual transformation requires. Its criterion is the felt movement of **consolation** versus **desolation**.

## Ignatius's Method

[[Ignatius of Loyola]] developed discernment of spirits (*discretio spirituum*) in the *Spiritual Exercises* (*Exercitia spiritualia*), from his own convalescence at Manresa: he observed the "coming and going" of consolation and desolation, and which imaginings left the soul lastingly at peace versus disturbed. Frick's illustrating example is Ignatius's vision of a beautiful serpent-like thing "with many eyes": it gave consolation in the presence of the almshouse but *desolation* in front of the cross — and by that changed felt-tone Ignatius discerned it as "the devil," casting it aside. The undogmatic, spontaneous character of a vision emerging from the unconscious indicates its authenticity; it is then elaborated "with strong backing from the will" — imagination *processed by discernment*, which Jung found congenial because it neither destroys the image nor lets it possess the person.

## Why Jung Cared

Jung ran a 1939–40 seminar on the *Exercitia spiritualia*, comparing Ignatius's visions to his own (1913 onward). He was intrigued by the difference between **active imagination** (where the unconscious manifests itself autonomously) and a more passive, guided imagining, and by whether imagination *opens* or *closes* the door toward the uncontrollable ([[Faith in O|O]]). Discernment is the quality-control step that keeps imaginative/visionary material transformative rather than possessing.

## The Bipolar Spirit Archetype

Discernment is necessary because the archetype of the **spirit (*Geist*)** is bipolar. Jung insisted *Geist* signifies effervescence — *Begeisterung*, enthusiasm — "but which may also be destructive." One can be **seized** (*ergriffen*) by the spirit constructively, or **inflated** and possessed by it. Jung's cautionary cases: Nietzsche inflated by the archetype of the spirit (the old wise Zarathustra), and Jung's own diagnosis of German "Wotan" spirituality. See [[Inflation-Jungian]].

## The Three Failure Modes

Frick names three defensive extremes when confronting the spirit archetype and spiritual transformation:

1. **Spiritual bypassing** — avoiding transformation by rationalization, persona-shaping, denial of the body, and ideologies of all kinds. (Not to be confused with genuine transcendence; it is transcendence used as an evasion.)
2. **Spiritual neglect** — failing to value the spiritual dimension, one's own or the other's, reducing it to more or less childish needs.
3. **Spiritual inflation** — being seized/possessed by a more or less malignant spirit, "often with the best intentions."

"All in all, spirituality requires discernment." And the discernment is **circular** — "not cross-sectional, not even longitudinal" — the same shape as the whole transformation process, which encompasses standstill and deadlock.

## Shadow of Spiritual Accompaniment

Frick extends discernment to the "subtle power of spiritual abuse" (Johnson & VanVonderen) — spiritual neglect, manipulation, or violence in communities. Understanding and mentalizing these shadows helps wounded people find their own spiritual trajectory rather than being "dominated by a guru or spiritual perpetrator." The paradigm of *constructive* discernment is [[Spiritus contra spiritum]] (Jung's letter to AA's Bill Wilson).
