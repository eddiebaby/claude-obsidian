# Uroboros

The ancient image of a serpent or dragon devouring its own tail. In Neumann's developmental psychology of consciousness, the uroboros is the primary symbol of the pre-ego, undifferentiated state — the primordial wholeness before any subject/object distinction exists.

The word derives from Greek: *oura* (tail) + *boros* (devouring). "The uroboros is properly called the 'tail-eater'" (Neumann).

---

## The Primordial State

The uroboros is not merely an image. It is a structural description of the psyche before consciousness has differentiated itself from the unconscious totality.

In this state:
- Self and world are merged — no interior/exterior boundary
- Conscious and unconscious are undivided
- There is no ego, no observer, no subject
- The psyche feeds on itself — it is self-sufficient, requiring nothing outside itself
- Time is circular, not linear: what is consumed is also what is born

Neumann: "The world is experienced as all-embracing" in the uroboric state. The uroboros "fertilizes itself in the mouth by eating its own tail."

This is the symbolic registration of the infant's pre-verbal, pre-individuated experience — and also of states of deep absorption, meditation, sleep, and certain mystical states in adults.

---

## Historical and Cross-Cultural Occurrence

The uroboros symbol appears independently across widely separated cultures:
- Ancient Egypt: the Ouroboros in funerary texts and alchemical imagery
- Ancient Babylon: documented there by Neumann
- Gnostic texts: the serpent circling itself as the Pleroma
- Alchemical tradition: the circular serpent as prima materia and as the goal (the *lapis*)
- Norse mythology: Jormungandr, the world-serpent encircling Midgard
- Revelation of St. John: serpent imagery with circular structure
- Medieval illustrations: Giotto and others incorporate the tail-eater

Neumann: "The uroboros was known in ancient Babylon" and "the uroboros can be traced in the Revelation of St. John." The symbol rests on a collective (archetypal) basis — its independence of derivation confirms it as an expression of the collective unconscious.

## Psychological Meaning: Neumann's Framework

### The Three Aspects of the Uroboros

**1. The Uroboros as Great Round**
The uroboros is the primal plenum — everything contained, nothing excluded. All archetypes are latent within it. It is the undifferentiated matrix from which the Great Mother herself will later emerge as the first differentiated figure.

**2. The Uroboros as Maternal World**
"The uroboros of the maternal world is life and psyche in one." The maternal character of the uroboros is pre-personal: this is not the personal mother but the archetype of the containing, all-enveloping matrix. The developing ego encounters this as warmth, safety, and dissolution.

**3. The Uroboros as Devourer**
The same wholeness that nourishes also threatens. The uroboros "reigns on as the great whirling wheel of life" — it pulls back what it has released. The pull toward regression, merger, dissolution is the uroboric pull. It is the dragon the hero must overcome.

### Development Out of the Uroboros

"When the ego begins to emerge from its identity with the uroboros" (Neumann), it enters the matriarchal stage under the dominance of the Great Mother — but has not yet found full independence. The uroboric pull remains the primary threat to ego formation at every developmental stage.

"The ego emerges from the primary security of the uroboric state" — this emergence is experienced as loss as well as gain. Security is surrendered; vulnerability and individuality are gained.

### Uroboros in the Individual Life Cycle

Neumann maps the uroboros across the human lifespan:
- **Infancy**: the uroboric state is the literal psychological condition — pre-ego, pre-verbal
- **Adolescence**: the first dragon fight breaks the uroboric hold of the maternal world
- **Midlife**: "the uroboros persists for an unnaturally long time" in those who fail to individuate — a sign that the ego has not adequately differentiated
- **Old age / death**: "the uroboros appears as 'the ocean of Godhead'" — the longing to dissolve back into the whole is not regression at this stage but completion. The uroboros arches over the full arc of life.
- **The mature man**: "uroboros symbolism in the mature man is an indication that he must once more free himself from this world... and come to himself"

## The Uroboros and the Self

A crucial distinction: the uroboros is not the Self (in Jung's sense). The Self is the totality of the psyche including the differentiated ego. The uroboros is the state before any differentiation — it is pre-Self, or the inchoate condition from which the Self will eventually emerge.

However, the uroboros "symbol will reappear as the mandala in the psychology of the adult." The mandala — the circular symbol of psychic wholeness — is the achieved form of what the uroboros represents as unrealized potential. The uroboros is the beginning; the mandala is the culmination. Both are circular; the difference is the presence or absence of a differentiated center.

See [[Mandala Symbolism]] for the mandala as the uroboros' transformed successor.

## The Uroboros in Alchemy

Alchemical tradition knew the circular serpent as both the prima materia (the raw chaotic beginning) and as a symbol of the completed work (the lapis). Neumann recognized this duality as psychologically accurate: the uroboric unconscious is both the starting material for individuation and the symbolic representation of the final integration.

The alchemical motto "One is All" (Hen to Pan) captures the uroboric quality: in the beginning, everything is one and undifferentiated; in the end, everything is re-unified at a higher level of integration.

## CW 13: Zosimos and the Sacrificial Uroboros (pars. 105–111)

Jung's commentary on the Visions of Zosimos gives the uroboros its sharpest alchemical definition. Three simultaneous operations are attributed to it directly (par. 105):

> **"The dragon slays itself, weds itself, impregnates itself."**

Self-destruction, self-union, self-generation in one act. "Self-devouring is the same as self-destruction, but the union of the dragon's tail and mouth was also thought of as self-fertilization."

The divine water (*aqua permanens*) has a "dyophysite nature" (*τὸ στοιχεῖον τὸ διμερές*): active and passive, masculine and feminine, devouring and generating — "which constitute the essence of creative power in the eternal cycle of birth and death." The uroboros is this cycle made visible.

### Ion = Uroboros = Sacrificer (par. 111)

Jung's central interpretive move: **Ion = uroboros = sacrificer** — three aspects of the same principle.

In the first vision, Ion submits to torment; the sacrificer (Ion) dismembers Ion and burns him; Ion transforms into a "mutilated anthroparion" that tears its own flesh with its own teeth. This is the uroboros: Ion spews forth his own flesh and rends himself. The homunculus Ion becomes *is* the uroboros.

This equation is confirmed by the temple's form: "neither beginning nor end in its construction" = the circular structure of the uroboros made architecturally explicit.

### Dismemberment and the Four Elements

The purpose of the uroboros-sacrifice: "to create the beginnings of order in the *massa confusa*, 'in accordance with the rule of harmony'" (par. 111). Dismembering the uroboros = dividing it into the four elements. The four elements are the first differentiation of chaos into order.

> [!key-insight] The Four Functions
> Par. 111: "Without knowing anything of alchemy or its operations, I worked out many years ago a psychological typology based on the four functions of consciousness as the ordering principles of psychic processes in general. Unconsciously, I was making use of the same archetype." The four alchemical elements = the four psychological functions. Zosimos articulated the archetype Jung independently recovered.

### The Dragon at the Temple Entrance

The temple instruction (III, i, 5): "A dragon lies at the entrance, guarding the temple. Lay hold upon him; immolate him first; strip him of his skin, and taking his flesh with the bones, separate the limbs; then, laying the flesh of the limbs together with the bones at the entrance of the temple, make a step of them, mount thereon, and enter, and you will find what you seek."

The dragon = the uroboros as guardian of the threshold. The initiate must encounter, kill, and dismember it before the interior (the spring of purest water, the lapis) is accessible. The dragon's dismembered body *becomes the staircase*. This is the *nigredo* as prerequisite.

## The Uroboros and the Hero's Dragon

The dragon the hero must fight is typically uroboric in character — a great serpent, a world-encircling monster, a devouring chasm. This is not coincidence. The hero's enemy is the pull back into the undifferentiated state, the comfort of dissolution, the fear of individuation.

"The dragon fight is against the primordial archetype of the uroboros." The hero does not destroy the uroboros — that would be impossible and would mean destroying the unconscious itself. The hero transforms his relationship to it: from unconscious identification to conscious differentiation. The treasure the dragon guards (the captive, the anima, the wisdom) is what the uroboros has been containing all along.

## Practical / Clinical Notes

In individual psychology, uroboric dynamics appear as:
- Merger fantasies, symbiotic relationships, difficulty separating from the maternal
- Addictive states (the addicted person seeks dissolution, the uroboric return)
- Creative absorption states (positive uroboric immersion)
- Depression and withdrawal as regression to the undifferentiated
- Mystical states — genuine regression to the ground, or genuine transcendence (the distinction matters clinically)
- The fear of dying as the fear of uroboric dissolution (and sometimes the longing for it)

The uroboros is not pathological in itself. At the appropriate developmental moment, the uroboric state is protective and necessary. It becomes problematic when it persists beyond its developmental moment or is reinstated as a defense against the demands of differentiation.
