# God-Image (Imago Dei)

The God-image (*Imago Dei*) is Jung's psychological term for the inner representation of the divine: the numinous archetype that humans across all cultures and epochs have projected outward as "God." Psychology cannot determine whether God exists independently of the psyche. It can investigate what the God-image is and how it functions within the psyche.

Source: [[jung-answer-to-job]] (primary); [[jung-cw9ii-aion]] (Christ as symbol of the Self); [[Self (archetype)]] (structural equation); [[jung-cw11-psychology-religion]] (earliest empirical/clinical statement of the psychological-boundary rule, 1937).

> [!note] The 1937 statement of the boundary rule
> The Terry Lectures (CW 11, para 102) state the psychological-vs-metaphysical boundary in its clearest, most compact form, fourteen years before *Answer to Job*: "It would be a regrettable mistake if anybody should take my observations as a kind of proof of the existence of God. They prove only the existence of an archetypal God-image, which to my mind is the most we can assert about God psychologically." See [[jung-cw11-psychology-religion]] for the full dream-series case study this conclusion is drawn from.

## The Core Claim: God-Image Equals Self

In *Aion* (CW 9ii, para 42), Jung states directly: "The self... is a God-image, or at least cannot be distinguished from one." In the same work (para 60): "All statements about the God-image apply also to the empirical symbols of totality."

The Self archetype — the totality of the psyche, the archetype of wholeness — and the God-image make the same claims:

- Totality (containing all opposites)
- Ground of being (the center from which all else derives meaning)
- Transcendence of the ego (the Self is "other" to the ego; so is God)
- Numinosity (evoking awe, fear, and fascination — Rudolf Otto's *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*)
- Ordering function (mandalas as expressions of both the Self and the divine center)

This equation is psychological, not theological. Jung is not claiming that the Self is God in the metaphysical sense. He is claiming that what humans call God, when they experience it as an inner numinous reality, is psychologically the same as what he calls the Self.

## The Imago Dei in Christian Tradition

The phrase *Imago Dei* ("image of God") comes from Genesis 1:26-27: humanity created "in the image and likeness of God." Medieval and patristic theology debated what this image is:

- **Origen**: the *Imago Dei* is imprinted on the soul, not the body; the soul participates in the divine intellect.
- **Augustine**: distinguished between the image of God that is Christ (perfect) and the image implanted in humanity as a *possibility* — a capacity for becoming like God through grace.
- **Thomas Aquinas**: the image is in the intellect; the soul mirrors the Trinity in its tripartite structure (memory, intellect, will).

Jung takes the Augustinian distinction and turns it psychological: the image of God in the soul is the archetype of the Self, the potential wholeness that individuation actualizes. The difference from theology is that for Jung this wholeness includes the dark side (shadow); it is not purely spiritual or intellectual.

## Psychological God vs. Metaphysical God

Jung is precise about this boundary:

**What psychology can study:** The God-image as a psychic reality. How it functions in individuals and cultures. What it demands. How it changes. The consequences of its suppression or inflation.

**What psychology cannot study:** Whether there is a God independent of the psyche. Whether the God-image corresponds to a transcendent reality. Theological or metaphysical questions about God's nature.

This distinction matters for reading *Answer to Job*. When Jung says "Yahweh is unconscious of his own shadow" or "God needs human consciousness," he is making claims about the psychological contents of the God-image as it appears in the Bible — not metaphysical claims about divine being.

The move is methodological and careful, but its implications are radical: the God-image, like any archetype, can be one-sided, can be compensated, can develop. This is what *Answer to Job* demonstrates.

## The Unconscious God: Yahweh's Shadow

The most important development of the God-image theme comes in *Answer to Job*. The biblical Yahweh is psychologically characterized by:

1. **Omniscience without self-knowledge**: Yahweh knows all facts but is not reflectively aware of his own contradictions.
2. **The coincidentia oppositorum**: Yahweh contains opposites — creator and destroyer, merciful and wrathful, just and arbitrary — without consciousness of the tension between them.
3. **The shadow as "son of God"**: Satan in Job is explicitly one of the "sons of God," not an independent evil force. He is Yahweh's own dark aspect, externalized and named but not integrated.

This is the psychological structure of the unconscious: the opposites exist within it, but consciousness has not brought them into relationship. The God-image of the Old Testament is psychologically richer than the Christian God precisely because it does not sanitize the deity — it shows the full coincidentia oppositorum.

## The Development of the God-Image

One of Jung's important claims: the God-image changes across history. This is not because God changes (metaphysical question) but because the human psyche's relationship to the God-image evolves.

**Old Testament Yahweh:** Numinous totality. Unreflective. Both creative and destructive. Chooses a people arbitrarily. Makes bets at human expense. The coincidentia oppositorum in raw form.

**Christ (New Testament):** The God-image becomes incarnate. It enters human consciousness. It acquires moral integrity, reflective suffering, and the capacity for love. But in doing so, it becomes one-sided: purely good (*Summum Bonum*), without shadow. The shadow is projected onto Satan, Judas, the Antichrist.

**The Antichrist (Book of Revelation):** The compensatory return of the dark side. The repressed shadow of the purely good Christ returns with apocalyptic force.

**Sophia / Assumption of Mary:** The God-image begins to incorporate the feminine. The masculine Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) moves toward a quaternity. This is the second answer: the completion that Christ's one-sided maleness left undone.

This developmental arc is also the map of individuation at the collective scale.

## Why Human Consciousness Matters to God

In *Answer to Job*, Jung argues that the God-image needs human consciousness:

"Existence is only real when it is conscious to somebody. That is why the Creator needs conscious man even though, from sheer unconsciousness, he would like to prevent him from becoming conscious." (Para 617, established text)

The claim is psychological: the Self archetype cannot become fully real, cannot achieve genuine wholeness, without the mediating function of ego-consciousness. The unconscious totality needs the partial, limited ego to reflect it. Without that reflection, the divine contents remain unconscious — powerful and numinous, but unintegrated.

This is the reverse of the usual religious claim (that humans need God). Jung says the relationship is mutual: the God-image presses toward incarnation, toward consciousness, because it requires the finite human perspective to know itself.

---

## The Quaternity: Completing the God-Image

Jung argues that the Christian Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) is a *triad* missing its fourth element. In alchemical and psychological terms, three is incomplete: it lacks the grounding fourth that would make it a whole.

The missing fourth is variously: the feminine (Sophia), the earthly (matter vs. spirit), the dark (shadow vs. light), the body (vs. the purely spiritual Trinity).

The papal dogma of the Assumption of Mary (1950) — which Jung discusses near the end of *Answer to Job* — he reads as an unconscious psychic compensation: the collective psyche is restoring the feminine to the divine quaternio. The Mother of God is elevated to quasi-divine status, reconstellating the Sophia archetype.

The complete God-image is therefore a quaternio: Father (power/source), Son (consciousness/logos), Spirit (movement/connection), Sophia/Mary (wisdom/eros/earth). This maps onto the four functions of consciousness in Jung's typology and the four cardinal directions of the mandala.

## Individuation and the God-Image

The personal implications of the God-image for the individuation process:

- The God-image in dreams and active imagination is typically the first appearance of the Self archetype. It arrives numinous, overwhelming, autonomous.
- The task is not to worship or identify with the God-image, but to enter into relationship with it: to hold the ego's perspective while being moved by what the Self presents.
- The shadow of the God-image (the dark divine, the deus absconditus) must be confronted. Inflation (identifying ego with the God-image) and dissociation (denying it entirely) are the two failure modes.
- The quaternity principle applies individually: integration is not complete until the fourth function (the inferior function, the shadow, the feminine for men / masculine for women) is brought into the mandala of the whole.

See [[Individuation]] for the full framework. The God-image is specifically the symbolic register in which these movements appear in religious persons or in persons for whom religion is the natural symbolic language of the depths.

## Key Distinctions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| *Imago Dei* | The image of God within the soul; the psychic archetype of the divine |
| God-image | Jung's term for the psychological reality of the divine archetype; synonymous with the Self at the experiential level |
| Metaphysical God | Whether God exists independently of the psyche; question outside psychology's scope |
| Yahweh | The Old Testament God-image: numinous, coincidentia oppositorum, unreflectively powerful |
| Christ | The New Testament God-image: incarnate, morally conscious, but one-sided (purely good) |
| Sophia | The wisdom-feminine God-image; the second answer; completes the quaternity |
| Coincidentia oppositorum | The union of opposites that the complete God-image must embody |

---

> [!contradiction]
> Theological tradition (Orthodox, Catholic, Protestant) insists that God is pure *Summum Bonum* with no shadow or dark side. Jung's claim that the God-image contains an unintegrated shadow directly contradicts this. Jung's response: the theological tradition has done what individuals do when they project their shadow — they externalized the dark onto Satan. This does not eliminate the shadow from the psyche; it makes it unconscious and more dangerous. The contradiction is not resolvable across the two frameworks; it marks the exact boundary between theology and depth psychology.
