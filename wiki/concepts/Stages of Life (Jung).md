# Stages of Life (Jung)

Jung's developmental model of the human lifespan, first fully articulated in the essay "The Stages of Life" (1931), collected in *Modern Man in Search of a Soul*. A parallel treatment appears in CW 8 (¶749–795, flagged as synthesized in that ingest). The model is distinguished by its emphasis on the *second half of life* — specifically, that midlife is not decline but transformation, requiring a completely different psychological programme than the first half.

Sources: [[jung-modern-man-search-soul]] Ch. 5 (directly read); [[jung-cw8-structure-dynamics-psyche]] ¶749–795 (synthesized).

---

## The Central Premise: Problems Require Consciousness

Jung opens the essay with a philosophical framing: animals and young children have no real problems because instinct resolves everything. The capacity for problems is the capacity for consciousness. "Problems thus draw us into an orphaned and isolated state where we are abandoned by nature and driven to consciousness." This is painful, but it is the condition of psychic development. Every genuine problem solved widens consciousness.

Corollary: the attempt to evade problems (by denying them, repressing them, seeking easy certainties) produces stultification and eventually neurosis.

## The Four Phases

### 1. Childhood

No real psychological problems of the child's own. The child is embedded in the parents' psychic atmosphere: "not yet completely born, but still enclosed in the psychic atmosphere of its parents." Psychic birth — the genuine beginning of a separate self — comes only at puberty.

Consciousness at this stage: "sporadic, being limited to the representation of a few connections" — islands of memory in darkness. The ego-complex forms gradually; the child begins to say "I" only when the ego-contents have acquired their own energy.

### 2. Youth: Puberty to ~35

With puberty comes the eruption of sexual life and "a psychic revolution." The ego asserts itself without stint. The world opens. The characteristic challenges:

- Demand for adaptation: entering a profession, finding a partner, establishing a place in society
- The temptation of false presuppositions: exaggerated expectations, unjustified optimism, denial of inner difficulties
- The pull toward regression: clinging to childhood's unconscious security, refusing responsibility

The characteristic failure of youth is the "more or less patent clinging to the childhood level of consciousness." Something in us "wishes to remain a child; to be unconscious, or, at most, conscious only of the ego; to reject everything foreign, or at least subject it to our will."

Resolution: differentiate a particular aptitude, achieve social being, adapt to the world. This is the *normal* and appropriate solution — but it is only temporarily valid. It costs something: "We limit ourselves to the attainable, and this means the renunciation of all other potentialities."

### 3. Midlife: The Turn (~35–45)

"Statistical tables show a rise in the frequency of cases of mental depression in men about forty. In women the neurotic difficulties generally begin somewhat earlier."

The change begins subtly: a shift in character, traits returning that had vanished in childhood, interests weakening and new ones arising, moral convictions hardening into rigidity. The man who had been flexible becomes fanatical. The woman who had been devoted to others now pushes toward her own goals. These are signs of a reversal already in progress from below.

Jung's sun metaphor: "In the morning it arises from the nocturnal sea of unconsciousness and looks upon the wide, bright world... At the stroke of noon the descent begins. And the descent means the reversal of all the ideals and values that were cherished in the morning."

**The gender reversal.** Men in the second half develop more feminine qualities (tenderness, relatedness, interiority); women develop more masculine qualities (decisiveness, social authority, incisiveness). "A man of forty or fifty years winds up his business, and his wife then dons the trousers." This is psychic compensation: the undeveloped contrasexual qualities now demand their due. When it happens unconsciously, marriages break, personalities shift alarmingly. When met with awareness, it is genuine maturation.

**The error.** "Wholly unprepared, they embark upon the second half of life... we take this step with the false presupposition that our truths and ideals will serve us as hitherto. But we cannot live the afternoon according to the programme of life's morning — for what was great in the morning will be little at evening, and what in the morning was true will at evening have become a lie."

### 4. Second Half: The Afternoon (~45 to death)

The task of the second half is not more achievement, more social expansion, or competitive youth-mimicry. It is inward: "Ageing people should know that their lives are not mounting and unfolding, but that an inexorable inner process forces the contraction of life. For a young person it is almost a sin to be too much occupied with himself; but for the ageing person it is a duty and a necessity to give serious attention to himself."

The second half calls for:
- Development of inner life over outer achievement
- Engagement with the unconscious (dreams, active imagination, reflection)
- The search for meaning rather than success
- Preparation for death: understanding that the psyche may have purposes that extend beyond individual survival

"A human being would certainly not grow to be seventy or eighty years old if this longevity had no meaning for the species." The afternoon of life "cannot be merely a pitiful appendage to life's morning."

**The guardians of culture.** In primitive societies the old people hold the mysteries and the laws — the cultural heritage. Jung observes that modern Western culture has failed to cultivate this: "In the United States it is almost an ideal for the father to be the brother of his sons, and for the mother if possible to be the younger sister of her daughter." This is living the second half by the programme of the first — and it produces the lamentable substitutes: hypochondria, niggardliness, doctrinairism, eternal adolescence.

## Consciousness Development — The Three Stages of Psychic Phase

Jung introduces a parallel structural model of how consciousness develops within the lifespan (not to be confused with the four temporal phases above):

1. **Anarchic / chaotic stage**: Pure recognition, no stable ego. The child's first "knowing" — making connections between contents, but no continuous self.
2. **Monarchic / monistic stage**: The developed ego-complex. One center governs; the ego is king. "The world is mine to understand and manage."
3. **Dualistic stage**: The emergence of a second center of equal importance — the problem, the counter-claim, the shadow-content. "I discover I am not only who I thought I was." This is the beginning of real psychological work.

The dualistic stage is what forces genuine development. Without it, the ego remains inflated and the unconscious compensates.

## The Schools for Forty-Year-Olds Problem

Jung notes that ordinary schools prepare young people for the first half of life: professional competence, social adaptation, relationship. There are no schools for the second half. Religion once served this purpose — the great religions "were always such schools in the past" — but modern Western civilization has lost access to them as living systems.

The consequence: most people enter the second half of life completely unprepared, attempt to apply first-half solutions to second-half problems, and develop neurosis or stagnation.

The psychotherapist who sees these patients must do what religion once did: orient the person toward meaning, toward the inner life, toward the transformation that the psyche requires.

## Relationship to Individuation

The Stages of Life model is not separate from [[Individuation]] but its temporal backbone:
- First half: the hero's journey of ego-building and social adaptation
- Midlife: the crisis of the hero-myth — the test that the old self cannot pass by old means
- Second half: the deeper individuation, oriented toward the Self rather than the ego
