# a priori

Latin: "from what is before" — knowledge or categories that are independent of, and logically prior to, particular sensory experience. Standing Latin-phrase concept page per vault convention.

## Definition

In Kantian epistemology, *a priori* categories are the mind's built-in structuring forms — the conditions under which experience becomes possible at all, as opposed to *a posteriori* knowledge, which is derived from experience. Kant's claim was that a limited, fixed number of such categories (causality, substance, unity, etc.) organize all possible experience without themselves being learned from it.

## Context in Jones (2023)

[[Raya Jones|Jones's]] essay traces this term through the philosophical lineage Jung himself sketched for the archetype concept: Jung (1948a) linked his own idea "through medieval philosophy onward via Spinoza to **Kant, who posited a limited number of *a priori* categories**, and to Schopenhauer, who 'carried the process of simplification still further, while at the same time endowing archetypes with an almost Platonic significance'" (Jung, 1948a, paras. 275–276).

Jung's earliest formal statement of the archetype concept (1919) explicitly used this vocabulary: he contrasted instincts with "pre-existent forms of apprehension … viz., the 'archetypes' of apperception, which are the prior determining constituents of all experience" (p. 19) — i.e., archetypes as the psyche's own *a priori* categories, structuring apprehension the way Kant's categories structure cognition of the physical world.

Jones reads this as "as I read it, these living dispositions could be understood as pre-existing in the way that dispositions to walk or to talk pre-exist infants' learning to do it" — archetypes as *a priori* capacities-for-experience-of-a-certain-type, not *a priori* contents.

The term also surfaces in the paper's discussion of [[Charles Sanders Peirce|Peirce]], "commonly regarded as a Kantian philosopher although scholars debate the extent to which he departs from Kant's theory of categories" — situating both Jung's and Peirce's projects as descendants of the same Kantian *a priori* move, applied to different domains (psyche vs. representation).

## Why It Matters for Archetype Theory's Credibility

The *a priori* framing is precisely what later gets contested in the biological-transmission debate (see [[Archetype]] §Science and Credibility, [[Deconstructing Archetype Theory]]). Kant's *a priori* categories were never claimed to be genetically inherited structures in the germ-plasm — they were transcendental conditions of cognition, not biological facts. Jung's move to ground archetypes in heredity and "the germ-plasm" is a step beyond the Kantian *a priori* that Kant himself never took, and it is this extra step — not the *a priori* framing itself — that [[Christian-Roesler|Roesler]] and others identify as scientifically untenable.
