# Aphantasia

## Definition

The absence or severe reduction of voluntary visual mental imagery — the inability to "see" a mental picture on demand, even though other cognitive functions (memory, reasoning, dreaming) may remain intact. Term coined by Zeman et al. (2015), cited in [[turchin-active-imagination-lucid-dreaming]].

## Why It Matters Here

[[Alexey Turchin]] explicitly warns against conflating aphantasia with the much more common "**mental-screen-only**" imaginal type he documents (see [[Mental Screen]]): someone lacking a bright **visual screen** can still generate rich, complex, emotionally intense imagery on a low-resolution "mental screen" — closer to the imagery one has while reading a novel than to true absence of imagery. True aphantasia is a narrower, more complete condition. The practical distinction matters for any active-imagination or visualization-based practice: a mental-screen-only participant is not aphantasic and should not be told their session "didn't work" just because nothing appeared behind closed eyes.

## Practical Implication for Active Imagination

Per Turchin's pilot, roughly half of participants had mental-screen-only or no images at all, and **the type is not trainable** — extensive effort does not convert a mental-screen person into a visual-screen one (informal military spatial-visualization research is cited as corroborating: spatial visualization is not a trainable skill). The correct response to "I don't see anything" is not more visualization coaching but reassurance that any way of experiencing the exercise — including purely verbal storytelling with no imagery at all — is valid and can still be emotionally real. See [[Mental Screen]] for the full two-screen model this sits inside.

## App Relevance

For any active-imagination or dream-visualization feature (see [[Individuation App Differentiators]] item 4), this is a hard design constraint: the feature cannot assume visual imagery is universal, must accept text/verbal-only engagement as a first-class mode, and should not frame "brightness" of imagery as a marker of success.
