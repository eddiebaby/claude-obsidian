# The Philosophical Tree (Arbor Philosophica)

**The philosophical tree** (*arbor philosophica*) is Jung's term — drawn from alchemy's own vocabulary — for the tree as a symbol of the self seen as a *process*, rather than a static structure. "If a mandala may be described as a symbol of the self seen in cross section, then the tree would represent a profile view of it: **the self depicted as a process of growth**" (par. 304). Where the mandala freezes wholeness into a geometric instant, the tree shows wholeness unfolding through time — rooted in the body and earth, growing toward and being fed by the light, dying back, and renewing.

Primary source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]], Essay V, "The Philosophical Tree" (pars. 304–482) — Jung's last essay in CW 13, written 1945 (revised 1954), originally intended for a Festschrift for the botanist Gustav Senn. Epigraph, from Faust: *"All theory, my friend, is grey, / But green life's golden tree."*

The essay has two parts: **Part I** (pars. 304–349) presents and interprets 32 spontaneous tree pictures drawn by Jung's patients; **Part II** (pars. 350–482) traces the historical and alchemical background of the tree symbol, section by section.

## Part I: The Patient Picture Series (pars. 304–349)

Nineteen of the thirty-two pictures were made by patients before Jung himself knew anything about alchemy, and the rest before *Psychology and Alchemy* was published — Jung stresses this repeatedly as evidence that the parallels with alchemical tree-symbolism were **not learned, but spontaneously and independently produced** by the unconscious. This is the same argument he makes elsewhere for the autonomy of archetypes: the image recurs because the psychic structure that produces it is universal, not because of cultural transmission.

### Recurring motifs across the series

- **World-tree / world-axis** (Figs. 1–2): a solitary tree on an island, or rising from the globe of the earth — the painter's felt "spiritual isolation," compensated by the tree's perfect symmetry (a union of opposites, the "motivating force and goal of the individuation process").
- **Tree as fountain of fire-and-water** (Figs. 5–6): "our water is fire" — the union of opposites made visible; the tree grows simultaneously *upward and downward*, and the four mountains beneath are not a mere mirror-image of the four above — **each is a world in itself**.
- **Tree breaking through the earth's crust** (Fig. 7): an "irresistible," natural process that "cannot be checked by any amount of resistance" — individuation as a force, not a decision.
- **Dragons/crocodiles guarding the tree** (Figs. 9–10): the tree's growth is dangerous to disturb, "guarded by dragons," yet the tree itself is unthreatened, since its growth is independent of human consciousness. The doubled tree joined by a ring, guarded by crocodiles = Mercurius *duplex*, the union of opposites the crocodiles (separated, and therefore dangerous) represent.
- **Tree and snake** (Figs. 11–13): two aspects of Mercurius — tree = passive/vegetative/earthbound corporeality, snake = active/animal/emotionality/soul. "Without the soul the body is dead, and without the body the soul is unreal."
- **Sapphire/treasure hidden in the roots, uroboros as constancy** (Figs. 16–17): the tree guards a treasure (recalling the pearl of great price, the grain of mustard seed) — but the alchemists meant not the Kingdom of Heaven but the *admirandum Mundi Maioris Mysterium*. The self-devouring uroboros = hieroglyph of eternity (Horapollo); the alchemists called the lapis "*Hermaphroditi flos saphyricus*" because, like a fruit that is also a seed, it is "forever dying yet eternal."
- **Regressive/inturned tree** (Figs. 18–20): a tree that cannot lift itself from the earth despite its cosmic nature — the patient's justified fear of the "exacting demands of the future," the threat upward growth poses to earthbound security and moral inertia. Jung: patients often confess "their fear of any such autonomous development of their psychic contents," and the corrective is showing them the same fantasy already recorded four hundred years earlier in an alchemical text — proof that "he is not alone in a strange world which nobody understands."
- **Tree-nymph / independent Eve** (Figs. 21–23): the sleeping figure hidden in the tree's trunk awakens and half-emerges — an autochthonous primordial being, an Eve who came into existence independently rather than being drawn from Adam's side, compensating the "one-sidedness ... of the biblical myth of the secondary creation of Eve."
- **Quaternity of light, four rivers, four mountains** (Fig. 24): "an acceptance of wholeness by the little female figure, an intuitive apprehension of the self." Jung inserts a testy aside here about critics who accuse him of an obsession with the number four: "Just for once, they should look into an alchemical treatise—but that is evidently too much of an effort."

### KEY: the danger of inflation (Figs. 25, par. 332–333)

A later picture shows the female figure drawn *into* the light-symbol rather than merely bearing it — Jung reads this as an increased danger of **identification with the Self**: "Anyone who has passed through such a development will feel tempted to see the goal of his experiences and efforts in union with the self." This is not merely an intellectual insight but "primarily a moral one." Failure to recognize the natural impulse to identify with the Self as a temptation to be resisted (rather than consummated) produces repression and dissociation instead of development — regression rather than realization.

**Par. 333, key formulation**: "the tree has no leaves, and its branches could just as well be roots. All its vitality is concentrated in the centre, in the human figure that represents its flower and fruit. A person whose roots are above as well as below is thus like a tree growing simultaneously downwards and upwards. **The goal is neither height nor depth, but the centre.**"

### The chakra/animus material (Figs. 26–30)

A patient influenced by theosophy produces an inverted-cross mandala with a black fish drawn from the unconscious — Jung reads this through the *svādhiṣṭhāna-chakra* (Tantric water-region, associated with fish/moon symbols) and criticizes Western theosophy for filling consciousness with "ideal conceptions" while avoiding the harder work: "One does not become enlightened by imagining figures of light, but by **making the darkness conscious**." Later pictures (Figs. 29–30) show a masculine daemon "waking up" — the animus, personifying masculine thinking in a woman — with citations from the Bhagavad Gītā ("I am the game of dice") tracing the mood of numinous liberation to Krishna-as-self. Jung's caution: the animus, like any archetype, has "a Janus face" and is "quite unfitted to represent totality" — a common error is to let the animus (or anima) occupy the place that belongs only to the Self.

### Literary coda: Noël Pierre's "Soleil Noir" (par. 348–349)

Jung closes Part I with a modern French poem describing a spontaneous vision: a universal midpoint, spiral rotation, a giant catalpa hung with the hearts of the dead, a dwarf ("little sage") of natural wisdom at each fork, an island at the river's end holding a grey stone cube — "the philosophers' stone = treasure guarded by the tree." An entirely independent, contemporary confirmation of the same archetypal cluster.

## Part II: History and Interpretation of the Tree Symbol (pars. 350–482)

### §1 — The Tree as Archetypal Image (par. 350)

Common associations across cultures: growth, life, unfolding (physical and spiritual), development both upward and downward, the **maternal aspect** (protection, shade, nourishment, but also being "rooted to the spot"), old age, personality, and finally **death and rebirth**. "The psychoid form underlying any archetypal image retains its character at all stages of development, though empirically it is capable of endless variations."

### §2 — The Treatise of Jodocus Greverus (pars. 354–357)

Greverus (1588): the whole opus is the sowing and tending of a tree in a walled garden — soil = purified Mercurius; Saturn/Jupiter/Mars/Venus = the trunk; sun and moon supply the seed. Explicitly *not* common metals: "the [metals] of the Philosophers." Greverus's closing hymn calls the work "a gift of God, containing the secret of the undivided oneness of the Holy Trinity" — which strikes the modern reader as exaggerated, but Jung insists these comparisons were not spurious mystification: the alchemists genuinely felt a parallel between their process and religious mystery, because both are organized by **the same archetypes** (the *tertium comparationis*).

### §3 — The Tetrasomia (pars. 358–368)

The reduction of a **quaternio of opposites to unity**. Two benevolent planets (Jupiter, Venus) and two malefic (Saturn, Mars) form an alchemical quaternity; the fourfold Mercurius corresponds to the four sons of Horus (Hapi, Tuamutef, Amset, Qebhsennuf — protectors of the four Canopic organs) and to Ezekiel's four cherubim (man/lion/ox/eagle faces) and Daniel's four beasts. Jung traces the cross as quaternity-symbol through Paulinus of Nola ("extended on the four arms ... he reached out to the four quarters of the world") to its psychological reading: **the cross as quaternity refers to the self, to man's wholeness** — hence its healing effect. See [[Fourfold-Quaternio]].

Daniel's fourth beast, monstrous and devouring ("a fourth kingdom ... which shall devour the whole earth"), is read as the **daemonization of the human quarter** when power-lust dominates — "a psychological process which can be observed only too often both individually and collectively... It has recurred countless times in the history of mankind."

### §4 — The Image of Wholeness (pars. 369–373)

**Aqua permanens** = an aspect of Mercurius and, per Zosimos, "the whole" (τὸ πᾶν) — "two natures, one substance." Because the microcosm is identical with the macrocosm, the aqua permanens effects an *apocatastasis* — restoration of all individual things to original wholeness: "every grain becomes wheat, and all metal gold" (Meister Eckhart). The moral equivalent of physical transmutation into gold is **self-knowledge** — a re-remembering of the *homo totus*. See [[Aqua-Permanens]].

### §5 — The Nature and Origin of the Philosophical Tree (pars. 374–379)

Gerard Dorn's description of the "metallic tree": nature plants its root (the stone that will yield metals/gems/salts) in her womb; the trunk divides into branches that are veins running literally through the whole globe of the earth "as in the human body the veins spread through the different limbs." The tree dies each cycle and "another new [tree] is there" — growth, expansion, death, rebirth as one continuous system, imagined as a planetary circulatory system.

**Dorn vs. Aristotle** (par. 377–378): Dorn was a fanatical Platonist, hostile to Aristotle, Albertus, Avicenna — "men ... inimical to the light of God and the light of nature" who "turned the name Sophia into Philosophia." Jung reads this as the ancient dispute between **realism and nominalism**, decided in the modern era in nominalism's favor: the empirical scientist tries to forget or repress the archetypal premises built into cognition itself, in the name of "objectivity"; the Hermetic philosopher held onto those premises as *inalienable components of the empirical world-picture*. Modern physics' discovery that "the observer cannot be finally eliminated" is, for Jung, evidence that Dorn's instinct — however "fanatically" defended — was not simply wrong.

### §6 — Dorn's Interpretation of the Tree (pars. 380–382)

Dorn distinguishes **"vegetabilia materiae"** (concrete, literal organisms) from **"vegetabilia naturae"** — a broader, Platonic nature that includes psychic "*animalia*": a bleeding sponge, a shrieking mandrake. These are mythologems and archetypes given the form of natural objects. Dorn calls the "Sophists" who take these symbolic names literally (using actual urine, milk, eggs, hair) frivolous triflers — a concretistic misunderstanding of what is, in fact, psychic material.

### §7 — The Rose-Coloured Blood and the Rose (pars. 383–391)

Khunrath's lion, "lured forth from the Saturnine mountain," has rose-coloured blood — the *filius macrocosmi* (lapis) correlated with **Christ as filius microcosmi**, whose blood is the red tincture. Jung insists these panegyrics were not bad taste or exuberant fantasy but **serious, primordial religious experience** that the alchemists, as good Christians, had to reconcile with their faith — the same phenomenon as a patient discovering their singularly personal dream fits an unknown myth exactly.

**The rose mystique** (pars. 387–390): traced through Mechthild of Magdeburg's vision of Christ's heart as a five-petaled rose, through Mary-as-rose allegories (*hortus conclusus*, *rosa mystica*), to Dante's heavenly rose — the rose as **mandala**, and, in the worldly register, as the beloved (the "*fedeli d'amore*"). The rose-coloured blood is not natural blood but "**symbolic blood, a psychic substance**, the manifestation of a certain kind of Eros which unifies the individual as well as the multitude." The Rosicrucian motto *per crucem ad rosam* directly continues this line.

> [!key-insight] Blind love vs. love with understanding (par. 391)
> "Love alone is useless if it does not also have understanding... The blinder love is, the more it is instinctual, and the more it is attended by destructive consequences, for it is a dynamism that needs form and direction. Therefore a compensatory Logos has been joined to it as a light that shines in the darkness." Solicitude without insight "can explain even a Torquemada."

### §8 — The Alchemical Mind (pars. 392–397)

Petrus Bonus (14th c.) discovered that the alchemical opus anticipated, "feature for feature," the Christian myth of the Redeemer's generation, death, and resurrection — and concluded (astonishingly, to Jung) that Hermes Trismegistus, Moses, and Plato had *prophetically anticipated* Christ's coming, rather than that alchemists were unconsciously projecting Christian material onto matter. Jung calls this blindness "instructive": Bonus's mind, contemplating chemical transformation, became "suffused with archetypal, mythological parallels," exactly as happens today "when the imagination is given free play in the observation ... of the products of the unconscious."

**Anti-reductionism, and the Freud disagreement** (pars. 395–397): myths and alchemical symbols "are not euhemeristic allegories that hide artificial secrets" — they try to *translate natural secrets into the language of consciousness*, not conceal something already known. The alchemist reduced his symbols to the chemical substances he worked with; "modern man reduces them to personal experiences, as Freud also does" — both moves are structurally identical acts of premature closure. Freud's own dogmatic rigidity, in Jung's reading, came from succumbing to the numinous power of the one archetype he *did* discover — incest — and over-generalizing from it: "*I only wonder what neurotics will do in the future when it is generally known what their symbols mean*," Freud once said to Jung. Jung's rejoinder: **"symbols mean very much more than can be known at first glance."** Reducing them forecloses exactly the compensatory function that would let them do their work.

### §9 — Various Aspects of the Tree (pars. 398–403)

- **The climbing motif** (Ripley Scrowle): the serpent of paradise as Melusina/Lilith in the tree's crown; the adept meets her halfway up — the shamanic "heavenly journey," in which the shaman meets his heavenly spouse, transposed into medieval Christian imagery.
- **The dead/leafless tree**: the old English legend of Seth's vision — the tree of paradise stands bare and bark-less after the Fall, coiled by a skinless snake; on a second look it has renewed itself, bark and leaves restored, and a swaddled infant (Christ, the second Adam) lies in its crown.
- **The truncated tree / tree of death**: the Egyptian tale of Bata (19th dynasty) — his soul, placed atop an acacia, is cut down and regrows from a fallen seed; killed again as a bull, two persea trees grow from his blood; cut down again, a chip of wood impregnates the queen and Bata is reborn as Pharaoh. "The tree here is an instrument of transformation."
- **The fruit-bearing tree**: four blossom-colors = the four elements combined in the opus; its fruit is "the bread of life" (John 6:35) reaching back to the Ethiopic Book of Enoch — manna, Host, and panacea "form here an unfathomable mixture."

### §§10–17 — Uroboros, Bird and Snake, the Feminine Tree-Numen, the Lapis, and the Dangers of the Art (pars. 404–448)

*(Summarized from the picture-caption plates; full analytic text not yet re-verified against the numbered paragraphs.)* The inverted tree in East Siberian shamanism; the bird/snake polarity (vertical spirit vs. horizontal instinct); the feminine tree-numen as either Self (in women's pictures) or anima (in men's, e.g. the animus figures of Figs. 29–30 above); the tree as a form of the lapis; the real psychological dangers of engaging this material without adequate ego-strength (identification, inflation, regression); and the recurring motif of **torture/dismemberment** as the price of transformation (echoing Zosimos's visions in Essay II — see [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]]).

### §18 — The Relation of Suffering to the Coniunctio (pars. 450–457)

Dorn's "golden hook" (God catching Leviathan with the cross-as-hook) traced to the Manichaean myth of the Primordial Man as bait, and then to the Gnostic **Sophia-Achamoth** myth in Irenaeus: Sophia, separated with suffering from the Pleroma, formless and unconscious, is given *form but not intelligence* by the pitying Christ on the cross, then left alone to feel her own suffering and desire "better things."

Jung reads this as a precise psychological allegory of **anima-loss in a man who identifies wholly with reason and spirituality**: masculine consciousness perceives psychic suffering (Sophia's affects) but does not make itself conscious of *why* — a state that "can be observed today not only in all masculine neuroses but among so-called normal people who have come into conflict with the unconscious." Neither the light (Christ/spirit) nor the darkness (Sophia/emotion) will yield ground first; this is why **discrimination cannot be reunified without the extinction of consciousness**, and why the unconscious, not the rational intellect, is what produces the symbols of wholeness (quaternity, circle, cross). St. Augustine's image of the cross as bridal bed and the fairytale motif of the hero finding his bride in the crown of a great tree both express the coniunctio as **a culminating point of life that is simultaneously a death**.

### §19 — The Tree as Man (pars. 458–462)

Old Rabbinic tradition: the tree of paradise *was* a man. The tree is an intermediate form between the Primordial Man and ordinary man. **For women, the feminine tree-numen represents the Self** (confirmed by the "Scriptum Alberti" and the patient pictures above); **for men, the feminine tree-figure is instead the anima** — a projection, not the Self itself, unless the man's own femininity is differentiated enough to be integrated with consciousness. Correspondingly, the patients who drew Figs. 29–30 rendered the tree-numen as the animus.

Because the opus is "a life, death, and rebirth mystery," the tree acquires the quality of wisdom (cf. the Barbeliot Gnostics: "From man and gnosis is born the tree, which they also call gnosis"). The **inverted tree** (Nakassä, East Siberian shamanism) has a carved human face at the roots — the shaman's own greater personality, climbed in ecstatic trance to "find his true self in the upper world." Eliade: the shaman needs these ecstatic journeys because "it is above all during trance that he becomes truly himself" — confirming, independently, Jung's reading of shamanistic symbolism as **a projection of the individuation process**, identical in structure to alchemy and to the modern dream-fantasy material.

### §20 — The Interpretation and Integration of the Unconscious: the Eagle Dream (pars. 463–481)

Jung's fullest single worked example of **why he refuses to reduce dream symbols to known persons or causes**. A correspondent (X) reports a friend (Y)'s dream: back in a concentration camp, Y sees a mighty eagle circling overhead, and escapes by shooting it down with a rocket-propelled aircraft. The dream followed a day after Y read Jung's *Answer to Job*. X assumes the eagle represents *himself* (X had lent Y the book and felt like Y's psychological mentor, "spying" on him from above); Y instead thinks the eagle is Jung, the book's author.

Jung's response: **"when our dream says 'eagle' it means an eagle."** He rejects Freud's model of a censoring disguise-mechanism: "a dream is quite capable, if it wants to, of naming the most painful and disagreeable things without the least regard for the feelings of the dreamer. If the dream does not in fact do so, there is no sufficient reason for supposing that it means something other than what it says." The correct procedure is not to guess which acquaintance the symbol conceals, but to investigate **what the eagle means as an eagle** — its general, cross-cultural meaning — and only then ask how that general meaning connects to the dreamer's actual situation (Y is Jewish; the book concerns the fate of man's God-image; the concentration-camp setting and the "eagle eye" of surveillance are not incidental).

> [!key-insight] Devaluation, not disguise (par. 479)
> When a dream seems to disguise a real person under a symbol, the more likely explanation is not concealment but **devaluation** — the dream is refusing to grant that person authority. Example: a woman patient dreams of her analyst as a hairdresser ("who fixes the head"). The dream is not hiding the analyst's identity; it is telling the patient that the analyst should have no more significance than a hairdresser who sets her head right so *she* can use it herself.

**The general method that follows** (pars. 477–481): where existing collective ideas (religious, mythological) are still alive and efficacious, they act as a guide to integrating unconscious material (cf. Niklaus von der Flüe's terrifying vision of God gradually reshaped, with the help of an existing devotional text, into an image of the Trinity). Where they are dead or absent, the analyst must reconstruct the general/comparative meaning from scratch. Confrontation with the unconscious "usually begins in the realm of the personal unconscious ... and from there leads to archetypal symbols which represent the collective unconscious." The reductive procedure (Freudian or literalist-alchemical) "leads away from the unconscious and merely reinforces the one-sidedness of the conscious mind"; amplification — supplying context and comparative mythologems — is what actually lets a symbol do its integrative work. See [[Dream-Analysis-and-Interpretation]] for the fuller method this exemplifies.

### Closing paragraph (par. 482)

Jung ends the essay, and CW 13, on a note of hard-won realism: mere intellectual understanding is not sufficient — "no understanding by means of words and no imitation can replace actual experience." Alchemy itself "lost its vital substance when some of the alchemists abandoned the *laboratorium* for the *oratorium*," dissolving into nebulous mysticism, "while others converted the *oratorium* into a *laboratorium*, and discovered chemistry." Both halves are admirable in their own way — "but no one asks about the fate of the psyche, which thereafter vanished from sight for several hundred years."

## Psychological Synthesis

The philosophical tree completes the picture that Mercurius (Essay IV) and the Zosimos visions (Essay II) build from the substance side: where Mercurius is the *principium individuationis* imprisoned in matter and the lapis is individuation's finished symbol, the tree is **individuation's growth curve** — rooted in body and instinct, subject to real developmental dangers (inflation, regression, inturned growth), gendered differently for men and women (Self vs. anima/animus), and inseparable from suffering, since the coniunctio it images is "a culminating point of life and at the same time a death."
