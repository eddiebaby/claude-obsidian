# Psychedelic Renaissance

The label used across psychedelic-research literature for the accelerating scientific, public, and commercial interest in psychedelic medicines that began in earnest in the 2010s-2020s, following ~30 years of international prohibition after the first wave of pre-prohibition psychedelic research in the 1950s-60s. [[osterhold2023-chasing-the-numinous]] treats the renaissance as a Jungian case study: real clinical promise shadowed by a collective psychological pattern the paper diagnoses using [[Cultural Complex]] theory and the Buddhist [[Hungry Ghost]] image.

Source: [[osterhold2023-chasing-the-numinous]] (*Journal of Analytical Psychology* 68(4), 2023).

Navigation: [[depth-psychology]] | [[Numinous Experience]] | [[Cultural Complex]]

## The Clinical Promise

Under a psychedelic, a person enters "a temporary, reversible state of consciousness that significantly differs from the baseline state" (Revonsuo 2010, p. 257). Habitual defenses and one-sided narratives soften; suppressed material becomes accessible; the person contacts "new perspectives and orientations, not just intellectually but in an emotional and embodied experience, that is often described as deeply meaningful or life changing." Peer-reviewed publication volume grew from 31 studies (1988) to 722 (2018) (Petranker et al. 2020), with promising results across PTSD, treatment-resistant depression, substance/alcohol use disorders, and end-of-life anxiety.

**Entheogen**: the term (Greek, "generating the God within") used for psychedelics in their spiritual/mystical register — oneness, unitary consciousness, encounters with ancestors, sacred figures, or God, paralleling shamanic ritual use documented cross-culturally for millennia (Winkelman 2019, 2005; Schultes et al. 2001). This is the paper's bridge from clinical psychiatry to [[Numinous Experience]]: entheogens are read as one possible technology for accessing the same numinous ground that dreams, active imagination, and religious ritual access by other means.

## The Shadow Side: A Gold Rush

Alongside the science, "a psychedelic gold rush has begun, and investors and organizations are jockeying for position." Over 50 publicly traded companies are tied to psychedelic-drug development in the US; the market is projected to grow from $2B (2020) to $10.75B (2027) (Phelps et al. 2022). Patent races aim to "erect legal fences around new chemicals, new chemical formulations, and new applications" (Cormier 2022) — Compass Pathways has pursued patents amounting to near-blanket coverage of psilocybin for depression. Underground guide networks charge thousands of dollars per "journey" with no standardized competency vetting; ethical scandals (sexual, physical, financial abuse) have surfaced at both underground and licensed international retreat settings. Ayahuasca tourism to Peru grew roughly 25x between the 1980s and 2017, straining local traditions and access.

## Jung's Own Position

Jung wrote little directly on psychedelics and described himself as reserved and lacking deeper knowledge on the topic (Jung & Adler 1976, p. 172). His concerns, expressed in letters rather than published work:

- **Integration burden**: unconscious contents encountered too fast become individual moral tasks the person must then live out — "the more you know of it, the greater and heavier becomes your moral burden."
- **Distrust of "pure gifts"**: "I am profoundly mistrustful of the 'pure gifts of the Gods'. You pay dearly for them" (1976, p. 173) — a direct challenge to marketing claims that a single psychedelic session equals years of psychotherapy.
- **Vision vs. integration**: "the personal value lies entirely in the philosophical achievement, not in the primary vision" (1928, para 229) — the experience itself is not the work; the slow articulation and living-out of what it means is the work.
- **Sorcerer's apprentice risk**: modern man risks evoking psychic forces ("ghosts") without the "corresponding development of morality" needed to handle them responsibly — the same pattern, in Jung's view, behind unchecked scientific/technological progress generally (1976, p. 173).

See [[Inflation-Jungian]] for the two specific clinical dangers Jung names (deflation — "crushed under the contents of the unconscious," 1928 para 221; and inflation — feeling "godlike," 1928 para 227) and how this paper applies both to psychedelic-session aftermath.

## The Western Meta-Cultural Complex

The paper's explanatory frame for *why* this particular therapeutic development attracts this particular collective pattern: see [[Cultural Complex]] for full treatment. Three intersecting orientations:

1. **Outward/empirical epistemology** — validity requires external, measurable proof.
2. **Heroic conviction** — transformation should be swift, complete, won through one decisive act.
3. **Deficiency-solution orientation** — inner lack is resolved by external acquisition, never by inner work alone.

Together these predict, before any individual chooses anything, that a genuinely promising medicine will be marketed and consumed as a shortcut, patented as a monopoly, and mythologized as a wonder-drug — following the identical prior arc of Valium (1970s) and Prozac (1990s) (Kocsis 2009).

## Hungry Ghosts as the Diagnostic Image

Full treatment: [[Hungry Ghost]]. The Buddhist hungry ghost — insatiable because it cannot metabolize what it consumes, karmically caused by *mātsarya*/miserliness — is offered as the archetypal amplification of the gold-rush pattern: corporate hoarding/patenting, seeker over-consumption of "spiritual experiences" without integration, and guide/therapist power-hunger, all read as variations on acquisition without digestion.

---

## What the Paper Does *Not* Argue

The authors are explicit this is not an argument against psychedelic-assisted therapy, nor a claim that the clinical findings are invalid, nor a judgment of "the suffering individual in true need of relief" or of "the genuine interest of therapists and researchers." The target is specifically the **collective, cultural-complex-driven shadow** riding alongside the legitimate science — a distinction worth preserving when this page is cited (do not flatten to "Jungians are against psychedelics").
