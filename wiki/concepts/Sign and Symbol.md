# Sign and Symbol

Jung's foundational distinction between two modes of representation, and the specific claim — central to [[Raya Jones|Jones's]] 2023 reading — that the word "archetype" itself lives simultaneously as **both**.

## Jung's Dichotomy

Jung draws a strict either/or: "An expression that stands for a known thing remains a mere sign and is never a symbol" (Jung, 1921, CW 6, para. 817).

- **Sign**: a fixed, conventional expression for something already known. Arbitrary, learned, exhausted once decoded (a red light means stop; the skull-and-crossbones means danger).
- **Symbol**: "the best possible expression at the moment for a fact as yet unknown or only relatively known" — it stands for "something that is only divined and not yet clearly conscious" (Jung, 1921, para. 817). A symbol is living and inexhaustible; when the unknown it points to becomes fully known, the symbol collapses into a sign and dies (this half of the claim is drawn out more fully in [[jung-man-and-his-symbols]], Part 1).

This is not a taxonomy of things but a *function* — the same expression can be sign or symbol depending on whether its referent is known or only divined. "Jerusalem" is a mere sign when naming the city in the Middle East; it becomes a symbol in the Biblical vision of "the holy Jerusalem, descending out of heaven from God" (Revelation 21:10), where — per Jung — it indicates the mother archetype.

## The Archetype's Dual Life

Jones's central move: "archetype" itself is a sign *and* a symbol simultaneously, and debates over the concept are largely tacit attempts to negotiate this duality.

- **As sign**: the word functions as a technical/generic term. It implies archetypes are something *known* to trained psychologists, who decipher recurring patterns in dreams and myths.
- **As symbol**: it functions as "the best possible expression at the moment for a fact as yet unknown" — because the archetype-as-such is, on Jung's own account, fundamentally unrepresentable: "archetypal representations (images and ideas) … should not be confused with the archetype as such. They are very varied structures which all point back to one essentially 'irrepresentable' basic form" (Jung, 1954a, CW 8, para. 417).

Jones connects this to Neumann's (1959) formulation — "the archetypal as such is imageless and nameless," while "the form that the formless assumes, as an image arising in the medium of man … is transient and must undergo change and transformations" (p. 92) — and to the *Tao Te Ching*: "The nameless was the beginning of heaven and earth. The named was the mother of the myriad creatures." The irony: by naming the imageless thing "archetype," Jung's theory necessarily ceased to be nameless and became a transient product of a particular modern discourse — subject, like any named thing, to reinterpretation, misinterpretation, and reformulation.

## Why the Distinction Matters for Credibility

If "archetype" were purely a sign, the theory would be a straightforward empirical claim, falsifiable in the ordinary way (see [[Archetype]] §Science and Credibility, [[Deconstructing Archetype Theory]]). If it is irreducibly also a symbol, Jung's own definition guarantees it can never be "clearly conscious" or exhaustively specified — which is consistent with Jung's explicit caveat that "clear-cut distinctions and strict formulations are quite impossible in this field, seeing that a kind of fluid interpenetration belongs to the very nature of all archetypes" (Jung, 1949, para. 301). Jones reads the century of controversy over archetype theory's scientific status as the structural consequence of trying to hold both functions at once: a concept built to be irrefutable (as symbol) evaluated by a standard that requires refutability (as sign).

## Contrast with Peirce

[[Charles Sanders Peirce|Peirce's]] icon/index/symbol trichotomy (see [[Peircean Semiotics]]) looks superficially similar but answers a different question — how representations formally convey information, not why some representations carry disproportionate psychological weight. The two frameworks do not map onto each other one-to-one; a picture of a dragon is a Peircean icon but a Jungian symbol.
