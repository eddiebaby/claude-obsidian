# Melusina

Melusina is the alchemical water fairy — a hybrid woman/serpent figure from medieval legend who enters alchemy as a personification of the *aqua permanens* and the anima imprisoned in matter. Jung treats her in CW 13 (Essay III) as a manifestation of the *filius regius* parallel: as the kingly substance hidden in the sea, she cries for deliverance and redemption from the waters of matter.

Primary source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay III (pars. 181–184 and illustration captions).

---

## The Figure

Melusina appears in medieval legend as a beautiful woman who is secretly a serpent from the waist down — a liminal being, neither fully human nor fully animal. She can integrate into human life only under strict conditions; when the secret of her nature is discovered, she is lost.

In alchemy her serpentine nature identifies her with the uroboros / serpent symbolism, and her longing for a soul identifies her with the *anima* imprisoned in matter — "the longing of Melusina for a soul and for redemption has a parallel in that kingly substance which is hidden in the sea and cries out for deliverance." (par. 182)

---

## Melusina as Aqua Permanens

The key alchemical identification: Melusina = the *aqua permanens*. A woodcut from Reusner's *Pandora* (Basel, 1588) — quoted in Jung's commentary — depicts:

- **Melusina** as the aqua permanens, opening the side of the *filius* (allegory of Christ) with the lance of Longinus
- **Eve** (earth) in the middle, reunited with Adam (Christ) in the *coniunctio*
- From their union: the hermaphrodite = the incarnate Primordial Man
- **The athanor** (furnace) with vessel at centre = where the *lapis* (hermaphrodite) will arise
- Vessels on either side = Sol and Luna

This image encodes the entire alchemical process: Melusina/water opens the king's side (like the lance piercing Christ's side — releasing blood and water); the *coniunctio* of Sol and Luna produces the *filius*; the hermaphrodite = the reconciliation of opposites.

The detail of the lance of Longinus is striking: just as the lance opened Christ's side and released the blood-water that feeds the sacrament, Melusina opens the side of the alchemical Christ-parallel to release the divine water. She is both the weapon and the substance.

## The Filius Regius and the Cry from the Waters

Michael Maier's *Symbola aureae mensae* gives the "king's lament" in full:

> "He lives and calls from the depths: Who shall deliver me from the waters and lead me to dry land? Even though this cry be heard of many, yet none takes it upon himself, moved by pity, to seek the king... Only a few believe his lament, and think rather that they hear the crashing and roaring of Scylla and Charybdis."

This cry — the cry of the god hidden in matter — resonates across multiple traditions:
- Hippolytus on the Naassenes: the "Uncharacterized Man" (ἀχαρακτήριστος) found in earthly clay, calling from "many waters"
- Psalm 23 / Isaiah 43: the ascent (ἄνοδος) and regeneration (ἀναγέννησις) of Adam
- *Rosarium*: "At the end of the work the king will go forth for thee, crowned with his diadem, radiant as the sun, shining like the carbuncle"

The alchemical optimism: "Despise not the ash, for it is the diadem of thy heart, and the ash of things that endure." The Church Fathers saw the dark depths of matter as pure evil; the alchemists saw the dark background of the soul as containing "a king in need of, and capable of, redemption." (par. 183)

## Psychological Reading

Jung reads Melusina as a form of the *anima* who is simultaneously the *aqua permanens* — the transformative water and the female figure are the same archetype in two different modes:

- As figure: the anima imprisoned in the unconscious, longing to be heard and integrated
- As substance: the divine water / transformative principle hidden in the prima materia

The "sea" (*mare nostrum*) in alchemy is "their own darkness, the unconscious." Melusina is the anima of the sea — the queen of unconscious depths, bearing the transformative water within her serpentine nature.

> [!key-insight] The anima as *aqua permanens*
> When the anima appears as a water figure (mermaid, Melusina, Lorelei, nixie), she is carrying the alchemical water symbolism: she both lures into the depths AND is the transformative substance found there. The encounter with the anima-as-water-serpent IS the descent into the prima materia. Integration is not escape from the water but drinking from it.

## Coniunetio Animae cum Corpore (MS Sloane 5025, 1588)

A second key illustration in CW 13 shows a variant of the Melusina motif:

**"The anima as Melusina, embracing a man rising out of the sea (= unconscious): a *coniunetio animae cum corpore*. The gnomes are the planetary spirits in the form of *paredroi* (familiars). British Museum, MS. Sloane 5025, a variant of the Ripley *Scrowle* (1588)."**

This image crystallizes the psychological reading precisely. The man rises from the sea (unconscious) into the embrace of Melusina (anima). The union of the two = *coniunetio animae cum corpore* — the conjunction of soul and body. The gnomes standing at the pillars = the planetary spirits = the seven planetary forces that govern psychic life = the *paredroi*, the attendant daemons present at the sacred marriage.

The body-soul conjunction is not the final goal (that is the *coniunctio* of Sol and Luna producing the *lapis*) but the necessary first step: before the spirit can ascend, the soul must unite with the body in the depths. The man rises — he is pulled upward by the embrace of the anima from the dark water of the unconscious.

Compare with the Lambspringk illustration (same series): *The King's Son (*filius regis*) and the mystagogue Hermes on a mountain* — "the two are called spirit and soul because they represent volatile substances which rise up during the heating of the prima materia." The text: "Another mountain of India lies in the vessel, which the Spirit and Soul, as son and guide, have together ascended." The ascent on the mountain = the volatilization of the body after the *coniunetio animae cum corpore* has grounded the work. The sequence: sea-embrace (conjoin soul to body) → mountain-ascent (spirit and soul rise together).

## Cleopatra and Komarios — Waters Awakening the Dead (par. 191)

The "Instruction of Cleopatra by the Archpriest Komarios" is one of the earliest surviving Greek alchemical texts. It contains a dialogue that recapitulates the Melusina/water symbolism:

The Archpriest Komarios and companions say to Cleopatra:
> "Make known to us how the highest descends to the lowest, and the lowest ascends to the highest, and the midmost draws near to the lowest and the highest, so that they are made one with it; how the blessed waters come down from above to awaken the dead, who lie round about in the midst of Hades, chained in the darkness; how the elixir of life comes to them and awakens them, rousing them out of their sleep."

Cleopatra answers:
> "When the waters come in, they awaken the bodies and the spirits, which are imprisoned and powerless... Gradually they bestir themselves, rise up, and clothe themselves in bright colours, glorious as the flowers in spring. The spring is glad and rejoices in the blossoming ripeness they have put on."

The alchemical water = the divine water (*aqua permanens*) = Melusina herself. The "dead in Hades" = the unconscious contents imprisoned in matter. The water awakens them; they rise, clothe themselves in bright colours, blossom in spring. This is the alchemical resurrection — the *albedo* following the *nigredo* — expressed as a nature myth of spring's return after winter.

The Komarios text also reinforces the Aniada/springtime imagery: the *opus alchymicum* should begin in spring (month of Aries, ruled by Mars). The Aniada — "fruits and powers of paradise and heaven; they are also the Christian Sacraments... those things which by thought, judgment, and imagination promote longevity" (Ruland, par. 193) — are to be gathered "in the true May." The hierosgamos of Poliphilo (*Hypnerotomachia Poliphili*) likewise occurs in May.

## The Hermaphrodite / Rebis

Melusina appears in the *Rosarium philosophorum* illustration as part of the hermaphrodite sequence — the *Rebis* ("double thing," *res bina*): the end-product of the *coniunctio* of Sol and Luna, a figure that is simultaneously male and female, body and spirit, matter and soul.

Two key illustrations in CW 13 show this:
1. **The hermaphrodite as *filius/rex*** (Rosarium 1550): crowned, bat-winged, standing on the uroboros; with Pelican (circulatory distillation), arbor philosophica, and chthonic triad (three-headed serpent). The axiom of Maria (1+3) encoded: the *filius* as mediator of one and three.
2. **The Rebis** ("Book of the Holy Trinity and Description of the Secret of the Transmutation of Metals," 1420): standing on the serpent/dragon, crowned, with moon above.

The hermaphrodite is the goal: the conjunction of the two that produces the third — neither male nor female but containing both, as the lapis contains both matter and spirit.

## The Anima at the Catastrophic Threshold (pars. 215-216)

Jung identifies the specific psychic situation in which Melusina appears. The anima "belongs to those borderline phenomena which chiefly occur in special psychic situations. They are characterized by the more or less sudden collapse of a form or style of life which till then seemed the indispensable foundation of the individual's whole career. When such a catastrophe occurs, not only are all bridges back into the past broken, but there seems to be no way forward into the future." (par. 216)

One is confronted with "a hopeless and impenetrable darkness, an abysmal void that is now suddenly filled with an alluring vision, the palpably real presence of a strange yet helpful being, in the same way that, when one lives for a long time in great solitude, the silence or the darkness becomes visibly, audibly, and tangibly alive, and the unknown in oneself steps up in an unknown guise."

Melusina's reality is neither purely subjective nor objective: "she has her particular psychic reality in the sense that she is a glamorous apparition who, by her very nature, is on one side a psychic vision but also, on account of the psyche's capacity for imaginative realization (which Paracelsus calls Ares), is a distinct objective entity, like a dream which temporarily becomes reality." (par. 216)

Comparisons: Lady Polia (*Hypnerotomachia Poliphili*) dissolves into thin air just before the union — "Poliphilo, most dear beloved, farewell!" Helen in *Faust II* vanishes only with the dissolution of her son Euphorion. These are three degrees of the anima's reality and persistence.

## The Raymond of Poitiers Legend (pars. 217-218)

Jung gives the Melusina legend in full as an example of the archetype:

Count Emmerich of Poitiers adopted Raymond, the son of a poor kinsman. The relation was harmonious. On a hunt, pursuing a wild boar, Raymond accidentally killed the Count with a rebound of his sword. "Raymond was inconsolable, and in despair mounted his horse to flee he knew not where." He came to a meadow with a bubbling spring, found three beautiful women, one of whom was Melusina, "who by her clever counsel saved him from dishonour and a homeless fate." (par. 217)

"Raymond found himself in the catastrophic situation we have described, when his whole way of life had collapsed and he faced ruin. That is the moment when the harbinger of fate, the anima, an archetype of the collective unconscious, appears." (par. 218)

The legend's distribution: Celtic roots (probably), but the half-woman/half-animal (fish or snake) motif is global — India (Urvashi and Pururavas, *Shatapatha-Brahmana*), North American Indians, Pomponius Mela's "Senae" (island spirits who can change shape, cure incurable diseases, know the future). Conrad Vecerius: Melusina comes from an island where nine sirens dwell. Paracelsus names her alongside "Syrena." (par. 218)

The mercurial serpent in alchemy was called *virgo* and depicted as Melusina before Paracelsus. Hence: Melusina = Mercurius = anima AND Wise Old Man (*senex*) flowing together. Mercurius is called *versatilis, versipellis, mutabilis, servus* or *cervus fugitivus*, Proteus. (par. 218-219)

## The Acts of Melusina — Veil of Maya (pars. 220-222)

The appearance of Melusina is caused by the "intervening" Scaiolae — the driving spiritual forces emanating from the *homo maximus*. The rigorous solitude of the alchemist, combined with the endlessness of the work's obscurities, "was sufficient to activate the unconscious and, through the power of imagination, to bring into being things that apparently were not there before." (par. 220)

**The goal** (par. 220): "There is no doubt that the goal of the philosophical alchemist was higher self-development, or the production of what Paracelsus calls the *homo maior*, or what I would call **individuation**."

Melusina emerges from the watery realm and assumes human form — sometimes quite concretely, as in *Faust I* (Gretchen). But all concretizations are shattered. "Another power takes over, 'that difficult Adech,' who 'at the end changes himself.'" (par. 220)

**Par. 221 — KEY**: "It is Adech, the inner man, who with his Scaiolae guides the purpose of the adept and causes him to behold fantasy images from which he will draw false conclusions... Not his ego, that fragment of a personality, is meant; it is rather that **a wholeness, of which he is a part, wants to be transformed from a latent state of unconsciousness into an approximate consciousness of itself.**"

**The veil of Maya** (par. 222): The "acts of Melusina" = "deceptive phantasms compounded of supreme sense and the most pernicious nonsense, a veritable veil of Maya which lures and leads every mortal astray." From these phantasms the wise man extracts the "supermonic" elements: "he extracts everything meaningful and valuable as in a process of distillation, and catches the precious drops of the *liquor Sophiae* in the ready beaker of his soul, where they 'open a window' for his understanding."

"Paracelsus is here alluding to a discriminative process of critical judgment which separates the chaff from the wheat... It requires no art to become stupid; the whole art lies in extracting wisdom from stupidity. **Stupidity is the mother of the wise, but cleverness never.**"

The "**fixation**" = alchemically the lapis; psychologically "**the consolidation of feeling**. The distillate must be fixed and held fast, must become a firm conviction and a permanent content." (par. 222)

## Melusina as Shakti — The Return to Water (pars. 223-227)

§4B: The Hierosgamos of the Everlasting Man.

"Melusina, the deceptive Shakti, must return to the watery realm if the work is to reach its goal. She should no longer dance before the adept with alluring gestures, but must become what she was from the beginning: **a part of his wholeness**." (par. 223)

"As such she must be 'conceived in the mind.'" This leads to "a union of conscious and unconscious that was always present unconsciously but was always denied by the one-sidedness of the conscious attitude." (par. 223)

The *coniunctio* that results: "an experience that simply cannot be reproduced in words, but whose very nature carries with it **an unassailable feeling of eternity or timelessness**." The "mille nomina" (thousand names) disguise this fact — nothing tangible or discursive is being described. (par. 223)

**Melusina as Morgana/Aphrodite** (par. 225): Melusina = Morgana, the "sea-born" = Aphrodite, the "foam-born." Union with the feminine personification of the unconscious = "a well-nigh eschatological experience." Its Christian reflection: the Apocalyptic Marriage of the Lamb (Rev. 19:6-10) — "And I heard as it were the voice of a great multitude, and as the voice of many waters... for the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready."

The angel who speaks to John: in Paracelsus's language, he is "the *homo maior*, Adech." (par. 226) The angel says: "I am thy fellowservant, and of thy brethren." — "**Adech is not my self, he is also that of my brothers**." (par. 226)

The specific definition of the *coniunctio* experience: "the self which includes me includes many others also, for the unconscious that is 'conceived in our mind' does not belong to me and is not peculiar to me, but is everywhere. **It is the quintessence of the individual and at the same time the collective.**" (par. 226)

Goal: "the year Aniadin," the time of perfection, "when the One Man reigns for ever." (par. 227)

## Dorn's Psychological Gloss — Melusina as Vision in the Mind (par. 214)

The most important interpretive moment comes from Dorn's commentary on Paracelsus's *De vita longa* (1583). Where Paracelsus speaks of "Melusina," Dorn glosses:

> "**[Paracelsus] says that Melusina, i.e., the vision appearing in the mind** (*apparentem in mente visionem*), departs from her nymphididic nature into another transmutation, in which she will remain if only that difficult Adech, that is, the inner man, permit, that is, approve: who brings about both, that is, death and life of the Scaiolae, that is, the mental operations. The first times, that is, the beginnings, of these he permits, that is, favours; but at the end he changes himself, namely because of the distractions that intervene and impede, so that the things begun, that is, the operations, do not obtain their effect."

Dorn demythologizes Melusina: the water fairy is *apparentem in mente visionem* — the vision appearing in the mind. She is the anima-as-imagination-function, the mental image that guides the work toward its goal. Her "nymphididic nature" = the spontaneous, untamed, nature-bound quality of the initial vision. She "departs" from this nature only if the *inner man* (Adech) permits — only if the opus succeeds in transforming the spontaneous fantasy into a fixed, completed psychic achievement.

The Cyphanta (distilling vessel): The "supermonic figments" (enigmatical speculations) in the Cyphanta "open a window, that is, the understanding" — the alchemical vessel contains and concentrates the vision; from the enclosed space of the vessel, insight emerges. But to become "fixed" (brought to an end, completed), the speculations must "oppose the acts of Melusina" — that is, the nymphididic, fleeting, imaginative quality must be resisted until the vision crystallizes into knowledge.

The goal, in Paracelsus's words: "that we may safely attain that life over which Aniadus dominates and reigns, and which endures for ever with him, in whom we are present without end." — "And herewith we end our discourse on longevity."

**The psychological reading**: Dorn shows that Melusina is not primarily a mythic figure but a *psychological function*. The anima appears first as a nature-vision (nymphididic) — untamed, seductive, evanescent. The work consists in holding that vision, containing it (Cyphanta), and transforming it from fleeting imagination into stable insight. The "difficult Adech" (the Self) both permits the vision at first and then seems to impede it — because the ego's premature certainties are the true distraction, not the vision itself. The vision must outlast the ego's projections.

> [!key-insight] From figure to function
> Melusina = the anima = the vision in the mind. The alchemical myth encodes a psychological phenomenology: the anima-image first appears as nymphididic (spontaneous, nature-bound fantasy); the work transforms her into fixed knowledge (*fixatio*). What the water fairy represents — the transformative water, the aqua permanens — is precisely the dissolving-and-reconstituting function of the imagination in the individuation process.
