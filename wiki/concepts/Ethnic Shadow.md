# Ethnic Shadow

The ethnic shadow is [[Divine Charura|Charura]] and [[Stephen Bushell|Bushell]]'s (2023) term for the collective [[Shadow]] content specifically organized around a people's, nation's, or individual's history of racial domination, conquest, and colonization — split off from consciousness and carried transgenerationally within the [[Cultural Layer of the Psyche|cultural layer]] of the psyche.

Source: [[JOAP-68-1-Charura-Bushell-Duoethnographic-Colonialism]] (*JAP* 68/1, 2023).

---

## Jung's Own Encounter with the European Ethnic Shadow

The paper's exemplar is Jung's own 1925 conversation with Taos Pueblo chief Mountain Lake (Ochwiay Biano), recounted in *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*. Mountain Lake told Jung: "The whites always want something; they are always uneasy and restless. We do not know what they want. We think they are mad... they say they think with their heads." Asked what the Pueblo think with, Mountain Lake pointed to his heart. Jung wrote that this "struck our vulnerable spot, unveiled a truth to which we are blind," releasing a flood of imagery — Roman legions smashing into Gaul, the Roman eagle over the Nile, then Columbus, Cortés, "conquistadors who with fire, sword, torture, and Christianity came down upon these remote pueblos dreaming peacefully in the sun." Jung's conclusion: "What from our point of view we call colonization... has another face — the face of a bird of prey seeking with cruel intentness for distant quarry" (Jung 1963, p. 250). Charura & Bushell read this passage as Jung directly confronting his own "European ethnic shadow of colonialism," following [[Andrew Samuels|Schulman & Watkins]]' (2003) argument that the Mountain Lake encounter forced Jung "beneath the surface" to face his ethnic shadow as a white colonialist.

---

## A Two-Level Dialogue

Facing the ethnic shadow, the authors argue, requires work at two levels simultaneously:

1. **Societal/external**: dialogue with those who have carried the burden of the colonizing archetype's oppression.
2. **Internal**: dialogue with the energies and images arising from the archetype itself — an active-imagination-adjacent confrontation with one's own complicity, projection, and inherited privilege or wound.

---

## Asymmetric but Structurally Parallel

The paper is explicit that the ethnic shadow does not sit only on the colonizer's side. Charura notes the capacity, among some Black people, "to split ideas from action, psyche from culture" — projecting onto "the bad colonizer and slave trader" while denying "the colonizer within the cultural layer of their own psyche," in which slave trade, tribal wars, and oppression of different others have also been part of African societies for millennia. This is offered not to relativize colonial history but as a caution against a one-sided victim/perpetrator splitting that would itself be a shadow avoidance.

Bushell's parallel confession: "I had to acknowledge that in me was the slave trader, the plantation owner, the importer, and manufacturer, the one who did not see the humanity of the traded people." His conclusion after visiting the International Museum of Slavery, Liverpool: "the West faces total spiritual bankruptcy if we cannot value, respect, cherish and dare I say love the ancestors and contemporaries of those we have killed and ruined."

---

## Mechanism: Cultural Complexes

The authors draw on [[Sam Kimbles|Kimbles]]' (2014) theory of [[Cultural Complex|cultural complexes and phantoms]]: dynamics of belonging, othering, shadow, and identity politics "enter the superego of our collective consciousness long before one is conscious of how they inform development of our attitudes toward self and others." This is offered as the mechanism by which ethnic-shadow content is transmitted and why even a figure as reflective as Jung "would not have escaped these dynamics, given his context."

---

## Surfacing in Collective Consciousness

The paper reads several contemporary movements as instances of ethnic-shadow (and adjacent collective-shadow) material pressing into consciousness from the cultural layer: **Black Lives Matter** (racial violence), **#MeToo** (abusive male attitudes toward women), the removal of statues of men active in colonialism, and **Extinction Rebellion** (unsustainability of material domination). These are framed as "reactive, conscious movements... supported by deep archetypal shifts," not merely political phenomena.

---

## Clinical Stance

Naming Jung "colonial" and "racist" and stopping there is, per Samuels (2018) — whom the authors follow — simplistic; so is dismissing the critique as "he was just a man of his time." The clinically useful move is neither defense nor mere condemnation but the two-level facing described above: "we see depth work in this context as engaging with the inner process of forging greater awareness, working through the ethnic shadow to see the archetypal dynamic of colonizer/colonized, active in all forms of oppression of the other."
