# Confrontation with the Unconscious

## Definition

The period from roughly autumn 1913 to 1917 during which C.G. Jung engaged deliberately and systematically with an overwhelming flood of fantasy-images and visions following his break with Freud. Named and described in chapter VI of [[jung-memories-dreams-reflections|Memories, Dreams, Reflections]], it is the experiential source from which virtually all of Jung's major theoretical contributions were derived.

Henri Ellenberger's term "creative illness" — applied to comparable crises in Freud, Nietzsche, and others — fits: a period of psychological near-dissolution that culminates in a new, more integrated orientation.

---

## Historical Context

After completing *Symbols of Transformation* (CW 5) in 1912, Jung's break with Freud was final by 1913. He had stepped down as president of the International Psychoanalytic Association and lost the professional community that had provided intellectual scaffolding. He describes the aftermath: disorientation, the loss of what myth to live by, and eventually the onset of the vision-flood.

The broader context was pre-WWI Europe — a civilization about to break apart. Jung had visions of a wave of blood spreading over Europe months before August 1914; when war was declared he recognized them as not personal pathology but as a premonition of collective events. This recognition — that some inner material comes not from the personal but from the collective layer — was the experiential seed of the distinction between the personal unconscious and the [[Collective Unconscious]].

---

## Phenomenology of the Period

Jung describes the onset as a deliberate self-experiment: he let himself descend inward to see what he would find. He reached several levels.

**Depth one (ca. 1000 feet, in his metaphor):** figurative — difficult material, but still recognizable.

**Depth two (the cosmic abyss):** he found himself "at the edge of a cosmic abyss. It was like a voyage to the moon, or a descent into empty space." He encountered a crater-landscape, the atmosphere of the other world, and two figures:

- **Elijah**: an old man with a white beard; intelligent, representing Logos, the factor of insight.
- **Salome**: a young blind girl; representing Eros, the erotic-relational element. Jung was instinctively suspicious of her.
- **The black snake**: hero-companion in myth; indicating a hero-myth pattern was in play.

Jung was astute enough to recognize the anima-wise-old-man pair as a primal dyad but resisted over-interpreting: "It is more meaningful to let the figures be what they were for me at the time."

Elijah was eventually superseded by a new figure: **Philemon**. See [[Active Imagination]] for the full account of this emergence.

## The Siegfried Dream

A key dream from 1913 (before the vision-flood proper began): Jung and a dark-skinned savage shoot and kill Siegfried as he races down a mountain at dawn in a chariot of bones. Jung wakes with "overpowering compassion, as though I myself had been shot."

Interpretation: Siegfried = the heroic-ego ideal, the Germanic mode of imposing will. Killing him is a [[Psychological Sacrifice]] — the ego-ideal must be surrendered because "there are higher things than the ego's will." The compassion signals secret identity: the dreamer and Siegfried are the same figure. The dreaming ego has to destroy what it secretly is.

## Method: The Active Engagement

Jung's response to the material was neither suppression nor passive reception but active disciplined engagement. He:

1. Recorded everything in the **Black Books** (working journals), in immediate imagistic notation.
2. Reworked selected material into the **Red Book** (Liber Novus) — elaborately calligraphed, illuminated with painted images and mandala drawings.
3. Walked and talked with inner figures in the garden (Philemon in particular) — the germinal form of what became [[Active Imagination]].
4. Maintained his professional practice and family life as a deliberate anchor against ego-dissolution: "I had to find an Archimedean point."

He calls this the discovery that "there are things in the psyche which I do not produce, but which produce themselves and have their own life." Philemon was the clearest embodiment of this discovery. The method of engaging autonomous inner figures in dialogue — giving them weight, asking questions, receiving answers — directly generates the technique later codified as active imagination.

---

## The Septem Sermones ad Mortuos (1916)

A ghostly crisis in 1916 (family experiencing apparitions, footsteps, children disturbed) was resolved when Jung sat down and wrote continuously for three evenings, producing the *Septem Sermones ad Mortuos* (Seven Sermons to the Dead), attributed to the Gnostic writer Basilides. The moment he took up the pen, "the whole ghostly assemblage evaporated."

The Sermons contain early formulations of what became CW theory: the polaristic nature of the psyche, life as the tension of opposites, the Pleroma as the undifferentiated ground. The polarity principle underlying psychic energy theory ([[CW8 Theoretical Foundations]]) appears here in proto-form.

---

## Resolution: The Mandala and the Self

The confrontation period wound down between 1918 and 1920. The resolution came through the mandala. While stationed at Château d'Oex in 1918-1919, Jung drew a small mandala each morning as a psychic record of his inner state. He observed the center: "Everything, all the paths I had been following, all the steps I had taken, were leading back to a single point — namely, to the mid-point."

The theoretical conclusion extracted from this: "The goal of psychic development is the self. There is no linear evolution; there is only a circumambulation of the self." This is the core of [[Individuation]] as Jung formulated it. The confrontation period *was* individuation, lived through before it was theorized.

The 1927 "Window on Eternity" dream confirmed the insight, as did the 1928 arrival of Richard Wilhelm's *Secret of the Golden Flower* — the first external corroboration, which broke Jung's post-confrontation isolation.

## Notes on Naming

Jung titled the chapter "Confrontation with the Unconscious." The term "nekyia" (Odysseus's descent to the underworld) appears in Jung's later writing as a mythological amplification for the same experience — the hero's necessary descent before return. The Jungian literature sometimes calls it "the Night Sea Journey" (drawn from Frobenius's solar mythology). All three terms refer to the same phenomenological structure: the ego's deliberate encounter with the deep unconscious, with attendant danger and transformative potential.

Henri Ellenberger (in *The Discovery of the Unconscious*, 1970) documents this period as a classic "creative illness" — a pattern found across creative and intellectual figures where breakdown precedes breakthrough. His account, based on interviews and documents, remains the most detailed external scholarly reconstruction of what Jung actually went through.
