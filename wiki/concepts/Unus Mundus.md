# Unus Mundus

> **One-line definition:** The "one world" — the unified substrate prior to the split between psyche and matter, which synchronicity makes momentarily visible and which is the deepest ground of individuation.

---

## Origin of the Term

*Unus mundus* (Latin: one world) is a concept from medieval and Renaissance scholastic philosophy, taken up most influentially by Gerhard Dorn (16th-century alchemist and physician). Dorn used it to name the single, undivided world that exists before it differentiates into spirit and matter, subject and object, inner and outer.

Jung encounters the term through his study of alchemy and imports it into analytical psychology in *Mysterium Coniunctionis* (CW 14, 1956), his late masterwork on the alchemical coniunctio (conjunction of opposites). It also appears in the context of synchronicity as its metaphysical underpinning.

---

## The Problem It Solves

Synchronicity poses a theoretical question: *how* can a psychic event (a dream, a vision) and a material event (a beetle tapping at a window) hold the same meaning without any causal connection between them?

The causal model assumes psyche and matter are separate domains; for something in the psyche to affect matter requires a physical mechanism. If synchronicity is real — if the connection is genuinely acausal and genuinely meaningful — then the psyche-matter boundary cannot be absolute. There must be a level of reality at which they are not yet separated.

That level is the unus mundus.

---

## Jung's Use of the Concept

The unus mundus in Jung's late thought functions as a formal postulate: *if* synchronicity exists, *then* psyche and matter must share a common ground at which meaning-without-causality is possible. The archetype is the recognizable form of that common ground, operating in what Jung calls its *psychoid* dimension.

"Psychoid" means: neither purely psychic nor purely physical; on the border between the two; proto-phenomenal. The archetype in its psychoid aspect is the form in which the unus mundus leaves traces on both sides of the psyche-matter divide simultaneously.

The alchemical language Jung uses: the unus mundus is the *prima materia* before differentiation — the state before the "world" split into subject and object, mind and thing. The goal of the *coniunctio* in alchemy (and by analogy, in individuation) is a *return* to this prior unity, not as regression but as a more comprehensive integration.

---

## Individuation and the Unus Mundus

At the ordinary level, individuation is a psychological process: integrating the unconscious, confronting the shadow, developing a relation to the Self. But in Jung's late thought, individuation has a cosmic dimension.

The Self — the archetype of wholeness — is not merely the totality of the individual psyche. In the unus mundus framework, the Self is the point at which the individual psyche touches the undivided ground of being. Genuine individuation, fully carried through, would mean participation in the unus mundus: a state where one's inner wholeness and the world's wholeness are experienced as expressions of the same reality.

This is why synchronistic events cluster around deeply individuated or strongly constellated psychological states. When the psyche moves close to the Self, it moves close to the unus mundus, and the boundary between inner and outer becomes more permeable.

---

## Relation to Synchronicity

Synchronicity is the *evidence* for the unus mundus, not merely an illustration of it. Each synchronistic event is a moment when the ordering principle underlying both psyche and world (i.e. the unus mundus acting through the archetype) becomes visible in experience. The event is singular (not repeatable, not caused), meaning-saturated, and straddles the inner-outer boundary — all of which are what the unus mundus would predict.

Conversely, the unus mundus is the *explanation* for why synchronicity works: if there is a common substrate, then an archetype activated on the psychic side can organize matter on the physical side without any causal mechanism, because both sides are expressions of the same underlying order.

## The Alchemical Background (Dorn's Three Degrees, CW 14 Ch VI — primary source, directly read)

[[jung-cw14-mysterium-coniunctionis]] (Ch VI §§8-10, paras 749-789) gives Jung's fullest and final statement of the *unus mundus*, via his reading of Gerhard Dorn. Dorn names three degrees of conjunction (*coniunctio*):

1. ***Unio mentalis*** — psychic self-knowledge; uniting the warring contents of one's own mind. Jung identifies this stage's modern equivalent explicitly as **active imagination**: "Take the unconscious in one of its handiest forms, say a spontaneous fantasy, a dream, an irrational mood, an affect... and operate with it... the alchemical operation seems to us the equivalent of the psychological process of active imagination" (par. 749). See [[Active Imagination]].
2. **The spagyric marriage** — union of the now-unified mind with the body, i.e. conscious with unconscious in the fuller, embodied sense. "We would say that with this production of a physical equivalent the idea of the self had taken shape" (par. 758). See [[Coniunctio]] for the Rex/Regina symbolism of this stage.
3. **Union with the *unus mundus*** — the third and highest degree: the union of the whole man (mind and body, now one) with the undivided ground of being.

> **Par. 760** (key, directly read): "The One and Simple is what Dorn called the *unus mundus*. This 'one world' was the *res simplex*. For him the third and highest degree of conjunction was the union of the whole man with the *unus mundus*. By this he meant... the potential world of the first day of creation, when nothing was yet 'in actu,' i.e., divided into two and many, but was still one."

Jung traces the philosophical ancestry to Philo Judaeus (*De mundi opificio* — the *mundus archetypus*, man as a "little heaven" reflecting the stars) and to Plotinus (Fourth Ennead, "the unity of the soul"). He explicitly equates Dorn's third stage with "the relation or identity of the personal with the suprapersonal atman, and of the individual tao with the universal tao" (par. 762) — treating the Eastern parallel as substantive, not merely decorative.

Stage 3 is explicitly eschatological for Dorn and implicitly so for Jung: it is the limit-concept of individuation, not an ordinary clinical achievement. But the direction it names is what makes synchronistic events feel like more than coincidence — they feel like glimpses of a world less divided than ordinary experience suggests.

### The Union Happens in "a Third Thing"

Opposites do not unite by simple fusion. Jung is explicit that the union itself is a new, paradoxical entity, not a blend of the two originals:

> **Par. 765** (key, directly read): "If a union is to take place between opposites like spirit and matter, conscious and unconscious, bright and dark, and so on, it will happen in a third thing, which represents not a compromise but something new, just as for the alchemists the cosmic strife of the elements was composed by [a]... transcendental entity that could be described only in paradoxes." Man himself, like the philosopher's stone, is a *lithos ou lithos* — "the stone that is no stone."

### The Psychophysical Background Hypothesis

The chapter's central metaphysical claim — and the direct textual root of the synchronicity argument:

> **Par. 769** (key, directly read): "The background of our empirical world thus appears to be in fact a *unus mundus*. This is at least a probable hypothesis which satisfies the fundamental tenet of scientific theory: 'Explanatory principles are not to be multiplied beyond the necessary.' The transcendental psychophysical background corresponds to a 'potential world' in so far as all those conditions which determine the form of empirical phenomena are inherent in it."

Jung draws the explicit physics parallel that underlies his collaboration with Wolfgang Pauli: "Microphysics is feeling its way into the unknown side of matter, just as complex psychology is pushing forward into the unknown side of the psyche... both have developed concepts which display remarkable analogies" (par. 768).

### The Self, "God," and the Limits of the Claim

Immediately following the unus mundus material, Jung addresses what kind of claim he is and isn't making (§10, "The Self and the Bounds of Knowledge"):

> **Par. 776** (key, directly read): "The alchemist's statements about the lapis, considered psychologically, describe the archetype of the self. Its phenomenology is exemplified in mandala symbolism, which portrays the self as a concentric structure, often in the form of a squaring of the circle."

> **Par. 778** (key, directly read): "The self, in its efforts at self-realization, reaches out beyond the ego-personality on all sides... the experience of the self is always a defeat for the ego. The extraordinary difficulty in this experience is that the self can be distinguished only conceptually from what has always been referred to as 'God,' but not practically... The prototype of this situation is Job's encounter with Yahweh."

But Jung explicitly refuses the metaphysical leap this might invite. He proposes the more cautious term **psychoid** for the unknown factor at the root of both psyche and matter:

> **Par. 786, 788** (directly read): "It therefore seems to me... wiser not to drag the supreme metaphysical factor into our calculations, at all events not at once, but, more modestly, to make an unknown psychic or perhaps *psychoid* factor in the human realm responsible for inspirations and suchlike happenings... it seems to me very much more cautious and reasonable to take cognizance of the fact that there is not only a psychic but also a *psychoid unconscious*, before presuming to pronounce metaphysical judgments which are incommensurable with human reason."

This is the load-bearing definitional move: the *unus mundus* is offered as a **probable working hypothesis**, grounded in the parallel structure of microphysics and depth psychology, not as a theological assertion. The "psychoid" qualifier on the archetype (see [[Archetype]]) is precisely what keeps the unus mundus a scientific postulate rather than a metaphysical claim — the same caution Jung exercises throughout [[Self (archetype)]]'s God-image discussion.
