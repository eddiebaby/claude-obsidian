# Privatio Boni

**Latin**: *privatio boni* — "privation of good," i.e. the absence or diminishment of good.

The doctrine, formalized by Basil the Great, Dionysius the Areopagite, and above all Saint Augustine, that **evil has no independent substance or existence of its own**. Evil is not a positive force but a deficiency — a hole where good ought to be, the way darkness is merely the absence of light or cold the absence of heat. Since God is *summum bonum* (the highest good) and the sole source of being, nothing that fully exists can be evil; what we call evil is being diminished, incomplete, or turned away from its proper end.

---

## Why the Doctrine Exists

*Privatio boni* solves an acute theological problem: if God is omnipotent and wholly good, how can evil exist at all without either limiting God's power or compromising God's goodness? Augustine's answer (developed explicitly against the Manichaeans, who held that light and darkness were two co-equal, substantially real cosmic principles) is that evil isn't a *thing* God would have had to create or permit as a rival force — it is simply the falling-away of created things from the good they were made to be. This lets theology keep God innocent of evil's origin while still explaining evil's reality as a lived experience: it is real as an absence, not as a presence.

## Descartes and the Doctrine

[[Rene-Descartes]] took the *privatio boni* directly from Augustine's *De Genesi ad litteram* and *De Genesi contra Manichaeos* — texts he was reading at the exact time of his three 1619 dreams, when he was himself considering writing a commentary on Genesis. In his *Cogitationes privatae* he writes that God is *intelligentia pura* ("pure intelligence") and that when God separated light from darkness, he separated the good angels from the evil ones; since the *privatio* cannot be separated from its "habitus" (habitual behavior), darkness and evil angels are only a privation and its form of existence.

Von Franz ([[vonfranz-dreams-1998]]) reads this as the unconscious inhibition that kept Descartes from investigating the mind-body problem, the *passiones*, and the reality of evil more deeply. His waking terror at the "ghosts" and his interpretation of the storm as "the influence of evil spirits" show the unconscious pressing the problem of evil on him directly — in the most dramatic and personal form — while his conscious theology had already foreclosed the possibility that evil could be anything but a shadow with no substance of its own. Descartes remained, in her phrase, "ensnared in a Christian prejudice": his Trinitarian mechanistic worldview asked no question about the Fourth — the totality that would have to include the reality of the dark.

## Jung's Critique

Jung's fullest argument against *privatio boni* is in [[jung-cw9ii-aion]]. Psychologically, the doctrine fails because the [[Shadow]] is experientially and clinically *real* — not a mere deficiency of consciousness but an active, autonomous force with its own energy, will, and capacity to erupt. A Self-symbol built on the premise that evil is "nothing" cannot integrate the shadow; it can only deny it. Jung's alternative symbols of the Self — the lapis, the round fish, the [[Coniunctio]] — explicitly *unite* light and dark, Christ and Antichrist, rather than defining one pole out of existence. See [[Self (archetype)]] for the full argument.

Jung also draws the geometric parallel that recurs throughout the Descartes material: the divine Trinity has a "lower," chthonic counter-triad which, "by reason of its symbolism, betrays affinities with evil, though it is by no means certain that it expresses nothing but evil." A quaternity split along its diagonal produces two opposing triads — the doctrine of the Trinity, applied without its shadow-complement, structurally *is* a doubled, one-sided triad. This dark spirit is the cause of collective catastrophe when its reality is denied, but rightly understood it can also become the *causa instrumentalis* of individuation.

---

## Why It Matters for Dream Interpretation

Von Franz treats *privatio boni* as a diagnostic marker: wherever a dreamer's (or a historical figure's) conscious theology insists that darkness is nothing-in-itself, the dream compensates by making darkness insistently, autonomously present — ghosts, storms, an evil spirit, ineradicable ambivalence. The unconscious does not observe the doctrine's logic; it treats evil as a real, active partner in the psyche's economy, whether or not the dreamer's conscious worldview has a category for it.
