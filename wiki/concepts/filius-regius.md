# Filius regius

> **Latin:** *filius* ("son") + *regius* ("royal, of the king") = **"the royal son."** Also *puellus regius* ("kingly boy") in the same texts.

---

## Definition

The renewed, transformed son who resolves the sick or sterile king's condition in the alchemical "king's renewal" trope — the product of the [[coniunctio-oppositorum]] and the endpoint of the [[Visio Arislei]]. Not simply the old king's heir: the *filius regius* is "unlike either parent," a genuinely new configuration rather than a repetition of what came before. Jung and the alchemists treat him as a synonym for the [[Lapis-Philosophorum|lapis]] in human/royal form — Michael Maier gives the *filius regius*'s own lament, "He lives and calls from the depths: Who shall deliver me from the waters and lead me to dry land?" (quoted in [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]], §3, pars. 181-184, in the context of [[Melusina]]).

## Psychological Reading

The *filius regius* is the psychological product of a successful union of opposites — not a return to a prior state but a third thing, unlike either contributing element. This is the standard Jungian reading of the coniunctio's offspring generally: a new configuration of consciousness that could not have been predicted from either starting position alone. His cry "from the depths" figures the emergent Self's own initial helplessness — newly born, still submerged, needing to be recognized and "delivered" into consciousness before it can do the renewing work the old king's kingdom needs.

## In This Source

[[ajuonuma2023-alchemical-oedipus]] identifies the *filius regius* with **Oedipus himself**. Beya (identified with Laius in the paper's androgyny argument) "births her brother, renewing Rex Marinus's kingdom; the son... is unlike either parent" — mapped onto Oedipus's death at Colonus, read as "a blessing for Athens (Theseus) and a curse for Thebes (Polynices, Creon)," Jean-Pierre Vernant's "Oedipus is double." Both the *filius regius* and Oedipus are read as **[[Pharmakon|pharmakon]]** — simultaneously remedy and poison: the *filius regius* "bestows on men all health and prosperity... but to the wicked, who abuse it, eternal punishment" (Waite 1678/1893, p.86), exactly mirroring Oedipus's double-edged legacy to Athens and Thebes. Ajuonuma's paper also notes the reborn king is sometimes named the ***puellus regius*** ("kingly boy"), which Jung reads as "an allegory of Christ."
