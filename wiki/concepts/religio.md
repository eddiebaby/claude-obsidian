# religio

Latin: "religion," in the sense of scrupulous, attentive observance — not (in Jung's preferred etymology) a doctrinal or institutional system, but a disposition of careful regard toward something that commands it.

Context: [[zeng2023-i-ching-jungian-application]] (Zeng, *JAP* 68/5, 2023), quoting Jung's 1950 foreword to the I Ching (CW 11, ¶982).

---

## Definition

*Religio* is the Latin noun conventionally translated "religion," but its derivation was already disputed in antiquity. Cicero (*De Natura Deorum* II.72) derives it from ***relegere*** — "to go over again," "to read/consider carefully," "to treat with scrupulous attention" — making *religio* fundamentally a matter of careful, repeated attentiveness. The Christian apologist Lactantius (*Divinae Institutiones* IV.28), by contrast, derives it from ***religare*** — "to bind fast" — making *religio* fundamentally a matter of obligation and binding-back to the divine. Jung explicitly adopts the Ciceronian derivation.

---

## Use in the Source

Discussing his own I Ching consultation about hexagram 50 (the Cauldron), Jung writes that the I Ching "conceives of itself as a cult utensil serving to provide spiritual nourishment for the unconscious elements or forces ('spiritual agencies') that have been projected as gods — in other words, to give these forces the attention they need in order to play their part in the life of the individual. Indeed, this is the original meaning of the word *religio* — a careful observation and taking account of (from *relegere*) the numinous" (Jung, 1950/1975, ¶982).

The move is load-bearing for Jung's third ("psychical") dimension of I Ching use (see [[I Ching Divination]]). Jung is not claiming the I Ching is a religion in the institutional sense. He is claiming that the *disposition* the practice requires — careful, repeated, attentive regard toward something numinous and not fully graspable — is what "religion" originally meant, before it hardened into doctrine and institution. On this reading, treating the I Ching's answers with the same scrupulous attention one would give a significant dream is not a superstitious lapse but a return to religion's root sense. This is the same move Jung makes elsewhere when he defines religion (CW 11, 1937) as "a careful consideration and observation of... the *numinosum*" — see [[Religious Function of the Psyche]] and [[Numinous Experience]], where the identical etymological argument grounds his structural-necessity thesis about the psyche's built-in religious function.
