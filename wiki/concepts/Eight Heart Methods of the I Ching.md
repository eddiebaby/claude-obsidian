# Eight Heart Methods of the I Ching

> **One-line definition:** An eight-stage interpretive system for reading a cast I Ching hexagram, originated by the I Ching scholar Ba Bao in the Yuan Dynasty (1271-1368), psychologized by the Chinese analyst Heyong Shen ("the first Chinese Jungian analyst"), and applied clinically to transference/countertransference material by [[Mengchao-Li|Mengchao Li]].

---

## Origin

The **Eight Heart Methods** (八心法) is a structured sequence for moving from a cast hexagram to a considered interpretation, promoted by the I Ching scholar-practitioner Ba Bao (Bao, 2005) during the Yuan Dynasty. Heyong Shen — described in [[zeng2023-i-ching-jungian-application]] as the first Chinese Jungian analyst — developed a psychologized version of Ba Bao's method, adding a psychological perspective to what was originally a "moral interiorization method" (道德內化法). [[Mengchao-Li|Mengchao Li]], trained in both analytical psychology and object relations, applies Shen's version clinically and discusses it at length in an unpublished 2019 manuscript.

The general question the method is built to answer, framed as a therapeutic inquiry: *"In the current context, which way of reaction is in accordance with the way of therapy?"*

---

## The Six Stages Documented by Li (of Eight)

Li's paper covers six of the eight stages in clinical detail; the final two ("clear about the Dao of changes" and "comprehend the three principles of changes") he omits as elaborating moral/revelatory meaning without direct clinical application.

### 1. Confirm the Major Yin-Yang Status

"The doorway of the I Ching, positioning through the hexagrams Qian and Kun, the dominant ones manifest clearly." Determines whether conscious (Qian) or unconscious (Kun) factors dominate the current clinical situation. Worked example: a client repeatedly getting hexagram 2 (Kun, The Receptive) — strongly unconscious-driven, carrying a fantasy that the therapist will be fully receptive and free of charge, in accordance with Kun's own qualities.

### 2. Study the Meaning of the Hexagram

"The River Map and the Luo Book, five is the number of heart, and heart brings enlightenment." Reflect on what the current situation actually means. In Li's case, the client's hexagram changed (*Bian Gua*, a standard I Ching technique) from Kun into hexagram 7 (Shih, The Army) — read as the therapist needing to act like "the efficient general who maintains obedience... by his authority" (Wilhelm's commentary on hexagram 7).

### 3. Read Each Line for Feeling/Relational Pattern

"Look at each line for feelings through engagement, dispositions of the trigrams, image and archetypes." Each line reveals a relational pattern. Li used the **Na Jia** technique (allocating hexagram positions to the ancient "heavenly stems" calendrical system) and identified himself, via the third and sixth lines, in the "offspring" position — meaning he was not identifying with the maternal image his clients projected onto him, despite feeling controlled and constrained by it.

### 4. Infer the Middle

"Timing, positioning and meaning, as the essential of Kan and Li, find the moment and the middle." Considers the correct position of self and object as the ideal therapeutic attitude, derived from whether a yin or yang line sits centrally within its trigram. In Li's case, a yin line at the top of Kun (a "natural," even-numbered position) validated the maternal projection structurally, even though Li — a male therapist — found it difficult to identify with the mother-image himself.

### 5. Research the Lines

"The metaphor of the hexagrams Xian (No. 31) and Heng (No. 32) shows the sympathetic connections behind [things]. Delve into the subtleties." Xian pairs a young woman and young man; Heng pairs thunder and wind — both illustrate constant, sympathetic pairing. The stage's purpose: understand each line and its potential changes within the clinical situation in finer detail.

### 6. Understand the Reason

"It is important to know loss and benefit, on heaven the Dao is full of empty, means the same on earth." The action stage: the therapist makes a moral/clinical choice. In Li's case, the therapist would either refer the client elsewhere or accept a submissive role in the therapy, given the pattern already diagnosed.

### 7-8 (Not Elaborated by Li)

Zeng notes the final two stages — "clear about the Dao of changes" (hexagrams Fu and inner truth) and "comprehend the three principles of changes" (hexagrams Jiji and Weiji) — are omitted from Li's clinical discussion as concerning moral meaning and revelation rather than practical technique.

## Zeng's Critique

Li's application is "creative and systematic," per Zeng, but does not clearly separate the different theoretical registers it draws on: the emphasis on projections and positions is Kleinian; the "Great Mother" and hero terminology is Jungian archetypal language; the content Li omits in stages seven and eight is purely Confucian. A reader benefits from a clearer accounting of which register is doing the work at each stage — a criticism paralleling Zeng's broader concern (see [[I Ching Divination]]) that Jungian engagement with the I Ching risks conflating Chinese cosmological concepts with imported Western psychological ones without marking the seam.
