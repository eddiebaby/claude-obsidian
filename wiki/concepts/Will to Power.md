# Will to Power

Nietzsche's term for the fundamental drive of all living things - not primarily the desire to dominate others but the tendency of life to discharge its strength, to grow, to expand, to overcome. The Will to Power (*Wille zur Macht*) is Nietzsche's replacement for Schopenhauer's pessimistic "will to live": where Schopenhauer saw life as blind striving toward survival and reproduction (ending in suffering), Nietzsche sees a fundamentally affirmative drive toward self-expression and self-overcoming.

Source: [[nietzsche-beyond-good-evil]] (throughout, especially Parts I, V, IX). Also central to *On the Genealogy of Morals*, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, and *The Will to Power* (posthumous notes).

---

## What the Will to Power Is Not

Nietzsche explicitly distinguishes Will to Power from:

**Mere survival drive**: Darwinian "will to self-preservation" is the minimum, the reaction of the weak. The Will to Power exceeds survival; it wants to grow even at risk to survival.

**Political or physical domination**: Nietzsche is not glorifying conquerors or bullies. The highest expression of Will to Power is self-overcoming - the philosopher who overcomes his own timidity and writes the hard truth; the artist who overcomes her convention to create something genuinely new.

**Conscious desire for power**: The Will to Power is pre-conscious; it operates in drives, affects, and interpretive habits. We do not "decide" to will power; the drive expresses itself through us, including in our intellectual and moral lives.

---

## Three Expressions of Will to Power

**In the body and instincts**: The spontaneous discharge of energy, pleasure, movement, growth. Health, in Nietzsche's sense, is the smooth expression of will to power. Illness and neurosis are blockages.

**In cognition**: All intellectual interpretation is an expression of Will to Power. The philosopher who imposes a system on reality, the scientist who reduces chaos to law, the moralist who judges - all are expressing a drive to command, to organize, to master. This is not a reductive criticism; it is a recognition that cognition is active, perspectival, driven.

**In morality**: Moral systems are expressions of the Will to Power of the groups that created them. Slave morality (resentment-based, equality-enforcing, excellence-punishing) is the Will to Power of those who cannot act directly; it achieves dominance through the indirect route of guilt, obligation, and moral condemnation.

## The Power Complex: Jung's Reception

Jung did not import the Will to Power wholesale but he engaged it directly, primarily through Adler's reformulation (Adler's "drive for superiority" is the individual-psychological version of the same insight).

**Jung's position**: The Will to Power is real but not primary. It is what fills the space where Eros (the capacity for genuine relatedness, love, vulnerability) has been blocked or undeveloped. The power-driven person is not someone with too much vitality but someone whose relational capacity has not developed - or has been wounded.

**The power complex in clinical terms**: When individuation is blocked, the ego's response is often inflation toward power. The person compensates for the felt inadequacy (the inability to relate genuinely) with a drive to command, control, impress, or be admired. This is Will to Power in its shadow form - not the clean discharge of strength that Nietzsche describes but the reactive substitute for something that is missing.

**Adler vs. Freud vs. Jung on power**: Freud centered sexuality; Adler centered power; Jung held that both are real but partial. In the first half of life, sexuality (Eros, relatedness, embodiment) is often the primary motivator. In the second half, something closer to the Will to Power - the drive toward meaning, self-expression, self-overcoming - comes to the fore. The pathological version of either is the shadow version: compulsion, possession, inflation.

## Self-Overcoming (*Selbstuberwindung*)

Nietzsche's highest expression of Will to Power is not conquest of others but self-overcoming. The philosopher overcomes his need for certainty. The artist overcomes her habit. The noble person overcomes their earlier values in the service of growth.

**Parallel to Jungian sacrifice**: Self-overcoming has a direct parallel to [[Psychological Sacrifice]] - the ego's surrender of its current form in the service of the larger psyche. The difference is in the agent: for Nietzsche, the ego is the hero of self-overcoming; for Jung, the ego serves the Self, and it is the Self that commands the sacrifice. This is the critical divergence that leads to the inflation problem.

**Why self-overcoming alone is insufficient**: If the ego is both the one who sacrifices and the author of the new values, there is no check on inflation. The ego can simply declare that it has overcome itself and arrived at a higher stage - with no external measure. In Jungian terms, the unconscious (dreams, symptoms, synchronicities) provides the check that pure self-overcoming lacks.

## Genealogical Analysis as Psychological Method

Nietzsche's genealogical method - asking not "is this value true?" but "what drive does this value serve, what psychological state does it express?" - directly anticipates the psychoanalytic hermeneutic of suspicion.

Jung's amplification method is different in direction (it enriches the image rather than reducing it to a drive) but shares the basic insight that surface meaning is not the whole meaning. Both Nietzsche and Jung operate from the assumption that the explicit content (the stated moral principle, the manifest dream image) points toward something not directly visible.

## Will to Power and the Shadow

One application to Jungian shadow work: what a person morally condemns (and condemns most vehemently) often contains their own disowned will to power. The person who most insistently denounces ambition may be most driven by it; the person who insists on equality may be most afraid of inferiority.

Nietzsche's genealogy of morality is, in clinical application, an invitation to ask: "What does my moral indignation tell me about what I have refused to own in myself?" This is the Jungian shadow question asked in Nietzsche's register.
