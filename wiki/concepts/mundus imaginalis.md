# mundus imaginalis

Latin: "the imaginal world." A term coined by the French Islamicist and philosopher Henry Corbin to translate the Islamic mystical concept *ʿālam al-mithāl* — an intermediate ontological realm between the purely sensory and the purely intelligible, in which visionary/imaginal events are neither subjective fantasy nor physical fact, but "really real" in their own right. Widely adopted in post-Jungian and archetypal psychology (notably [[James-Hillman|James Hillman]]) as a name for the autonomous imaginal register engaged in active imagination and dream work.

Context: [[JOAP-68-1-Charura-Bushell-Duoethnographic-Colonialism]] (*JAP* 68/1, 2023).

---

## Definition

The *mundus imaginalis* is not "imaginary" in the dismissive sense of "made up." Corbin's coinage insists on the reality of the imaginal as a genuine ontological order — the register in which archetypal images, visions, and dream figures have their own autonomous life, neither reducible to subjective invention nor confirmable by sense-perception. It is the theoretical home of the Jungian claim (echoed throughout this vault, e.g. [[Active Imagination]]) that unconscious figures must be treated as genuinely other, not as products the ego controls.

---

## Use in the Source

Charura & Bushell write of the later stage of decolonized therapeutic work: "Later, as the work stabilises, opening into the imaginative space becomes fertile ground for a truly non-colonized space, wherein the shared *mundus imaginalis* provides the freedom to explore the emerging material in a non-colonizing way." And: "In each therapeutic relationship, the imaginal field is created anew and is reverenced as both parties symbolically 'take the knee' to the emergent material."

The theoretical work the term does here: because the *mundus imaginalis* belongs to neither the therapist nor the client alone — it is a *shared*, autonomous field — it cannot be unilaterally claimed or authoritatively interpreted by either party the way discursive/verbal interpretive space can be. This is precisely why the authors treat it as the antidote to the [[Colonizer Archetype]] in clinical practice: colonizing requires one party's narrative to dominate another's, and the imaginal field's autonomy structurally resists that dominance.
