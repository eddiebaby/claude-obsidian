# Mandala Symbolism

The mandala (Sanskrit: "circle") is a geometric symbol of psychic wholeness. In Jung's analytical psychology, mandala imagery - circular forms with a center and typically a fourfold (quaternary) symmetry - is understood as the primary symbolic expression of the Self, the regulating center of the total psyche.

Source: [[jung-cw9i-archetypes-collective-unconscious]] (CW 9i), Section VI: "Concerning Mandala Symbolism" (54 examples analyzed) and "Appendix: Mandalas." Also: "A Study in the Process of Individuation" (the 24 paintings). Frontispiece is Jung's own 1916 mandala painting ("Mandala of a Modern Man").

---

## What a Mandala Is

A mandala in the Jungian sense is any circular image with an organized center that functions psychologically as an expression of wholeness. The form requirements are loose:
- A circle or circular boundary
- A center (explicit or implied)
- Some degree of symmetric organization around the center
- Often: fourfold (quaternary) division; sometimes threefold (but Jung saw this as incomplete) or eightfold

The frontispiece mandala ("Mandala of a Modern Man," painted by Jung in 1916) illustrates the basic structure: the microcosmic system enclosed within the macrocosmic system of opposites; figures at the four cardinal points; concentric rings converging on a central sun.

---

## Where Mandalas Appear

1. **Spontaneous dreams**: Circular images arise in dreams independent of cultural knowledge, often at moments of psychic disorientation.

2. **Active imagination**: The deliberate engagement with unconscious imagery (a Jungian therapeutic technique) frequently produces mandala forms.

3. **Psychotic episodes**: Patients in acute psychosis sometimes produce mandala drawings - understood by Jung as the psyche's attempt to re-establish order against chaos.

4. **Religious and cultural art**: Tibetan Buddhist thangkas, Hindu yantras, Christian rose windows, Celtic knotwork, Native American sand paintings, Navajo sand painting "shooting chants." The same structure appears worldwide.

5. **Architectural space**: Sacred spaces (temples, cathedrals) often embody mandala geometry in their ground plans.

---

## The Mandala as Self-Expression

Jung's central claim: the mandala is not primarily a cultural artifact but an autonomous psychic product. When the ego is destabilized - by life crisis, creative breakthrough, psychic disorientation, or the confrontation with deep unconscious contents - the psyche spontaneously produces mandala imagery as a stabilizing and centering response. The mandala is, in this sense, the psyche drawing its own portrait of wholeness.

From the frontispiece description: "The microcosmic enclosed within the macrocosmic system of opposites." The mandala holds the opposites together within a containing boundary. This is the essential function: the containment and integration of psychic opposites.

---

## The Quaternary

Jung repeatedly emphasizes the importance of fourfold symmetry. The four cardinal points, the four psychological functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition), the four elements, the four evangelists: the number four appears across traditions as the number of completeness in space and time. A triadic symbol (like the Christian Trinity) represents spiritual aspiration but leaves the material, chthonic, or feminine dimension out - which is why Jung saw the Catholic dogma of the Assumption of the Virgin (1950) as psychologically significant: it added a fourth element to the Trinity.

## "A Study in the Process of Individuation": 24 Paintings

The central case study of Section VI. An American patient produced 24 spontaneous paintings over several years. Jung's analysis traces the progressive development of mandala imagery:

- Early paintings: amorphous forms, unclear centers, anxiety-laden imagery
- Middle paintings: emergence of circular patterns, increasing symmetry, clearer centers
- Late paintings: fully centered, quaternary mandalas

Jung reads this as the autonomous movement of the [[Individuation]] process: the Self drawing itself into consciousness through the medium of the patient's spontaneous imagery.

## Jung's Own Mandalas

Jung painted his own mandala in 1916 (the frontispiece of CW 9i). In "Memories, Dreams, Reflections," he describes painting mandalas daily during a period of intense inner uncertainty (roughly 1913-1917, during his break with Freud and his "confrontation with the unconscious"). He did not initially understand what he was doing; only later did he recognize the mandala as the expression of the Self.

He later wrote to R.F.C. Hull that the painting reproduced in the frontispiece - and four others in CW 9i - were his own.

## The Mandala and the Brother Klaus Parallel

In the opening essay of CW 9i (read directly, paras 12-19), Jung cites the 15th-century Swiss mystic Brother Klaus (Nicholas of Flue), who had a terrifying vision of a "piercing light resembling a human face" - so overwhelming it changed his countenance permanently. Klaus eventually came to terms with this vision by elaborating it as a mandala divided into six parts (the Trinity picture in the Sachseln church). Jung's interpretation: the mandala functioned as a "protective circle" that transformed the raw, chaotic numinosity of the original vision into an assimilable religious form.

This illustrates the mandala's clinical function: not just symbolic meaning but psychic containment.

## CW 13 Deepening: Circumambulatio and Temenos

The *Commentary on the Secret of the Golden Flower* (CW 13, pars. 31–45) gives Jung's most detailed clinical account of mandala formation. Key structural concepts:

**Circumambulatio**: The ritual circling of a sacred centre. In alchemical texts this denotes the spiral movement around the *opus* — returning again and again to the same central problem from different angles, at increasing depth. In clinical mandala production, the patient's drawings circle the centre compulsively before the centre itself can be approached. The circumambulatio is the preparation for the central encounter.

**Sulcus primigenius** (primordial furrow): The furrow the Romans plowed to mark the boundary of a new city — both a physical act and a sacred rite. The act of defining a *temenos* by drawing a circle around it. Every city was founded as a mandala: a protected, oriented space carved out of undifferentiated nature. The mandala's circular boundary is the psychological equivalent of this founding act.

**Temenos** (Greek: sacred precinct): The enclosed space that separates sacred from profane, the protected ground within which the divine may appear. In psychological terms: the protected inner space within which the Self-process can unfold. The mandala draws the *temenos* around the emerging centre. This is its clinical function — not merely to represent wholeness but to create the psychic container in which it can develop.

**The centre reacts upon the maker**: The process is not purely creative in the sense of the ego producing the image. The mandala, once begun, exerts a pull; the centre draws the maker toward itself. The image is as much a cause as an effect.

## Ten European Patient Mandalas (CW 13 Plates)

Ten mandalas produced by Jung's patients, all independent of Eastern influence (earliest: 1916). They demonstrate that mandala imagery is an autonomous psychic product, not a cultural borrowing. Key examples:

| Plate | Caption |
|-------|---------|
| ♀ 1 | The Golden Flower represented as the most splendid of all flowers |
| ♀ 2 | In the centre, the Golden Flower; radiating out from it, fishes as fertility symbols (corresponding to the thunderbolts of Lamaic mandalas) |
| ♂ 3 | A luminous flower in the centre, with stars rotating about it; walls with eight gates; the whole conceived as a transparent window |
| ♀ 4 | Separation of the air-world and the earth-world (birds and serpents); in the centre, a flower with a golden star; I Ching hexagrams at the cardinal points |
| ♀ 5 | Separation of the light from the dark world; the heavenly from the earthly soul; in the centre, a representation of contemplation |
| ♂ 6 | In the centre, white light shining in the firmament; first circle: protoplasmic life-seeds; second: rotating cosmic principles with four primary colours; third/fourth: creative forces working inward and outward; at cardinal points: masculine and feminine souls, each divided into light and dark |
| ♀ 7 | Representation of the *tetraktys* in circular movement (four figures chasing each other) |
| ♀ 8 | A child in the germinal vesicle with the primary colours included in the circular movement (yin-yang pattern with child) |
| ♀ 9 | In the centre, the germinal vesicle with a human figure nourished by cosmic blood vessels; the cosmos rotates around the centre; outer border: nerve tissue indicating the process takes place in the solar plexus |
| ♂ 10 | A mandala as a fortified city with wall and moat; within, sixteen towers; central castle with golden roofs; innermost: a golden temple |

Notable: Plate 2 shows fishes radiating from the Golden Flower centre — the same structural position as the thunderbolts in Tibetan Lamaic mandalas. A patient with no knowledge of either tradition spontaneously produced the same structural solution. This is the cross-cultural universality of the mandala in action.

Plate 8 (child in germinal vesicle) = the [[Diamond-Body]] in its seed form — the new centre being born within the circular field.

Plate 10 (fortified city) = the *sulcus primigenius* made explicit. The mandala as founding act of a city: wall, moat, gates, towers, inner sanctum.

## Jaffé: Mandala in Architecture and Urban Space (Man and His Symbols)

Source: [[jung-man-and-his-symbols]], Part 4.

Every building or city with a mandala ground plan is a projection of the wholeness archetype onto the outer world. The mandala does not stay inside dreams and art — it organizes how humans build their collective home.

**Rome as urbs quadrata**: The founding of Rome is a mandala ritual. Romulus:
1. Dug the *mundus* pit — a round hole into which offerings were cast for the gods of the underworld. The hole was covered with a large flat stone (the "soul stone," *lapis manalis*). On three specific days per year the stone was removed to release the spirits of the dead.
2. Harnessed a bull and cow and plowed a circle around the site — the *sulcus primigenius* (primordial furrow), which defined the sacred boundary.
3. Divided the settlement with two main arteries crossing at right angles (the *cardo* and *decumanus*), leading to four gates.

The city = circle (plowed boundary) + square (four quarters) + center (mundus) = mandala. Every Roman city followed this pattern; it was religious, not merely practical.

**Medieval cities**: surrounded by circular or polygonal walls, two main streets creating four quarters, cathedral or church at the intersection. The ideal form derived from **Revelation 21** (the Heavenly Jerusalem — square walls, twelve gates, the throne of God at the center). The city as earthly image of the cosmic mandala.

**Washington D.C.**: modern secular example; the radial street plan converging on the Capitol, the Mall as axial cross.

**Angkor Wat**: square temple complex, circular enclosures at four corners, central tower aligned to the cosmic axis.

**Paris, Place de l'Étoile**: twelve avenues radiating from a central point; a star mandala at the heart of the modern European city.

**The alchemists** worked the same territory as a spiritual problem: the *quadratura circuli* (squaring of the circle) was their symbol for the union of opposites — male and female, spirit and matter — at the center of the Great Work. The squared circle = the mandala = wholeness achieved through opus.

## Jaffé: Circle in Modern Art and the Dissociated Psyche

Source: [[jung-man-and-his-symbols]], Part 4.

In medieval and Renaissance art, the circle held its honored position as the symbol of perfection and the divine (rose windows, halos, the Last Judgment in tondo form). In modern painting, the circle has been:
- **Dethroned** from its formal dominance
- **Scattered** across the canvas asymmetrically
- **Separated** from the square and the center

Jaffé reads this as a symptom: "The separation of circle and square in modern art corresponds to the psychic state of 20th-century man: his soul has lost its roots and he is threatened by dissociation."

**Specific examples**:
- **Delaunay**: sun disks scattered as asymmetrical, floating planes of color — the solar symbol untethered
- **Matisse** (*Still Life with Vase of Nasturtiums*): green sphere on a black rectangular beam; circle touching rectangle but not integrated with it — "The two figures that from the beginning of time have formed a whole are torn apart"
- **Kandinsky**: loose assemblies of circles drifting "like soap bubbles" — beautiful, but not organized around a center
- **Klee** (*Limits of Understanding*): a simple sphere placed above a complex scaffolding of ladders and lines — the symbol of wholeness hovering above a structure that cannot reach it
- **Mondrian**: pure squares and rectangles; no circles at all; the center absent — "the harmony of harmonious compositions with squares"

The artists were not making this choice consciously. Their unconscious psyches were reproducing, in visual form, the collective state: the symbol of wholeness present but dissociated, the center yearned for but not held.

**Healing movement**: mid-century, the trend reversed. Artists began explicitly seeking the union of opposites. Pierre Soulages painted cataract veils of black behind which a clear blue or radiant yellow slowly emerged: "Light is dawning behind darkness." The Church became patron of modern art (Matisse's chapel at Vence; Rouault's windows at Assy). Alfred Manessier: "We have to reconquer the weight of lost reality... a new soul, in the measure of man."

## UFOs as Projected Wholeness

Source: [[jung-man-and-his-symbols]], Part 4. Full treatment in Jung's *Flying Saucers: A Modern Myth* (1958).

The "flying saucer" phenomenon appeared in mass reports beginning after World War II — a period of maximum psychic dissociation (East/West split, atomic threat, the apparent meaninglessness of industrial civilization). Jung's interpretation: the UFO is primarily a **visionary rumor** — a collective projection of the unconscious's drive to restore wholeness.

The round, disk-shaped object = the mandala = the symbol of psychic wholeness that the collective unconscious is urgently attempting to generate. The object is seen "up there" rather than felt inside because the projection mechanism casts inner content onto the outer sky when the inner connection to wholeness is broken.

The "technically perfect" quality that witnesses report (seamless, metallic, precise, emitting radiation) = the archetype's numinosity experienced as something impossibly competent — superior to human technology. The unconscious projects its own transcendent completeness onto an object beyond current human capacity.
