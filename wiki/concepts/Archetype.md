# Archetype

The archetype is Jung's term for the structural predispositions that constitute the contents of the [[Collective Unconscious]]. An archetype is not itself an image; it is a tendency to form images of a certain type. The specific image that appears in a given dream or myth is an "archetypal image" - the archetype's particular expression in a particular consciousness at a particular time.

Source: [[jung-cw9i-archetypes-collective-unconscious]] (CW 9i) - directly read (paras 4-7 of opening essay).

> [!contradiction] Contested by [[Deconstructing Archetype Theory]] (Roesler 2023/2025) and independently by [[Raya Jones|Jones]] (2023)
> This page presents the archetype in Jung's own terms — a biologically-rooted, universal structural predisposition ("analogous to a crystal lattice," "universal images that have existed since the remotest times"). [[Christian-Roesler|Christian Roesler]] argues that "archetype theory" is really **four separable theories** (biological, anthropological, transcendental, process) and that the biological and anthropological strands — including exactly the crystal-lattice/universality framing below — are **refuted by contemporary genetics, anthropology, and archaeology**; what is innate are capacities for relationship and sociality, not preformed images. Roesler keeps only the *process* strand, demoted from biological fact to a **clinical hermeneutics**. This is a live scholarly disagreement, not a settled correction. See [[Deconstructing Archetype Theory]] and [[roesler2025-process-of-transformation]] for the full argument; the sections below preserve Jung's own position.
>
> [[jones2023-dialectics-sign-symbol-archetype|Jones (2023)]] arrives at the same target — Jung's germ-plasm/hereditary grounding of archetypes — by a different route (semiotics, not genetics/anthropology), and quotes an earlier (2012) Roesler paper making the identical point: "we Jungians cannot go on basing our theory of archetypes on scientific assumptions which have been falsified.… It is important that we stop arguing that archetypes are transmitted genetically if we want to be taken seriously." Jones's positive alternative does not overlap with Roesler's process-hermeneutics: she proposes relocating the archetypal in the **probabilistic patterning of the experiential realm** (via Gibson's affordances) rather than a pre-existing structure "switched on" like an ignition key. See §Sign, Symbol, and the Categorization Problem below and [[Sign and Symbol]].

## Etymology and Philosophical History

From the text (para 5, read directly): "The term 'archetype' occurs as early as Philo Judaeus, with reference to the Imago Dei (God-image) in man. It can also be found in Irenaeus, who says: 'The creator of the world did not fashion these things directly from himself but copied them from archetypes outside himself.' In the Corpus Hermeticum, God is called the archetypal light. The term occurs several times in Dionysius the Areopagite."

In St. Augustine the word is absent but the idea is present: "ideae principales, 'which are themselves not formed ... but are contained in the divine understanding.'" Jung notes that "Archetype is an explanatory paraphrase of the Platonic eidos" - the Platonic form or idea. For Jung the term refers to "primordial types" that are "universal images that have existed since the remotest times" (para 5).

## Archetype vs. Archetypal Image

This distinction is critical and often missed in popular usage:

- **The archetype-as-such**: An invisible structural form; a predisposition; analogous to a crystal lattice that will organize its elements in a predictable structure. Cannot be directly perceived; can only be inferred.
- **The archetypal image**: What actually appears - in dream, vision, myth, or art. Always particular, always colored by the individual consciousness and cultural context in which it appears.

From the text (para 6): "The archetype is essentially an unconscious content that is altered by becoming conscious and by being perceived, and it takes its colour from the individual consciousness in which it happens to appear."

## Archetypes and Myth

Jung argues emphatically that myths are not allegories of natural phenomena (solar, lunar, meteorological). From the text (para 7, read directly): "The fact that myths are first and foremost psychic phenomena that reveal the nature of the soul is something they have absolutely refused to see until now. Primitive man is not much interested in objective explanations of the obvious, but he has an imperative need - or rather, his unconscious psyche has an irresistible urge - to assimilate all outer sense experience to inner, psychic events."

Mythologized natural processes (the sun's journey, the seasons, the phases of the moon) are "symbolic expressions of the inner, unconscious drama of the psyche which becomes accessible to man's consciousness by way of projection." The projection onto natural phenomena is primary; the natural events serve as a screen for inner experience.

## The Paradox: Universal Form, Individual Expression

The archetype operates like an instinct: it presses toward a typical kind of experience or behavior, but its specific form is always shaped by the individual and cultural context. Two people dreaming of the "mother" archetype may produce radically different imagery, yet both images draw on the same underlying structural predisposition.

This is why Jung's method involves "amplification": placing a specific dream image alongside parallel images from mythology, alchemy, and art - not to explain the image by reducing it to a known symbol, but to illuminate its meaning by showing what psychic territory that structural pattern typically governs.

## Numinosity

Archetypal images carry what Jung (following Rudolf Otto) calls "numinosity" - a quality of sacred dread, fascination, and awe. When an archetype is activated - when it erupts into a dream or vision or waking experience - the subjective response is unmistakably different from ordinary cognition. This numinosity is Jung's evidence that something of real psychic weight is at work, not merely a personal association.

## Sign, Symbol, and the Categorization Problem

Source: [[jones2023-dialectics-sign-symbol-archetype]] (Jones, 2023) - directly read.

The word "archetype" itself lives a dual life, per Jung's own strict dichotomy — "an expression that stands for a known thing remains a mere sign and is never a symbol" (Jung, 1921, para. 817). As a **sign**, "archetype" is a technical term known to trained psychologists. As a **symbol**, it stands for something Jung himself calls essentially "irrepresentable" (para. 417) — the archetype-as-such, as opposed to any particular archetypal image. Full treatment: [[Sign and Symbol]].

This duality generates a real categorization problem, admitted by Jung himself: "clear-cut distinctions and strict formulations are quite impossible in this field, seeing that a kind of fluid interpenetration belongs to the very nature of all archetypes" (Jung, 1949, para. 301). Concretely: Jung classified the same rebirth motif under **Mother** symbolism in 1912/1952 (*Symbols of Transformation*) but under **Individuation** in a 1939 lecture on the Khidr legend. There is no principled criterion in Jung's own corpus for deciding which archetype a given motif belongs to — the interpretive fluidity that makes archetypal amplification generative also makes archetypal *classification* logically unstable.

[[Charles Sanders Peirce|Peirce's]] icon/index/symbol trichotomy ([[Peircean Semiotics]]) is a useful comparison precisely because it does *not* map onto Jung's dichotomy — a picture of a mythological monster is a Peircean icon (representing by resemblance) but a Jungian symbol (standing for something psychologically unknown). Jones extends the comparison: archetype theory itself functions like a Peircean index (a signpost legible only to those trained to read it) — "one must speak Jungianism in order to follow the signage to specific archetypes."

Jones's own reconstructive proposal, distinct from Roesler's process-hermeneutics above: relocate the archetypal not in a pre-existing structure switched on by circumstance, but in a **probabilistic patterning of the experiential realm** (not every person who enters a cavernous cathedral experiences it as womblike) — using Gibson's ecological theory of *affordances* as the bridge between physical constraint (water does not afford walking) and symbolic expression (Jesus walking on water as the symbolic register of an impossible feat).
