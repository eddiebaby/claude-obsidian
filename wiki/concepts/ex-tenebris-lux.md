# Ex Tenebris Lux

**Ex tenebris lux** ("out of darkness, light") is the Latin paradox that closes Jung's Essay III on Paracelsus in CW 13 — the final judgment on Paracelsus as a spiritual phenomenon. Jung uses it to name the fundamental alchemical and psychological law: the light does not descend from above but arises from within the darkness itself.

Primary CW 13 source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]] Essay III, Epilogue, par. 238.

---

## The Passage

Jung closes Essay III with the fullest statement of Paracelsus's paradox (par. 238):

> "*Ex tenebris lux* — it was precisely because he was so fascinated by magic that he was able to open the door to the realities of nature for the benefit of succeeding centuries."

The argument: Paracelsus's "weakness" — his immersion in magic, folk superstition, hapax legomena, sorcery accusations, unconscious power-striving — was simultaneously the condition of his "strength" as the pioneer of empirical medicine and psychology. His darkness was the ground of his light.

---

## The Paradox's Logic

*Ex tenebris lux* is not a simple reversal (darkness is actually good). It states something more precise:

1. Light from above (divine revelation, *cognitio matutina*) is one source of illumination
2. But the specifically alchemical / *lumen naturae* light arises **from within** the darkness — from the *prima materia*, from the Saturnine heaviness, from the unconscious itself
3. Paracelsus could only access the second kind of light because he was genuinely immersed in the darkness — not despite his entanglement with magic but through it

This connects directly to the [[Lumen-Naturae]]: Paracelsus's *lumen naturae* ("light of nature") is the light that is already present within darkness, not a light descending to illuminate it from outside. "In the very darkness of nature a light is hidden, a little spark without which the darkness would not be darkness." (par. 197)

---

## Structural Parallel to the Alchemical Opus

The *ex tenebris lux* law structures the entire *opus alchymicum*:

| Stage | Alchemical | Psychological |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| *Nigredo* | Blackening; prima materia in its darkest state; putrefaction | The initial encounter with the shadow; depression; the contents most feared and avoided |
| *Albedo* | The white that emerges from the black; the light within the dark | First integration of shadow contents; dawning of the interior light |
| *Rubedo* | The red of completion | Full realization; the *lumen naturae* fully operative |

The light (*albedo*, *lumen naturae*, *stella matutina*) does not come from outside the *nigredo* — it is extracted from within it. *Ex tenebris lux*: the darkness is the ground of the light, not its enemy.

---

## Paracelsus as Exemplar

Paracelsus "consisted of two persons who never really confronted one another" (par. 155): the pious Christian physician and the magic-saturated alchemical philosopher. His failure to integrate this split was what produced the "daemonic creative energy" that Jung traces directly to Goethe's Faust and Nietzsche's Zarathustra (par. 163).

> "As a man he had one father, but as a spirit he had two mothers. His spirit was 'heroic, because creative, and as such was doomed to Promethean guilt.' The secular conflict at the turn of the sixteenth century was 'prerequisite for higher consciousness; for **analysis is always followed by synthesis, and what was divided on a lower level will reappear, united, on a higher one.**'"

*Ex tenebris lux* is not just a statement about Paracelsus — it is Jung's law of psychological development: the split, the conflict, the darkness that seems to be a failure of integration is the very material from which higher consciousness is synthesized.
