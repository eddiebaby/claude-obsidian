# Psychological Sacrifice

Sacrifice in the Jungian sense is not the offering of something external to appease a deity but an inner surrender: the sacrifice of the ego-bound identity, the old consciousness, the familiar but constraining sense of self. It is the psychological necessity at the core of transformation and individuation.

Source: [[jung-cw5-symbols-transformation]] (CW Vol. 5), Part Two, Chapter VIII: "The Sacrifice." Central to Jung's understanding of individuation and psychic transformation.

---

## What Must Be Sacrificed

**The identification with the ego**: The ego believes itself to be the totality of the self. It identifies with the persona (the social mask) and defends this identity fiercely. Individuation requires that the ego surrender this illusion - that it discover it is not the whole, that there is something larger (the Self) that knows and directs more than ego can.

**Ego's pretense to autonomy**: The developing ego must rebel against the mother (literal or archetypal) to establish independence. But mature consciousness recognizes that autonomy is illusory. The ego is not self-sufficient; it participates in the total psyche and in the collective.

**The familiar identity**: The ego has built a life around certain identifications: "I am the competent one," "I am the good one," "I am the victim," "I am the rebel." Transformation requires letting go of these fixed self-concepts. This is experienced as a kind of death.

**Control and certainty**: The ego wants to control the psyche and eliminate uncertainty. Individuation requires surrender of this wish - trusting the unconscious process that is larger than personal will.

---

## Sacrifice as a Psychological Requirement

Jung emphasizes that this is not optional or pathological. Psychic transformation requires sacrifice. It is the universal pattern:

- In mythology: the hero must sacrifice (or have sacrificed for him) the old identity. Christ's crucifixion is sacrifice. Mithras dies and is reborn. Osiris is murdered and restored.

- In religious experience: conversion, initiation, mystical death-and-rebirth. The saints speak of dying to self. The Buddha teaches non-self (anatman).

- In individual psychology: the neurotic symptom often signals that something wants to be sacrificed but the ego resists. The symptom is the psyche's way of forcing the sacrifice.

- In analysis: the patient often reports dreams of death, destruction, dismemberment - the unconscious preparing for transformation.

---

## The Sacrifice as Offering

Traditionally, sacrifice is an offering made with intention. This adds a crucial element: the psychological sacrifice is not something that happens to you but something you do. It is an act of will aligned with the deeper intention of the psyche.

This is why Jung emphasizes consciousness in individuation. You cannot simply be passively swept along. You must consent to the death of the old identity. You must make the offering consciously.

Example: A man identifies entirely with his role as breadwinner and provider. Analysis reveals that this identity cuts him off from his feelings, his creativity, his inner life. The fear of losing this identity is enormous - it feels like annihilation. But individuation requires that he sacrifice this narrow identity, accept the vulnerability and groundlessness that comes with relinquishing it, and discover a more complete self beneath.

The sacrifice is real. He loses something: the certainty, the social recognition, the clear role. But he gains something larger: wholeness, authenticity, capacity for genuine relationship.

---

## Sacrifice and the Depressive Position

Jung's understanding of sacrifice has affinities with Kleinian psychology's "depressive position" - the psychological state in which the subject recognizes ambivalence, loss, the limitations of the self and others. But where Klein sees this as a developmental hurdle, Jung sees it as a spiritual necessity.

The capacity to sacrifice is also the capacity to mourn, to accept loss, to recognize that nothing in ego-consciousness is permanent. This realization is initially depressing (hence the "depressive" in Klein's terminology). But it is also liberating: if ego-identity is not permanent, I am not bound to it.

## The Symbolic Language of Sacrifice

Sacrifice appears in Jungian symbolism through:

**Death imagery**: The grave, the underworld, dismemberment, drowning. These are not morbid fantasies but the psyche's way of rendering the death of the old self.

**Burning and fire**: Purification through destruction. The old must burn away for the new to appear.

**The wounded healer**: The figure (Christ, Prometheus, Chiron) who has been sacrificed and bears the wound. The wound is the mark of having undergone transformation.

**The crucifixion**: The crossing-out of ego, the intersection of personal and transpersonal, the point at which surrender occurs.

**Blood**: Sacrifice traditionally involves blood - life force. Psychological sacrifice is not bloodless. It is costly, it involves real loss.

---

## The Scapegoat Complex

Jung notes a crucial danger: the ego can misuse sacrifice language. If one person in a group (or in the psyche) is designated as the sacrificial victim while others remain exempt, the real work is avoided. The scapegoat mechanism allows the ego to feel purified while evading its own necessary death.

True sacrifice is personal: each must sacrifice their own ego-identification. This cannot be done for you or instead of you. And it cannot be projected onto others (making them the victims so you remain pure).

---

## Sacrifice in the Second Half of Life

Jung emphasizes that sacrifice becomes more necessary and more possible in the second half of life. The first half is about building ego-consciousness, establishing identity, achieving social and professional success. The second half requires that ego step aside and allow the Self to lead.

This is experienced as loss - loss of vitality, of future promise, of the illusion of unlimited potential. It must be mourned. But this sacrifice creates space for something deeper: meaning, integration, wisdom, wholeness.

The person who cannot sacrifice in the second half lives in denial, desperately clinging to youth and earlier identity. The result is often a kind of psychic stagnation or literalization (the old ways insisted upon rigidly, without flexibility or growth).

---

## Sacrifice and Love

Jung notes that genuine love requires sacrifice: the ego's willingness to surrender its separateness, to be transformed by encounter with another. The person incapable of sacrifice is incapable of love. This is why neurotic symptomatology often involves both: the inability to love and the inability to undergo necessary change.

True Eros (in contrast to ego-possession or dependency) is sacrificial: I give something of myself, not to gain control but to participate in something larger than myself.
