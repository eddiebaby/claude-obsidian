# Utterance (Bakhtin)

[[Mikhail Bakhtin|Bakhtin's]] analytic unit of communication, used by [[Raya Jones|Jones]] (2023) to frame the entire hundred-year history of Jung's archetype theory as a single, still-unfolding communicative act rather than a static doctrine to be judged simply true or false.

## The Definition

An utterance can be "anything from a 'short (single word) rejoinder in everyday dialogue' to the large novel or scientific treatise," and has "an absolute beginning and an absolute end: its beginning is preceded by the utterances of others, and its end is followed by the responsive utterances of others" (Bakhtin, 1986, p. 71). No utterance is self-contained; it is always a reply to what came before and a provocation of what comes after.

## Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Every utterance is pulled by two simultaneous forces (Bakhtin, 1981, p. 272):

- **Centrifugal forces** — "social and historical *heteroglossia*": the stratifying, diversifying pull of the many contexts, communities, and eras through which the utterance passes and is reinterpreted.
- **Centripetal forces** — the pull toward the "unitary language": a single, fixed, agreed-upon semantic core.

Jones applies this directly to "archetype": the centrifugal force throws interpreters into "attempts to reconcile Jung's theory with contemporary trends, social sensitivities, and scientific advances," while the centripetal force "keeps us fixated on the semantic meaning of the word *archetype* as an 'original pattern or model from which copies are made'" (OED).

## Why This Framing Matters

Treating archetype theory as an utterance rather than a fixed proposition reframes the entire debate surveyed in [[Archetype]] and [[Deconstructing Archetype Theory]]:

- It explains *why* the theory keeps being reformulated (Stevens's biological-entity reading, Maloney's evolutionary-compatibility reading, Roesler's four-strand deconstruction, Becker & Neuberg's cognitive-science reading) without any one reformulation ever "winning" outright — each is a responsive utterance in an ongoing exchange, not a final verdict.
- It relocates the interesting question from "is archetype theory true?" to "what is archetype theory *doing*, communicatively, for the community that keeps using and revising it?" — a move consistent with Jones's broader argument that Jung's own history-of-philosophy account of the archetype concept (Plato → Augustine → Kant → Schopenhauer → Jung) is itself "less a *history* of an idea than … the *story* of an archetype, in his sense of the term" (Jones, 2003, p. 657) — the theory performing its own thesis.
- It sits alongside [[Sign and Symbol]] as the second organizing frame of the source paper: sign/symbol names the concept's *internal* dual function; utterance names its *external*, historically extended existence as discourse.

Jones closes with a related concept from cultural sociologist Bouchard (2017): the **collective imaginary** — the "deepest symbolic structures" underlying a collectivity's norms, traditions, and narratives. Archetype theory's utterance "ripples" differently within the collective imaginary of analytical psychology than it does among non-Jungian psychologists, for whom it is at odds with the discipline's imaginary of science and scholarship.
