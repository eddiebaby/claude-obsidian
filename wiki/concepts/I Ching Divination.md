# I Ching Divination

> **One-line definition:** The ancient Chinese Book of Changes, used by Jung across four decades as a practical divinatory tool operating in three distinct dimensions — physical (previewing outer events), psychological (revealing one's own state), and psychical (engaging spiritual agencies) — and carried forward by contemporary Jungian analysts into clinical practice.

---

## What the I Ching Is

The *I Ching* (易經, "Book of Changes"; classically the *Zhouyi* 周易) is a Chinese divinatory and philosophical text built around 64 hexagrams — six-line figures formed by stacking two of eight three-line trigrams. A questioner casts a hexagram (traditionally with 50 yarrow stalks, or more casually with three coins) and reads the resulting text — the Judgement, the Image, and commentary on whichever individual lines are "moving" (about to change into their opposite) — as an answer to a question put to the oracle.

Chinese tradition credits the legendary emperor Fuxi with the eight trigrams (inspired by the mythical "River Map," or He Tu, brought by a dragon-horse), King Wen of the Zhou Dynasty (1046-256 BCE) with combining them into the 64 hexagrams, and Confucius with the first written commentaries (the *Yizhuan*). The scholarly study of the text and its interpretive traditions is called **Yi-ology**, historically split between two schools:

- **Image-number school** (*Xiang Shu*, 象數): divinatory; interprets the I Ching through the images, numbers, and structural relationships of trigrams and hexagrams themselves — interlocking trigrams, Yin-yang waxing/waning, the *Na Jia* (heavenly-stems/earthly-branches) calendrical-correspondence system, and hexagram timelines.
- **Philosophical school** (*Yi Li*, 義理): textual; treats the Confucian *Yizhuan* commentaries as primary, giving comparatively little weight to non-textual divinatory technique.

Jung's own engagement, per [[Jingchao Zeng]]'s analysis, sits closer to the image-number school: technique-oriented and practical rather than philological.

---

## Jung's Discovery and Engagement

Jung dated his own I Ching studies to 1919, first encountering the text on a journey to visit Scottish psychiatrist Maurice Nicoll. His engagement deepened sharply after meeting the German sinologist and translator [[Richard-Wilhelm|Richard Wilhelm]] in November 1920 — Jung invited Wilhelm to give a club seminar on I Ching divination the following year (December 15, 1921) and worked initially from James Legge's 1899 English edition (which he found inadequate — Legge, he noted, apparently never "bothered to put the method to a practical test") until Wilhelm's own German translation appeared in 1924. Jung's fullest mature reflections on the method are in his 1950 foreword to the English Wilhelm-Baynes edition (CW 11, ¶¶965-1014).

An episode that frames Jung's whole stance: in the mid-1930s he asked the modernizing Chinese philosopher [[Hu-Shih|Hu Shih]] — trained under John Dewey at Columbia, a pioneer of modern Chinese philosophy — what he thought of the I Ching. Hu dismissed it as "nothing but an old collection of magic spells, without significance," then admitted, when pressed, that he had consulted the oracle himself and gotten a sensible answer. Jung recorded his disappointment thirty years later: Hu Shih, a Chinese scholar, was "ashamed to know anything of the I Ching," while Jung, a European psychologist, insisted on taking Chinese tradition's own practical/divinatory dimension seriously rather than discarding it for modernization's sake. The irony is structural, not incidental: Hu's own doctoral thesis (Columbia, 1917, supervised by Dewey) had treated the I Ching only as a logic text, deliberately setting aside its divinatory use as part of modernizing Chinese philosophy for a Western audience.

## The Three Dimensions of Jung's Practical Use

Zeng's central taxonomic contribution, built from close reading of Jung's letters, seminars, and the 1950 foreword.

### 1. Physical Dimension — Previewing Outer Reality

Using the I Ching to preview the future potentials of real-world events. Jung's clearest case: before his 1925 journey to Africa, he threw hexagram 53 (Gradual Process), landing on line 3 — "the man goes forth and does not return." Fellow passengers who settled in Africa on that trip in fact died within months of arrival (recorded in [[Barbara-Hannah|Barbara Hannah]]'s biography of Jung, 1976). Wilhelm's commentary on the same line ("If we do not let things develop quietly but plunge of our own choice too rashly into a struggle, misfortune results") also anticipated the near-disaster of Jung's own overland crossing of Uganda, rescued at the last moment by a passing truck. A second case, recorded by [[Aniela-Jaffe|Aniela Jaffé]]: Jung asked Wilhelm to cast a hexagram diagnosing the state of Zurich's Psychological Club — a situation known to Jung but not Wilhelm — "the diagnosis that resulted was startlingly correct, and so was the prognosis."

### 2. Psychological Dimension — Revealing One's Own State

Jung: "When I throw a handful of peas, they will roll in all directions. Try to interpret this, and you will understand the importance of the moment... you will see how remarkably well the meaning of the I Ching matches the psychological situation" (1987/2008, p. 205). He observed clinically that the trigram **K'an** (water/the abyss) tended to turn up for patients dangerously overwhelmed by unconscious material, threatened with psychotic breakthrough. Writing the 1950 foreword itself, Jung cast K'an doubled ("abyss on abyss") and read it as an accurate self-diagnosis of the risk of "limitless and uncritical speculation" in the very text he was writing.

### 3. Psychical Dimension — the I Ching as an Animated Being

Jung's own theoretical addition, beyond the Chinese textual tradition proper: the claim that ***shen*** (神, "spiritual agencies," James Legge's "spirit-like things") animate the yarrow stalks, giving the I Ching "the living soul of the book" and licensing questioners to treat it as an intelligent interlocutor. Jung's demonstration case: consulting the I Ching about whether to publish the English translation at all, he drew hexagram 50, the **Cauldron** (*ding*, 鼎), and read the vessel image as the I Ching describing itself as a "cult utensil serving to provide spiritual nourishment for the unconscious elements or forces... that have been projected as gods." Jung ties this explicitly to the etymology of *religio* itself — see [[religio]] and [[relegere]] — arguing that careful, attentive regard for the numinous *is* religion's original meaning, and that I Ching divination is therefore a genuinely religious act in this sense. This is the dimension Zeng flags as most likely to conflate Chinese cosmology with Jungian psychological theory rather than faithfully representing the former — Jung is not merely reporting a Chinese concept here, he is actively theorizing beyond it.

## Contemporary Jungian Clinical Practice

Three practitioners carry Jung's model forward, each contributing a distinct method:

- **[[Jean-Kirsch|Jean Kirsch]]**: a disciplined three-phase practice (write the situation honestly → cast the hexagram → read and reflect symbolically) built from 45+ years of personal use; her published countertransference case (a client's decision to leave for Jungian training) is read by Zeng as touching all three of Jung's dimensions simultaneously.
- **[[Mengchao-Li|Mengchao Li]]**: applies the psychologized **[[Eight Heart Methods of the I Ching]]** (developed by Heyong Shen from the Yuan-dynasty scholar Ba Bao's original system) to transference/countertransference material, blending Jungian, Kleinian, and Confucian frameworks.
- **[[Dennis-Merritt|Dennis Merritt]]**: two clinical formats — a single three-hour consultation (clarify the real question, cast via yarrow stalks as itself a meditative act, read across three English translations) and an ongoing format for I-Ching-literate clients — plus an explicit link between the I Ching's holistic Chinese worldview, Jung's [[Unus Mundus]], and physicist David Bohm's implicate order.

Zeng's overall critical verdict, having surveyed all three: genuine clinical potential, demonstrated by Jung's own decades of serious, non-dogmatic use — but each contemporary practitioner imports at least one unexamined cross-cultural conflation (translation slippage between Chinese *junzi* and the Jungian wise-old-man archetype; unmarked mixing of Jungian/Kleinian/Confucian registers; philosophically questionable claims about the I Ching's native metaphysics of time). "Numerous issues of intercultural communication must be clarified to prevent misunderstandings" before wider clinical adoption.

## Relation to Synchronicity

The I Ching's working mechanism — a symbolically apt answer arising from a physically random process (yarrow-stalk division, coin tosses) at the moment a question is posed — is structurally the same claim as [[Synchronicity]]: a meaningful correspondence between an inner state (the question, the psychological situation) and an outer event (the hexagram cast) without direct causal connection. But Jung never subjected the I Ching to a statistical test the way he did with astrology (see [[Synchronicity]]'s account of the failed marriage-horoscope experiment). His I Ching practice was avowedly personal, interpretive, and participatory — an instance of what Maggie Hyde calls **Synchronicity II** rather than the objectively-testable **Synchronicity I**. See [[Synchronicity]] for the extended argument connecting this paper to the vault's existing astrology-and-synchronicity material.
