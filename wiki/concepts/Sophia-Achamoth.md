# Sophia-Achamoth

**Sophia-Achamoth** is the Gnostic figure of divine Wisdom fallen into formless suffering, separated from the Pleroma (the fullness of the divine realm) — a myth Jung reads as the clearest ancient template for what happens psychologically when a man's consciousness identifies wholly with reason and spirit and cuts itself off from its own feminine, feeling side (the anima).

Source: [[jung-cw13-alchemical-studies]], Essay V, "The Philosophical Tree," §18, "The Relation of Suffering to the Coniunctio" (pars. 450–457), quoting Irenaeus's report of Valentinian Gnosticism. Discussed within [[Philosophical-Tree]].

---

## The Myth (Irenaeus's Account)

The Ἐνθύμησις (reflection, "Enthymesis") of the Sophia who dwells above is compelled by necessity to depart, with suffering, from the Pleroma into the darkness and empty spaces of the void. Separated from the light, she is "without form or figure, like an untimely birth, because she comprehended nothing" — that is, she becomes unconscious. The Christ dwelling on high, "outstretched upon the cross" (Jung notes the Greek's strong emphasis on stretching: ἐπεκταθέντα), takes pity on her and gives her a *form* — but only in respect of substance, **not so as to convey intelligence** (i.e., not consciousness). Having done this, he withdraws his power and returns to the Pleroma, **leaving Achamoth to herself**, so that she, becoming sensible of the suffering caused by her separation, might be moved by desire for better things — while retaining, in the meantime, a faint "odour of immortality" left in her by Christ and the Holy Spirit.

From her resulting affects — sadness, fear, bewilderment, confusion, longing, alternating laughter and weeping — **the entire created world arises**.

---

## Jung's Psychological Reading

This is, for Jung, "obviously 'psychological'": a cosmic projection of **the separation of the feminine anima from a masculine, spiritually oriented consciousness** that strives for the final and absolute victory of spirit over the world of the senses — the position shared by both pagan philosophy and Gnosticism in that era.

Sophia's formlessness and unconsciousness (ἀγνοία) describe with precision the state of the anima in a man who identifies himself absolutely with his reason and spirituality: he is in danger of dissociating from his anima entirely, losing touch with the compensating powers of the unconscious. Clinically, the unconscious then responds "with violent emotions, irritability, lack of control, arrogance, feelings of inferiority, moods, depressions, outbursts of rage" — a πάθος (suffering of the soul) not perceived as such for lack of introspection, surfacing first as vague malaise, and eventually forcing physical or psychological symptoms that can no longer be ignored.

> [!key-insight] Perceiving suffering vs. understanding it
> "Christ (the principle of masculine spirituality) perceives the sufferings of Sophia (i.e., the psyche) and thereby gives her form and existence. But he leaves her to herself so that she should feel the full force of her sufferings." Psychologically: the masculine mind is content merely to *perceive* psychic suffering, without making itself conscious of the reasons behind it — leaving the anima in a state of agnoia. Jung: this is "typical" and observable "today not only in all masculine neuroses but among so-called normal people" caught in a one-sided (usually intellectual) and psychologically blind stance.

---

## Why Neither Side Yields

Even though the masculine spirituality (Christ) is momentarily willing to acknowledge Sophia's suffering, the withdrawal is telling: "as soon as the light catches a glimpse of the darkness and there is a possibility of uniting with it, the power drive that is inherent in the light as well as in the darkness asserts itself and will not budge from its position. The one will not darken its radiance, and the other will not give up its gratifying emotions." Neither notices that their suffering is one and the same, and both stem from the process of becoming conscious itself — an original unity splitting into two irreconcilable halves.

**There can be no consciousness without this act of discrimination**, nor can the resultant duality be reunified without the extinction of consciousness. Original wholeness therefore remains a *desideratum* — Sophia longs for it more urgently than the Gnostic Christ does, because discrimination and differentiation matter more to the rationalistic intellect than wholeness through the union of opposites. **This is why it is the unconscious, not the rational intellect, that produces the symbols of wholeness** — quaternities, crosses, circles.

## The Cross as Bridal Bed

Jung connects this directly to the coniunctio: St. Augustine likened the cross to a bridal bed; the fairytale hero finds his bride in the top of a great tree; the shaman finds his heavenly spouse there too; so does the alchemist. **"The coniunctio is a culminating point of life and at the same time a death"** — hence the "fragrance of immortality" the Gnostic text attaches to Sophia even in her fallen state. The anima is simultaneously the connecting link to the eternal (world beyond) and the thing that involves a man in the chthonic world and its transitoriness — she carries both directions at once.

---

## Relation to Melusina and the Anima Doctrine Elsewhere in Jung

Sophia-Achamoth belongs to the same family of figures as [[Melusina]] (CW 13, Essay III) — the anima appearing at "the sudden collapse of a form or style of life," both alluring and dangerous, both psychic vision and something felt as objectively real. Where Melusina appears to the *individual* man at a personal catastrophic threshold, Sophia-Achamoth's fall is staged at *cosmic* scale — the myth externalizes onto the structure of the universe itself what, in a living man, plays out as anima-loss and its slow, symptomatic return.
