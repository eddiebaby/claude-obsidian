# Pharmakon

> **One-line definition:** Ancient Greek term (φάρμακον) meaning simultaneously "remedy" and "poison" — and, via the related *pharmakos*, "scapegoat." Jacques Derrida's deconstructive reading (*Dissemination*, 1972/1981) makes the term's undecidability itself the point. Used by [[Reginald Ajuonuma|Ajuonuma]] (2023) to name the ambivalent, double-edged nature both Oedipus and the alchemical *lapis*/*filius regius* share.

> [!note] Greek, not Latin
> This page documents a Greek philosophical/mythological term, not a Latin phrase — filed as a concept page under the vault's general "significant concept" rule (not the standing Latin-phrase rule), because of its direct load-bearing role in this source's argument about ambivalence and the Self.

---

## The Term

*Pharmakon* denotes a substance whose effect depends entirely on dose, context, and administration — the same drug that heals can kill. Derrida argues the word cannot be stabilized to either "remedy" or "poison" alone; it "would be a substance ... if we didn't have eventually to come to recognize it as antisubstance itself" (1972/1981, p.70).

A related word, ***pharmakos***, names the ancient Greek ritual of expelling a person from a city to remove collective evil, and the expelled person themself — the scapegoat. The administrator of the *pharmakon*, the ***pharmakeus***, is a magician or sorcerer who wields "a power of fascination that makes one stray from one's general, natural, habitual paths and laws" (Derrida 1972/1981, p.70).

## In This Source

[[ajuonuma2023-alchemical-oedipus]] identifies both **Oedipus** and the alchemical ***filius regius*** (the renewed royal son — see [[filius-regius]]) as *pharmakon*:

- The *filius regius* "bestows on men all health and prosperity ... but to the wicked, who abuse it, eternal punishment" (Waite 1678/1893, p.86) — remedy and poison in one figure, depending on the recipient.
- Oedipus's death is a blessing for Athens (Theseus) and a curse for Thebes (Polynices, Creon) — the same double valence. Historian Jean-Pierre Vernant (1972/1988): "Oedipus is double."
- Both are also *pharmakos*: Oedipus is expelled by Creon to purify Thebes of his crimes; the *filius regius* is expelled by the unwise king because of its residual "nigredic" blackness — the taint of Gabricus's incestuous union with Beya (see [[Visio Arislei]]).
- Classicist Helene Peet Foley (1993/2020), discussing Oedipus as *pharmakos*, calls him "an extraordinary capacity to save and destroy" — a direct gloss on *pharmakon*.
- Ajuonuma reads Derrida's "antisubstance" formulation against the alchemists' paradox of the *lapis* as "the stone that is no stone" (see [[lapis-exilis]]) — proposing this is what underlies the mystery of Oedipus's death: he is called by the god as "other-than-man" ("Oedipus! Oedipus!... It is time: you stay too long," *Oedipus at Colonus*, lines 1627-1628). Literary critic Harold Bloom (1996) reads this moment as Oedipus becoming a god; Ajuonuma reads it as Oedipus becoming the ***homo altus*** (lofty man) — another synonym for the lapis.

## Why It Matters to the Self Argument

The *pharmakon*'s undecidability is not a flaw in the symbol but, on this reading, the *mark* of genuine Self-symbolism: the [[Self (archetype)|Self]] "cannot be equated either with collective morality or with natural instinct" (Jung 1942/1954, para. 394) — it is precisely a figure that holds together what ordinary moral categories keep apart. Both curse and blessing, remedy and poison, are held *simultaneously* in Oedipus at the moment of his death, exactly as the [[Shadow]] and the light are held together in a genuine (non-*privatio-boni*) symbol of wholeness.
