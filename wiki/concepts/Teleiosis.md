# Teleiosis

The Greek distinction between *perfection* and *wholeness* — one of Jung's most important terminological contributions. Individuation aims not at moral perfection but at *teleiosis*: completion, having reached one's goal.

Source: [[edinger-aion-lectures]] (Lecture 8, pp. 63-64); [[jung-cw9ii-aion]] (par. 123).

---

## The Greek Terms

The root **telos** (τέλος) means *goal*, *end*, *fulfillment*.

- **Teleios** (τέλειος) — having reached one's telos; complete, fulfilled, fully grown, mature. Not morally spotless.
- **Teleiosis** (τελείωσις) — the state of completion or wholeness; having arrived at the goal.

The key verse is Matthew 5:48: *"Be you therefore perfect... as also your heavenly Father is perfect."* (DV)

The standard translation misleads. "Perfect" in English connotes moral flawlessness. But *teleios* means completion — the thing that has reached its proper end. Better: "Be therefore *whole and complete*, as also your heavenly Father is *whole and complete*." The scripture describes the Aquarian task, not the Pisces striving.

---

## Perfection vs. Wholeness

| Perfection (one-sided) | Teleiosis (whole) |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| Eliminate the shadow | Integrate the shadow |
| Become sinless | Become complete |
| Ascend above the body | Include the body |
| Gothic upward movement | *Circulatio* through all four realms |
| *Privatio boni* | *Coniunctio oppositorum* |
| The Christian aeon's collective project | The Aquarian individual's task |

The monastic ideal aimed at perfection — the three vows (poverty, chastity, obedience) as radical frustration of greed, lust, and power. That had its collective value in taming the instinctual psyche. But it excludes the shadow and produces one-sided spiritual identification. Teleiosis requires the *circulatio* — going through all four levels of the [[Fourfold-Quaternio]], not just the upper spiritual quaternio.

## Basilides and the Principium Individuationis

Basilides (Gnostic, 2nd century AD) identified Jesus as the *principium individuationis* — the first to undergo the discrimination (*phulokrinesis*) of composite natures. Hippolytus reports:

> "Now Jesus became the first sacrifice in the discriminations of the natures [*phulokrinesis*], and the Passion came to pass for no other reason than the discrimination of composite things. For in this manner, he says, the sonship that had been left behind in a formless state [*amorphia*] needed separating into its components [*phulokrithenai*] ... in the same way that Jesus was separated."

Jung used this as the motto for *Aion* itself.

**The third sonship**: In Basilides' cosmology, there are three "sonships" — aspects of the divine that emerged from the non-existent God. The first two returned to the Father. The third was left behind in matter in a state of *amorphia* (formlessness, undifferentiation) — mixed into the prime matter of the world. It could not ascend because it was too burdened with psychic material. Jesus, the son of Mary, learned from the third Christ what was above him and carried the message back. Through this discrimination, the third sonship too could return.

Psychologically: the third sonship IS the individual psyche, mixed into matter, formless, unconscious. Individuation is its discrimination — the phulokrinesis by which what was amorphous becomes differentiated, conscious, able to return to its source. That process of discrimination, completed, is *teleiosis*.

---

## Repressed Individuation

The obverse of teleiosis is *repressed* or *involuntary individuation*. When the inner discrimination is not undertaken consciously, it happens from outside as fate (Jung, par. 126):

> "The psychological rule says that when an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside, as fate. That is to say, when the individual remains undivided and does not become conscious of his inner opposite, the world must perforce act out the conflict and be torn into opposite halves."

Edinger: "That repressed urge to individuate can become dangerously destructive. I think this dynamism may lie behind certain chronically 'unlucky' people who are perpetually accident prone; in extreme cases repressed individuation can kill you." (p. 64)

The "exteriorized individuation" — the unconscious opposite manifested in the outer world — is identical to what Jung called "external dreams": events that carry psychological meaning in the same way a dream does.

---

> [!key-insight] The goal is wholeness, not goodness
> Jungian analysis that aims at making someone a better person (more moral, less selfish, kinder) is aiming at perfection and missing the point. The goal is *teleiosis* — the person becoming fully themselves, including their darkness consciously contained. A complete person may be less pleasant than an "improving" person. The danger of repression is not moral failure but involuntary individuation — the undone work surfaces as accident, compulsion, or outer conflict.
